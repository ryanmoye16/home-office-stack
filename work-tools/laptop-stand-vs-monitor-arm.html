Buying Guide — Laptop Stands & Monitor Arms

Laptop Stand vs Monitor Arm

The short answer first: this is rarely a real contest, because the two don't lift the same screen. A laptop stand lifts the laptop you already own; a monitor arm lifts a separate external monitor you may not have yet. So the choice is made by your current setup, not by which product is "better." Here's the fork, what each route quietly commits you to, and the two stands and three arms we've reviewed on each side. If you already know it's a stand, go straight to our posture guide; if it's an arm for a big panel, see the ultrawide arm guide.

The fork: which screen are you actually lifting?

A laptop's screen and keyboard are bolted together at the hinge, and that one fact decides everything. Put the laptop flat on the desk and the screen sits well below eye level; lift the screen to eye level on a stand and the built-in keyboard rises with it, too high to type on comfortably. So a stand is never just a stand — it commits you to a separate keyboard and mouse at elbow height. That's the other half of the posture fix, and it's a real second purchase to budget desk space for.

An arm sidesteps the hinge problem by ignoring the laptop's screen entirely. It holds a separate monitor — one with VESA mounting holes (75×75 or 100×100 mm on every arm we've reviewed) — and clamps or bolts to the desk edge. In exchange for needing that monitor, you get things no stand offers: the screen floats, so the desk patch under it is free again, and you can pull the screen closer or push it back, changing viewing distance (OSHA's preferred range is 20 to 40 inches) instead of only height.

So ask what you own, in this order: laptop only, today and for the foreseeable future → stand. External monitor already in the setup, or firmly planned → arm. Both screens in daily use, laptop open beside the monitor → possibly both, covered below. Everything after this section is choosing within the route, not between routes.

The both setup: monitor on an arm, laptop on a stand

There is one setup where "stand vs arm" is the wrong question because the answer is both: an external monitor as the primary screen, on an arm, with the laptop open beside it as a second screen, on a stand. The stand's job in that arrangement is matching heights — raising the laptop's screen toward the monitor's eye line so your gaze crosses between them without a drop. A mid-range lift like the Lamicall's 5.7 to 8.1 inches usually lands close; the keyboard and mouse stay on the desk serving both screens, so the stand route's keyboard commitment is already paid by the monitor setup. If the laptop mostly sits closed, skip the stand — an arm alone is the whole purchase.

When neither is the right buy

If you work from the laptop in short sessions in different places, work flat and save the money — neither an installed arm nor a stand you won't unpack earns its place. If your desk can't take a clamp (glass top, hollow core, no rear edge) and you have no monitor yet, the arm route is closed until the desk or the monitor changes. And if neck or shoulder pain persists after the screen is genuinely at eye level and the keyboard at elbow height, the remaining problem is not a gear problem — no stand or arm in this guide should be bought as a treatment. Laptop stand vs monitor arm questions, answered honestly

Can a monitor arm hold a laptop instead of a monitor?

Not on its own. The arms we have reviewed end in a VESA plate (75×75 or 100×100 mm) that bolts to the back of a monitor; a laptop has no VESA holes. Laptop tray adapters that bolt to a VESA plate do exist, but we have not reviewed one, so we do not recommend a specific tray here. If your only screen is the laptop's, a stand is the direct route.

If I buy a monitor arm, do I still need a laptop stand?

Only if the laptop stays open as a second screen. With the external monitor on the arm as your primary display, the laptop can sit closed or flat beside it. If you keep it open next to the monitor, a stand raises its screen toward the monitor's height so the two line up — that two-screen arrangement is the one case where buying both is the right answer.

Which is better for posture, a laptop stand or a monitor arm?

Whichever one gets the top of your main screen to eye level while your keyboard stays at elbow height — the target is the same for both. A stand reaches it with the laptop's own screen plus an external keyboard. An arm reaches it with a separate monitor and adds depth adjustment, so you can also set the viewing distance, which a stand cannot change. Neither fixes posture if the keyboard ends up at the wrong height.

I only have a laptop. Should I buy an arm now and a monitor later?

No — an arm with nothing to hold is money parked on your desk edge. Buy the stand first if the laptop is your only screen; it solves today's screen height with the machine you own. Revisit the arm when an external monitor actually enters the setup, and check its weight without its stand against the arm's rating before you buy either.

Bottom Line

Let the setup you own make the choice

Laptop only: take the stand route — the Lamicall for fine height and angle adjustment, the Amazon Basics stand for simple fold-flat value — and budget for the external keyboard that makes it work. Monitor in the setup: take the arm route — the NB F80 for gas-spring positioning, the HUANUO for heavier panels, the Amazon Basics arm for the straightforward checklist. Both screens, both open: buy both, and let the stand match the monitor's height. Then go deeper in the posture guide or the ultrawide arm guide.
