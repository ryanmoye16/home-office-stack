Buying Guide — Monitor Light Bars and Monitor Arms

Best Monitor Light Bar and Monitor Arm Setup

A monitor light bar and a monitor arm are usually bought one at a time — and that is how a bar ends up clipped to a monitor that drifts every time the light is adjusted, or powered by a short cable that goes taut the first time the arm is raised to full height. Using the two together is a matching problem, not two separate purchases, so this guide runs four checks a single-product guide can skip. The shared window: does the monitor pass the bar's fit and the arm's fit at the same time? Movement against control: does adjusting the light disturb the arm holding the screen? Power and routing: can a short fixed USB cable travel the arm's full range with slack to spare? And the top-centre spot: does anything else — a webcam, most often — already want the place the bar has to sit? One bar is the natural match for an arm that moves daily, one bar is least disruptive on an arm that never moves, and one screen size honestly fails both bars, whatever its arm can hold.

Check one: the shared size window — the monitor has to pass the bar and the arm at the same time

A monitor on an arm with a bar on top of it is one screen passing two different fit checks, and it only works inside the overlap. The bar checks the screen from the front, by size; the arm checks the same screen from behind, by size, panel weight without its factory stand, and VESA pattern. Neither check knows about the other. A screen can be squarely inside the arm's range and still be wider than any bar here can cover evenly, and a screen a bar fits perfectly can be too heavy for the arm underneath it. The setup lives where the two windows overlap — so run both, on the same monitor, before anything is ordered.

On the bar side, the window closes at 32 inches for both bars we review. The Quntis Monitor Light Bar PRO+ fits monitors 15 to 32 inches. The Quntis Monitor Light Bar (Standard) fits monitors up to 32 inches. Inside that window the two bars do the same core job — auto-dimming, a 2700K to 6500K range, and light aimed at the desk rather than the screen — and split on controls, which is check two. Outside the window, the honest limit is recorded in owner feedback on both models: on a 34-inch or larger ultrawide, owners report uneven coverage at the edges, because a bar rated to 32 inches cannot spread its light evenly across a screen wider than its stated fit. An arm that happily holds an ultrawide does not change that. If your screen is 34 inches or wider, this pairing is not yours, and shopping by a bar's stated fit for your actual screen comes first.

On the arm side, the three windows are close but not equal, and weight is judged panel-only. The HUANUO Single Monitor Arm takes panels 13 to 32 inches and up to 22 lb — the highest ceiling of the three. The NB North Bayou F80 takes panels 17 to 30 inches, the range its manual states and the range this site publishes for it, and up to 19.8 lb — the lowest ceiling here. The Amazon Basics Single Monitor Arm takes panels up to 32 inches and up to 20 lb. All three arms accept VESA 75x75 and 100x100, so the pattern check is the same question three times: does your panel have one of those two patterns in its back? The two gas-spring arms also carry a floor as well as a ceiling — each is rated from 4.4 lb — so a very light panel can fall under a gas spring's working range even while it passes every size check. Judge each ceiling as a ceiling to stay visibly under, not a target to land on.

Check two: movement against control — how the arm moves decides how the light should be adjusted

This is the check that only exists because the monitor floats. On a factory stand, adjusting a light bar is a non-event: you reach up, touch the bar, and the monitor underneath it goes nowhere, because a stand is a fixed post. On an arm, the monitor is held by a mechanism whose whole purpose is movement, and a hand reaching up to the top of the screen is a hand pushing on that mechanism. How much that push matters depends on which arm is underneath — and how the bar is controlled decides how often the push happens at all. Bar and arm have to be chosen as a pair.

The arms split into two movement characters. The HUANUO and the NB North Bayou F80 are gas-spring arms: they adjust smoothly, with one hand, and owners of both describe actually repositioning the screen during the day, because the movement is easy enough to be worth doing. They are the move-it-daily arms, and that ease cuts both ways: an arm that moves willingly under a deliberate hand also moves under an incidental one. The Amazon Basics arm has no gas spring. Owners describe its adjustment as stiff out of the box, loosening only somewhat with use — a set-and-forget character. That stiffness limits repositioning — and, for this one check, is an asset: a small nudge meets an arm that resists small nudges by design.

The bars split into two control characters, and the pairing follows directly. The Standard bar's touch control sits on the bar itself: adjusting brightness or warmth means reaching up to the lamp and touching it, every time. On a free-moving gas-spring arm, each of those touches can nudge the monitor — a small drift you then correct, several times a day, for as long as the pairing lasts. The PRO+ removes the touch from the equation entirely: its wireless rotary remote sits on the desk, and brightness and color temperature are dialled from there, without a hand going near the bar or the monitor at all. So the natural match for a gas-spring arm is the PRO+ — the arm keeps its freedom to move, and the light stops asking to push on it. And the Standard's touch control is least disruptive where movement is already resisted: on the stiff, set-and-forget Amazon Basics arm, positioned once and touched rarely.

Check three: power and cable routing — a short cable that has to travel

Both bars are USB powered, and owners of both report the same honest limit: the fixed USB power cable is relatively short. On a factory stand, that limit is an annoyance about which port is nearest. On an arm it becomes a travel problem. The bar sits at the top of a screen that can rise, drop, extend, retract and swivel, and the cable has to make every one of those journeys with it — from the top of the moving screen back to a USB port that does not move at all. A cable routed neatly at one position can be pulled taut at another, and taut is the failure to design against. The routing question is not whether the cable reaches. It is whether it reaches everywhere the monitor can go.

The route itself follows the monitor's own cables, with slack left deliberately. Run the bar's cable down the back of the panel with the display and power cables, then along the arm the same way they travel. Where the arm lists an integrated cable channel, use it: the F80 lists an integrated channel, and the Amazon Basics arm lists an integrated channel as well, and a channel keeps a short cable moving with the arm instead of swinging free of it. The HUANUO's listed specs include no integrated cable channel, where the other two arms here list one — so on the HUANUO the same cable routes alongside the arm, secured with the monitor's own cables, rather than inside a channel the arm does not claim to have. Either way, the slack rule is identical: leave enough loose cable at each joint that no segment is ever pulled taut, at full extension or full height.

Then prove the route before you call it finished. Move the monitor slowly through its whole working range — highest and lowest, furthest out and furthest back, swivelled both ways — and watch the bar's cable for the entire journey. It should never straighten into a line, never pinch at a joint, and never be what stops the monitor moving. Choose the USB port with that journey in mind, not just the nearest one at rest: a port on the monitor itself travels with the screen and shortens the journey, while a port on the computer or a wall adapter anchors the far end and makes slack non-negotiable.

Check four: the top-centre spot — the bar and a webcam want the same place

The top centre of the monitor is one position, and two common desk devices both default to it. A light bar sits centred on the top edge so its light spreads evenly across the desk in front of the screen. A webcam sits centred on the top edge so it frames you squarely and puts your eyeline near the middle of the screen. This guide names the competition as a check and makes no webcam picks: decide before ordering which device takes the centre on your desk, whether the webcam can live off-centre, lower, or on a tripod without hurting your calls, and whether the bar still lights the desk evenly from the position left to it.

Working through webcam placement properly — height, angle, eyeline and what to do when the centre is taken — is the job of our video call setup guide for a home office, and it covers the call side of this trade in full. The point for this pairing is narrower: a bar ordered first and a webcam discovered second is how the centre gets fought over, so the spot belongs in the setup test below alongside size, movement and cable travel, not as an afterthought once both boxes are open.

When the bar or arm already in your room is the right answer

A bar and an arm bought separately are not automatically mismatched: if your monitor already passes both size windows, the light can be adjusted without the screen wandering, the USB cable already travels the arm's full range without going taut, and nothing else is fighting the bar for the top-centre spot, no pairing here buys you anything new. The cases that earn a rethink are specific — a screen that drifts every time the light is dimmed, a cable that tugs when the monitor is raised, a webcam and a bar taking turns in one position. Name your case in the terms of the four checks, and change only the half that fails.

Before you buy: the five-minute setup test

Five minutes of checking tells you which pairing you are shopping for. First, the window test: your monitor's size against both bars — 15 to 32 inches for the PRO+, up to 32 inches for the Standard, and nothing here covering a 34-inch or larger ultrawide evenly — and against your arm, with its weight without its stand inside the arm's band: up to 22 lb for the HUANUO, up to 19.8 lb across 17 to 30 inches for the F80, up to 20 lb for the Amazon Basics arm, VESA 75x75 or 100x100 confirmed on the panel's back. Second, the movement test: move your monitor the way you actually do in a day, then ask whether a hand reaching to the top of the screen several times a day would undo that positioning — if it would, the bar needs a remote. Third, the travel test: trace the bar's short USB cable from the top of the screen to the port you plan to use, at the monitor's highest, lowest and furthest positions, and confirm slack survives all three. Fourth, the spot test: look at the top centre of your monitor and name what already sits there. Those four answers usually name the pairing.

Monitor light bar and monitor arm setup questions, answered honestly

What is the best monitor light bar and monitor arm setup?

Of the products we review, it depends on how the arm moves. If the monitor moves daily on a gas-spring arm — the HUANUO Single Monitor Arm or the NB North Bayou F80 — the Quntis Monitor Light Bar PRO+ is the natural match, because its wireless rotary remote adjusts the light from the desk without touching the bar or the monitor at all. If the monitor is set once and left on the stiff Amazon Basics Single Monitor Arm, the Quntis Monitor Light Bar (Standard) pairs cleanly: its touch control sits on the bar, and reaching up to it does not fight an arm that prefers to stay where it was set.

Will a monitor light bar fit a monitor that sits on a monitor arm?

Yes, when the monitor passes both gates. The bar checks the screen size: the Quntis PRO+ fits monitors 15 to 32 inches and the Quntis Standard fits monitors up to 32 inches. The arm checks size, panel weight without its factory stand, and the VESA pattern: the HUANUO takes panels 13 to 32 inches up to 22 lb, the NB North Bayou F80 takes panels 17 to 30 inches up to 19.8 lb, the Amazon Basics arm takes panels up to 32 inches up to 20 lb, and all three arms accept VESA 75x75 and 100x100. A 34-inch or larger ultrawide fails the bar side — owners report uneven coverage at the edges — whatever the arm can hold.

Does adjusting a light bar move a monitor on a gas-spring arm?

It can with the Standard bar, and that is the pairing point of this guide. The Standard's touch control sits on the bar itself, so every adjustment means reaching up and touching the lamp — a small push a free-moving gas-spring arm can answer by drifting. The PRO+ removes the push entirely: its wireless rotary remote sits on the desk, so brightness and color temperature change without a hand going near the bar or the monitor. On a stiff set-and-forget arm such as the Amazon Basics arm, the same reach barely matters, because the arm resists small nudges by design.

How do I route a light bar's USB cable on a monitor arm?

Give the cable slack for the arm's full travel. Both bars are USB powered and owners of both report the fixed USB power cable is relatively short, so the cable must still reach a USB port at full extension and full height, never pulled taut. Route it with the monitor's own cables: through the integrated cable channel where the arm lists one, as the F80 and the Amazon Basics arm do, or alongside the arm where it does not, as with the HUANUO. Then move the monitor through its whole range and confirm nothing pulls, pinches, or lifts the bar.

Can a light bar and a webcam share the top of the same monitor?

Not the same spot — the top centre of the monitor is one position, and a centre-mounted webcam and a light bar both want it, which makes this a check to run before ordering rather than after. Decide which device takes the centre, whether the webcam can sit off-centre or on a tripod instead, and whether the bar still sits centred enough to light the desk evenly. Our video call setup guide works through webcam placement for a home office in full; this guide makes no webcam picks and simply names the competition for the spot.

Bottom Line

Pass both windows, match the control to the movement, and route the cable for the whole journey

Run the checks in order: the monitor inside the bar's fit and the arm's fit at the same time, the bar's control matched to how the arm moves, the short USB cable routed with slack for the full travel, and the top-centre spot settled before either box is opened. The move-it-daily answer: the Quntis Monitor Light Bar PRO+ on a gas-spring arm — the HUANUO Single Monitor Arm for the highest capacity here, the NB North Bayou F80 for pan and its integrated cable channel, inside its 17 to 30 inch range. The set-and-forget answer: the Quntis Monitor Light Bar (Standard) on the Amazon Basics Single Monitor Arm — touch control on the bar, met by an arm stiff enough not to answer it. And the honest non-pairing: any bar here on a 34-inch or larger ultrawide, which neither bar covers evenly.
