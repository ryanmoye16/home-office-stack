Buying Guide — Keyboards

Best Keyboard for Spreadsheets and Data Entry

The short answer first: spreadsheet and data-entry work is number work, so a keyboard for it runs three checks a general guide can skip. Numpad: is there a real integrated pad under your right hand, or will numbers ride the number row all day? Feedback: on a long run of entries, can your fingers audit each press by feel — a mechanical bump you follow without looking, or a short, quiet scissor stroke? And the desk costs: how much width does the board take, where does that push the mouse, and does it light its own keys when the room is dim? Three of the four boards here carry a real numpad. One refines the straight full-size board. One bets everything on a felt bump. One answers with a split frame and a built-in rest. And one compact board is named plainly as the skip for this job.

Check one: a real integrated numpad, or numbers on the number row

Start with the pad, because entry work lives or dies on where the numbers are. Three of the four keyboards we review have a real integrated numpad. The Logitech MX Keys S and the Logitech MX Mechanical are full-size 104-key boards with the pad built in, and the Logitech Ergo K860 is full-size with a numpad as well. On those three, a long run of figures — a column of invoices, a ledger, a sheet of results — keeps the right hand in one dedicated cluster, moving between the digits and the pad's own keys without travelling back to the main block between entries. That fixed home position is the whole case for the integrated pad: the hand learns one small map for numbers and stays on it for the length of the run, while the eyes stay on the source document or the cell above.

The fourth board fails this check outright, and this guide names it plainly. The Logitech MX Keys Mini has NO numpad — not a relocated one, not a layer, none — and its function keys share a single row with media actions. For prose that trade can be a fair one; for spreadsheets and data entry it asks the right hand to ride the number row for every figure, all day. So the Mini is the skip for this job, and it gets its own section below framed exactly that way. One honest middle route deserves naming without a product attached: if desk space genuinely forces a compact board, the version of that desk that works for entry is a compact board plus a separate numeric pad beside it. We have not reviewed a standalone pad, so this guide names the route as criteria only — a dedicated pad, within the right hand's reach, that connects the same way the board does — and recommends no specific one.

Check two: feedback you can audit by feel on long entry runs

Entry work is typed eyes-up, so the confirmation that a figure landed has to arrive through the fingers. The Logitech MX Mechanical builds its case on exactly that. Its low-profile Tactile Quiet switches are mechanical switches with a tactile bump and no click bar: each press announces itself as a bump your fingers feel partway down, quietly, so a run of entries can be followed by feel while the eyes stay on the source. Two limits belong in the same breath. The switches are NOT hot-swappable — the Tactile Quiet feel is the feel for the board's life — and the Clicky and Linear versions are different listings, as is the smaller Mini, so the feel is chosen at purchase and cannot be swapped in later if it disappoints.

The other full-size boards answer with a shorter, quieter kind of certainty. The Logitech MX Keys S and the Logitech Ergo K860 both use Perfect Stroke scissor keys — quiet, laptop-style travel — with spherically dished caps on the Keys S that centre the fingertip so an off-centre press still lands square. The confirmation there is the clean bottom of a short stroke rather than a bump partway down: less information per press, in exchange for near-silence and a feel most laptop-trained hands already know. The Mini carries Perfect Stroke keys too, for the reader weighing the criteria-only compact route. This guide sets no figures for either side — no typing-speed claims, no error-rate claims, no productivity claims — because the catalog supports none. The check is narrower and more honest: on your longest entry run, which confirmation do your fingers trust without looking?

Check three: the width on the desk, and the light on the keys

Every numpad board here is wide, but they spend the width very differently. The Ergo K860 is the widest at 17.95 inches, and the deepest by far at 9.17 inches, because the measurement includes its integrated pillowed wrist rest — a curved split keyframe in a single piece, with the rest built in and not removable. That width pushes the mouse farther right than any other board here, which is a real daily cost for work that alternates between the pad and the pointer. The MX Mechanical measures 17.08 inches wide with two flip-out rear tilt feet setting its angle. The MX Keys S is the narrowest of the numpad boards at 16.94 inches, a straight low-profile frame at a fixed angle with no tilt feet and no split. Against all three, the Mini's 11.65 inches shows what the numpad costs in desk terms — roughly a hand's width of mouse room is the compact board's real return, bought with the pad itself.

Light is the second half of the cost, and here the split board pays it alone. The MX Keys S and the MX Mechanical both carry white smart backlighting; on the Keys S it wakes as your hands approach, adjusts its brightness on its own, and drops to a low-power mode below 10 percent battery. The Ergo K860 has NO backlighting at all — stated plainly, because entry work often happens at the dim ends of the day, and an unlit board in a dim room asks the room to do the lighting. The trade runs both ways: with no backlight to feed, the K860 runs on two AAA batteries rated for up to 24 months, while the backlit boards recharge over USB-C on ratings of up to 10 days backlit or up to 5 months off for the Keys S, and up to 15 days backlit or up to 10 months off for the Mechanical. When the keyboard already on your desk is the right answer

Price the option already in front of you first. Number work is a real use case, but not automatically a purchase: if the board on your desk already has a numpad your right hand trusts, a feel your fingers follow without looking, and enough light for the hours you work, then no board here buys you anything you do not already have. The cases that earn a new keyboard are specific — numbers riding the number row because the pad is missing, presses your fingers lose on a flat stroke during long runs, a board so wide the mouse has drifted far to the right, or keys you cannot read at the dim end of the day. Name your case in the terms of the three checks above. Each points at a different pick, and a desk with no failing check points at no purchase at all.

Before you buy: the two-minute data-entry test

Two minutes of honest answers tells you which board you are shopping for. First, the numpad test: watch your right hand through one real entry run. If it lives on a pad, the full-size boards stay in front and the Mini is answered; if it never leaves the main block, the compact question stays honestly open. Second, the feel test: enter a column of figures without looking down. If your fingers wanted a signal the board did not give, the bump board is your shortlist; if the short, quiet stroke felt complete, the scissor boards stay open. Third, the desk test: measure the width your keyboard can take before the mouse is pushed farther right than your work allows, and note whether your entry hours include a dim room. Those answers, read against the table above, usually name the pick. Then judge it in the return window on a real day of figures.

Spreadsheet and data-entry keyboard questions, answered honestly

Which keyboard is best for spreadsheets and data entry?

Of the four keyboards we review, the Logitech MX Keys S is the pick for spreadsheets and data entry: a full-size 104-key board with an integrated numpad, quiet Perfect Stroke scissor keys, smart backlighting, and a USB-C battery that keeps working while it charges. The Logitech MX Mechanical is the feel alternative, with the same full layout and a Tactile Quiet mechanical bump instead. The Logitech Ergo K860 is the split conditional pick, with a numpad, an integrated pillowed wrist rest and no backlight. The Logitech MX Keys Mini is the named skip for this job: it has no numpad at all.

Do you need a numpad for spreadsheets and data entry?

If numbers are a regular part of the work, the honest answer from this set is yes — a real integrated numpad keeps the right hand in one dedicated cluster through a long entry run, instead of riding the number row. Three of the four keyboards we review have one: the Logitech MX Keys S, the Logitech MX Mechanical and the Logitech Ergo K860. The Logitech MX Keys Mini has no numpad, and its function keys share a single row with media actions. If desk space forces a compact board, the honest route is a compact board plus a separate numeric pad — we have not reviewed a standalone pad, so this guide names that criteria-only route rather than a product.

Is a mechanical keyboard better for data entry?

It depends on which feedback your fingers audit by. The mechanical board here is the Logitech MX Mechanical: its low-profile Tactile Quiet switches put a tactile bump on each press, felt rather than heard, with no click bar. The switches are not hot-swappable, and the Clicky and Linear versions are different listings, so the feel is chosen at purchase. The alternative is the Perfect Stroke scissor keys on the Logitech MX Keys S and the Logitech Ergo K860 — quiet, short, laptop-style travel with a clean bottom to each stroke. This guide sets no figures for typing speed or error rates for either feel; choose the confirmation your fingers trust on a long run.

Which of these keyboards has no backlight?

The Logitech Ergo K860. It has no backlighting at all, which matters for entry work in a dim room early or late in the day — its keys need light from the room. The other three are backlit: the Logitech MX Keys S and the Logitech MX Mechanical have white smart backlighting, and the Keys S wakes as your hands approach and adjusts brightness on its own, dropping to a low-power mode below 10 percent battery. The Logitech MX Keys Mini is backlit too, though it remains the skip here on the numpad check. The K860 trades the light away and runs up to 24 months on two AAA batteries instead.

Is the MX Keys Mini good for spreadsheets?

For spreadsheet and data-entry work, it is the named skip in this guide — stated plainly, it has no numpad, and its function keys share a single row with media actions. Its own facts are good ones: the same Perfect Stroke keys as the full-size MX Keys S, smart backlighting, Easy-Switch across three devices, and a compact 11.65 inch frame that pulls the mouse in close. It is Bluetooth only in the box, with no receiver included. It is the right board only if the numpad genuinely never gets used in your work — and if numbers arrive even occasionally, the full-size boards above are the honest answer.

Bottom Line

Pad first, audit by feel, count the width and the light

Run the checks in order: settle the numpad first, decide which feedback your fingers audit by on a long run, and accept the width and light costs with your eyes open. The data-entry answer: the Logitech MX Keys S, the straight full-size board with the light and no charging pause. The feel answer: the Logitech MX Mechanical, bump first, in the same full layout. The comfort answer: the Logitech Ergo K860, split and pillowed, the widest board here and the unlit one. And the honest skip: the Logitech MX Keys Mini, for desks where the numpad genuinely never gets used.
