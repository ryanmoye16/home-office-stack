Buying Guide — Laptop Stands

Best Laptop Stand for Posture

The short answer first: a laptop forces a trade-off — screen at eye level or hands in a good position, never both at once. A stand fixes the screen half; an external keyboard and mouse fix the other half. The four stands we've reviewed split the job by height: two adjustable seated picks, one tall stand for standing height, and one fold-flat travel option. Here's the target to aim for, then how to choose.

The target: top of the screen at or just below eye level

OSHA's workstation guidance is specific: the top of the screen should sit at or just below eye level, your viewing distance should be roughly 20 to 40 inches — about arm's length — and your gaze should angle down about 15 to 20 degrees. A laptop sitting flat on a desk fails the first number by a wide margin, which is why laptop work so often ends in a hunched neck and shoulders.

The stand is only half the fix. Raise the laptop far enough to fix your neck and its built-in keyboard ends up too high and too steep to type on. OSHA's answer for prolonged laptop use is a separate keyboard and mouse, placed so your hands sit at or just below elbow height with wrists and forearms roughly straight. Budget for that external pair as part of the same upgrade — a stand used with the laptop's own keyboard trades a neck problem for a shoulder and wrist problem.

So shop by height range, not features. A seated setup at a standard desk usually needs several inches of lift; standing work needs far more than a normal riser can give; travel needs a stand that folds away and accepts a lower, compromise height. That height split — not finish or brand — is how our four reviewed stands actually differ.

When none of these is the right buy

If your laptop is a second screen next to an external monitor, the posture target applies to the monitor, not the laptop — a monitor arm gets that screen to eye level and frees desk space, and the laptop just needs to sit level beside it. And if neck discomfort persists after the screen, keyboard, chair, and feet are all set to the guidance above, that's no longer a gear problem — get it checked rather than buying a taller stand.

Laptop stand posture questions, answered honestly

How high should a laptop stand raise the screen?

High enough that the top of the screen sits at or just below eye level, with your gaze angling down about 15 to 20 degrees — OSHA's workstation guidance. For most seated setups that means lifting the laptop several inches; how many depends on your desk and chair height, which is why adjustable stands like the Lamicall are easier to get right than fixed ones.

Do I need an external keyboard with a laptop stand?

Yes, for anything beyond short sessions. A laptop ties the screen and keyboard together, so once the screen is at eye level the built-in keyboard is too high and too steep to type on comfortably. OSHA's guidance for prolonged laptop use is a separate keyboard and mouse placed at or just below elbow height. A stand without an external keyboard trades a neck problem for a shoulder and wrist problem.

How far should a laptop screen be from my eyes?

OSHA puts preferred viewing distance at 20 to 40 inches from eye to screen — roughly arm's length. If you find yourself leaning in to read, increase the text size before pulling the laptop closer.

What is the difference between the Lamicall and the Rain Design iLevel 2?

The Lamicall adjusts in both height (5.7 to 8.1 inches in our catalog specs) and angle and folds flat for travel, fitting laptops up to 17.3 inches. The Rain Design iLevel 2 is a desk-only solid aluminum stand that does not fold; its front slider adjusts height with the laptop still on the stand, and its open design aids airflow. The Lamicall is the versatile pick, the iLevel 2 is the premium fixed-desk pick.

When would I need a tall stand like the Lifelong Upryze?

When a normal riser cannot get the screen to eye level — most often when you want to work standing without a standing desk, or on a tall counter. The Upryze raises a laptop up to 20 inches in our catalog specs, far beyond the 6 to 8 inch range of typical desk stands. If you only work seated at a standard desk, that extra range is height you will never use.

Bottom Line

Match the height range to how you actually work

For most seated desks, take the Lamicall — its height and angle range is the easiest to land exactly on eye level, and it still folds flat. For a permanent desk where you'll adjust height mid-day, the Rain Design iLevel 2 and its slider earn their keep. For standing height, only the Lifelong Upryze gets there. For travel and simple value, it's the Amazon Basics. Whichever you choose, pair it with an external keyboard and mouse — that's the other half of the posture fix.
