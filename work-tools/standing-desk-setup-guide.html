Standing Desk Setup Guide

The short answer first: a standing desk is not set up when it is assembled — it is set up when four things are true. Two heights are saved to its presets, so switching positions is a button press. Your feet land on a mat, not a hard floor. The screen is clamped to the desk, so it travels with the surface. And the cables are mounted to the desk, with one slack cable to the wall. Work through the steps in order, with the desks, arms, mat and cable gear we've reviewed.

Step one: find your two heights, then save them

Every other step hangs off this one. Stand at the desk, shoulders relaxed, and raise the surface until your forearms rest on it with your elbows at about 90 degrees — that is your standing height. Sit in your chair at its proper height and do the same — that is your sitting height. Neither number comes from a chart: both come from your elbows, and the screen check follows from them (top of the screen at or just below eye level in each position — step three handles the screen itself).

Save both heights the day the desk arrives. The four electric desks we review all carry memory presets — four each on the SHW 48, the FlexiSpot EN1 and the Marsail, and three on the VIVO 63 by 32 — and an unsaved preset is the most common reason a standing desk quietly becomes a fixed desk at sitting height. Their ranges frame who each desk can serve: the SHW travels 28 to 45 inches, the EN1 28.9 to 46.5, the Marsail 28.3 to 46.5, and the VIVO 25.3 to 51 — the widest travel of the four, and the one that reaches the shortest sitting and tallest standing elbows. Choosing between the desks themselves? Our standing desk comparison guide runs that range check desk by desk.

Count the load while you are at it. Everything steps two to four put on or under the surface counts against the desk's limit — 110 lb on the SHW, per its manual, and 176 lb on each of the other three. Monitors, arms, a laptop, a desktop PC on the surface: add them honestly, because a desk near its limit is a desk that wobbles and strains at full height, which is exactly where you will be standing.

Step two: give your feet a standing surface

A hard floor is the layer nobody budgets for, and the first one your body notices. Standing still on wood or tile loads your heels and calves in one fixed position; twenty minutes in, the shifting and leaning starts. An anti-fatigue mat is the fix, and it is deliberately step two — before the screen and the cables — because if standing is uncomfortable, the presets from step one stop getting used and the rest of the setup is decoration.

The Ergodriven Topo Mini is the mat we review, and its difference from a flat foam pad is the point: its calculated terrain — a 20-degree leading edge and a raised foot-rest node on a 25.2 by 19.2 inch polyurethane surface — gives your feet positions to move between, and owners consistently report shifting, stretching and changing stance without thinking about it, which is how standing stretches get longer. Its textured bottom grips the floor so the mat stays where your feet expect it.

Fit check: the Topo Mini is a standing surface, not a chair surface — slide it aside before rolling your chair back over its spot, and expect a chair's casters and a foam mat to disagree. It is also sized for one standing spot in front of one desk; a wide desk you pace along wants a longer mat, which is a different product we have not reviewed. And no mat makes all-day standing wise: it makes the standing stretches comfortable so the sit-stand rhythm in step one actually happens.

Step three: clamp the screen to the desk

The rule is simple: whatever holds your screen must be supported by the desk, so the screen travels with the surface. A monitor on its factory stand obeys the rule at the cost of its footprint and its fixed height. A monitor arm clamped or bolted to the desk obeys it and hands the footprint back — and it adds the adjustment that matters here, because your standing eye level is not your sitting eye level, and the screen's height above the surface often needs to change with the position.

For two positions, gas-spring adjustment is the honest pick. The HUANUO Single Monitor Arm carries monitors 13 to 32 inches and up to 22 lb on a gas spring owners praise for smooth lift and a steady hold once mounted, with C-clamp or grommet mounting and VESA 75x75 and 100x100. The NB North Bayou F80 is the same idea in a 17 to 30 inch, 19.8 lb envelope per its manual — gas-spring lift, height, tilt, swivel, rotation and pan, both mounts in the box — with owner feedback centred on the smooth adjustment and position hold. Either repositions with a light push when you change positions, which is precisely the moment a stiff arm teaches you to stop bothering.

Final check, at full height: raise the desk to your standing preset and type. Wobble that never shows seated appears standing, when the frame is at its most extended — an arm near its weight ceiling and a desk near its load limit are the usual causes, in that order.

Step four: make the cables travel with the desk

This is the step that separates a standing setup from a sitting setup with a taller desk. Your desk travels 17 to 25.7 inches vertically, depending on the model. Any cable that runs from the surface to a fixed point — a floor strip, a wall outlet, a PC tower on the floor — is pulled through that whole travel, twice a day, until something unplugs, strains, or tips. The rule that prevents all of it: everything on the desk plugs into a power strip mounted to the desk, and exactly one cable runs from that strip to the wall, long enough to stay slack at full height.

The Litwaro Under Desk Cable Management Tray is the reviewed piece built for exactly this job: a 13.38 inch carbon-steel mesh basket that C-clamps to a flat desk edge 0.4 to 2 inches thick — no drilling, no adhesive — and carries a power strip and its slack under the surface, rising and falling with the desk. It can face inward or outward, its clamp faces are padded, and its roughly 15 lb load rating is generous against a strip and its cables. One desk in our reviews arrives with this layer in the box — the SHW 48 includes an under-desk cable tray — so check what your desk supplied before buying a second one.

Finish with the travel test: with everything plugged in, run the desk from its lowest to its highest preset and watch the one wall cable and any PC or monitor cables that leave the desk. Nothing should go taut, lift, or pull at a port at either end of the travel. Slack that looks excessive at sitting height is exactly right at standing height.

When a standing setup is not the next purchase

Three honest cases. First, if your fixed desk already puts your elbows and screen at the right heights and your discomfort is about sitting still, a walking break and a chair that fits are cheaper experiments than a new desk — gear is not a treatment for pain, and persistent pain deserves a professional, not a product. Second, if your room cannot give a standing desk its footprint plus your standing space behind it, our standing desks for small spaces guide does that room math before any purchase. Third, if you work from a laptop alone, a laptop stand and a separate keyboard solve the screen-height half of this guide on the desk you own — our ergonomic desk setup guide builds that four-layer version. A standing desk earns its place when you will genuinely alternate — which is why step one of this guide is a pair of saved heights, not the desk itself.

Standing desk questions, answered honestly

What height should a standing desk be set to?

Set it by your elbows, not by a number on the handset: standing or sitting, your forearms should rest roughly level with your elbows at about 90 degrees and your shoulders relaxed, with the top of the screen at or just below eye level. Find that surface height once in each position and save both to the desk's memory presets — the four desks we review carry four presets each, except the VIVO 63 by 32, which carries three. A saved pair of heights is what turns standing from a project into a button press, which is the difference between a desk that gets used both ways and one that stays at sitting height.

Do you need an anti-fatigue mat with a standing desk?

For standing stretches beyond about twenty minutes, most owners end up wanting one: a hard floor transfers the whole standing load to your heels and calves, and a mat is the cheapest layer of the whole setup. The Ergodriven Topo Mini we review is a 25.2 by 19.2 inch polyurethane mat whose calculated terrain — a 20-degree leading edge and a raised foot-rest node — gives your feet positions to shift between, which owners credit for standing longer without thinking about it. The honest limits: it is a standing surface, not a chair surface, so slide it aside before rolling a chair back over its spot, and no mat makes all-day standing a good idea — the desk's presets exist so you can sit back down.

Should a monitor be on an arm or its own stand on a standing desk?

Either works, as long as the screen is supported by the desk itself so it travels with the surface. A monitor arm clamped or bolted to the desk does that and returns the factory stand's footprint to the desk; a factory stand does it too, at the cost of that footprint and of any height adjustment above the desk. The arm fork that matters for sit-stand use is adjustment effort: a gas-spring arm like the HUANUO or the NB F80 repositions with a light push when your standing eye level differs from your sitting one, while a set-and-forget arm like the Amazon Basics is deliberately stiff to move — fine if both your positions share one screen height, wearing if they do not. Whichever you mount, check it at full standing height: wobble that is invisible seated shows up standing, when the desk is at its least rigid.

How do you manage cables on a desk that moves?

Follow one rule: everything on the desk plugs into a strip that is mounted to the desk, and exactly one cable runs from that strip to the wall, with enough slack for the desk's full travel. The desks we review travel 17 to 25.7 inches between their lowest and highest settings, and that travel is what tears unmanaged setups apart — a strip left on the floor makes every device cable a moving cable. A clamp-on under-desk tray like the Litwaro carries the strip under the surface so it rises with the desk. A floor cable box is the wrong home for the desk's strip; it belongs under a fixed desk or a TV stand. Adhesive raceway is for the fixed runs — along a wall or a desk edge that never moves — not for the moving span between desk and wall.

How long should you stand at a standing desk?

Alternate, in stretches your feet and knees vote for. The setup in this guide is built around that rhythm: two saved heights so switching costs one button press, a mat that makes the standing stretches comfortable, and a chair that stays in reach for the sitting ones. Standing all day swaps one set of aches for another, and sitting all day was the problem the desk was bought to solve — the honest target most owners settle into is standing for part of each hour, not for the whole day. If standing brings joint pain rather than ordinary muscle tiredness, sit down and treat that as information, not a challenge.

Bottom Line

Heights saved, feet sorted, screen clamped, cables travelling

Run the steps in order and a standing desk becomes what it was bought to be: save the two elbow heights to the presets, put a Topo Mini where your feet land, clamp a gas-spring arm (HUANUO or NB F80) to the desk so the screen travels and repositions easily, and mount the strip in a Litwaro tray so one slack cable is the only thing that reaches the wall.
