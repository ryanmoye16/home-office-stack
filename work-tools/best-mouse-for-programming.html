Buying Guide — Mice

Best Mouse for Programming

Programming asks a mouse a different set of questions than most desk work. Down a file: when code runs to hundreds or thousands of lines, how fast does the wheel travel — and how cleanly does it stop? Across the editor: when lines run wide and panes sit side by side, is there a wheel that moves sideways, or does every trip become a reach for a scrollbar? And through the session: when coding stretches for hours, what shape is the hand holding, and how much arm movement does pointing demand? Four mice we review answer very differently: one passes the two movement checks outright, two answer with a handshake angle, and one answers by not moving at all.

Check one: down a long file — the vertical scroll test

The Logitech MX Master 3S passes this check fully, and the reason is its MagSpeed electromagnetic wheel. In ratchet mode it steps with the precision a short hop between nearby functions needs. Flick it harder and it free-spins at up to 1,000 lines per second — the mode for jumping from the top of a long file to the code far below it, or back up to the imports when the error points the other way. SmartShift switches between those modes automatically, so the wheel that creeps a few lines while you read can be spinning freely a moment later, without a settings trip in between. Programming mixes both distances in the same minute, so a wheel that only does one speed makes the hand pay for the other.

The Logitech Lift passes partially, and the other two are precision-only. The Lift's SmartWheel offers a precision mode and a speed-scroll mode, which is a real two-speed answer rather than a single fixed wheel — short moves stay controlled, longer runs through a file move faster. It is not a free-spin wheel in the MX Master 3S sense. The Logitech MX Vertical is precision-only: a precision scroll wheel with a middle click and no free-spin mode recorded, so a long file is travelled a notch at a time. The Logitech ERGO M575S sits at the same end, with a standard wheel and no free-spin mode recorded either. A full pass, a partial pass, and two precision-only wheels — for long files, that order matters daily.

Check two: across wide code — the horizontal movement test

Only one mouse in this guide passes check two, and it is not close. The MX Master 3S has a separate thumb wheel, set apart from the main wheel, whose job is horizontal movement. Code is where that wheel earns its place: long lines past the edge of the pane, diffs beside the file they change, a source file on one side and its tests or a terminal on the other. The thumb rolls sideways while the fingers stay where they were, instead of the whole hand leaving the work to find a scrollbar, drag it, and come back. The movement is sideways, repeated, and small each time — exactly what a dedicated wheel beats a scrollbar trip on.

The other three have no recorded horizontal wheel — and one says so outright. The Lift's recorded specs describe its SmartWheel and no thumb or horizontal wheel. The MX Vertical's recorded wheel is its precision wheel with a middle click only — no horizontal wheel is recorded for it either. The M575S is the most explicit of the three: its recorded specs state no horizontal scrolling, in the form of no horizontal tilt scrolling and no free-spin mode on its wheel. None of that makes those mice unusable on wide code — editors scroll sideways by keyboard and scrollbar — but the mouse itself offers no wheel-based way across. One pass, three honest failures, and the trackball is weakest here because its own specs answer no rather than leaving it unrecorded.

Check three: through a long session — the all-day comfort test

Comfort here is not one answer, because the four mice ask the hand to work in three different ways. The MX Master 3S is the conventional answer done thoroughly: a sculpted palm body for medium-to-large hands, with a thumb rest and thumb controls placed under it, so the hand rests fully on the mouse. That suits a programmer whose palm-down shape has never been the problem — the body fills the hand instead of asking the fingers to pinch a smaller shell. The boundary is built into the same shape: it stays palm-down, and smaller hands can find it large. If the palm-down twist is what a long session makes you notice, no amount of sculpting changes the angle.

The other three each change the session in a different way. The Lift and the MX Vertical both hold the hand at a 57-degree handshake angle instead of palm-down — the Lift in a compact, softly textured body with a thumb rest, sized for small-to-medium hands, and the MX Vertical in a larger textured body sized for medium-to-large right hands. Same angle, different scale: choosing between them is a hand-size decision first, and getting it wrong reverses the benefit, because the Lift crowds large hands. The M575S changes the movement instead of the angle: a stationary thumb trackball, so the body never moves, there is no arm movement, and the cursor is driven by the thumb rolling a ball. For a programmer whose strain is the sweeping, that stillness is the comfort case; its learning curve belongs in the same sentence.

The programming pick: Logitech MX Master 3S

The MX Master 3S is the programming pick because it is the only mouse here that does not ask the reader to choose which movement check to give up. Down a long file, the MagSpeed wheel covers both distances a coding day contains — ratchet precision for a few lines, free-spin at up to 1,000 lines per second for a few thousand, with SmartShift deciding between them as the flick changes. Across wide code, the separate thumb wheel moves horizontally while the main wheel keeps its vertical job, which is the single most code-specific control in this entire guide once lines and panes run wide. Through the session, the sculpted body supports a medium-to-large hand in full, its Darkfield sensor runs from 200 to 8,000 DPI in 50 DPI increments and tracks on glass at least 4mm thick, and its seven buttons plus a gesture button can be customised per app in Logi Options+. Bluetooth or Logi Bolt across up to three devices with Flow, Quiet Clicks, and a rechargeable battery rated at up to 70 days complete the file.

Fit check: the limits are size, handedness and two honest owner notes. The sculpted body suits medium-to-large hands and is large and heavy for small hands, and it is right-hand only, with no left-hand version. Its shape stays palm-down, so a programmer changing mice because of the palm-down twist should start with the vertical sections below instead. Owner reliability reports split on the left click, and the soft coating is the part owners watch wear and discolour over time — neither cancels the programming case, but both belong in the decision, and its full review records them in full.

The vertical conditional for smaller hands: Logitech Lift

The Lift is a conditional pick, and the condition is stated in its shape: a 57-degree vertical handshake angle, in a compact body with a softly textured grip and a thumb rest, sized for small-to-medium hands in the right-hand Graphite listing reviewed here. If that angle is why you are changing mice for long sessions, the Lift is how you keep the most programming movement while you change. Check one passes partially — its SmartWheel offers precision and speed-scroll modes, a genuine two-speed wheel for moving through files. Its optical sensor runs up to 4,000 DPI and is fully adjustable, it has six buttons with quiet clicks, Bluetooth or Logi Bolt across up to three devices with Flow, and a single AA battery rated at up to 24 months.

Fit check: check two fails, and this guide will not soften it. No thumb wheel and no horizontal wheel are recorded for the Lift, so wide lines and split panes are worked by scrollbar and keys, not by a sideways wheel. Large hands will also crowd the compact body — the MX Vertical below is the larger version of the same angle, and a crowded grip through a long session defeats the shape's own point. The 57-degree angle is fixed, and the battery is a single AA rather than rechargeable. Buy the Lift when the angle and the smaller hand size come first; do not buy it as a wide-code specialist, because on that check it is not one. Our full review makes its fit case in full.

The large-hand vertical conditional: Logitech MX Vertical

The MX Vertical is the same conditional logic at the larger scale: a 57-degree vertical body with a textured rubber surface and thumb rest, for medium-to-large right hands, bought because the hand wants the handshake angle through long sessions and needs the roomier body to hold it properly. Its programming file is the most mixed in the guide. Check one is precision-only — a precision scroll wheel with a middle click, with no free-spin mode recorded, so long files are travelled deliberately, notch by notch. Check two fails as it does for the Lift — no horizontal wheel is recorded. What it returns is pointer-speed control on the mouse itself: an optical sensor from 400 to 4,000 DPI in 50 DPI steps, plus a cursor speed switch on top for instant changes — one setting for placing the cursor inside a line, another for crossing to a second pane. Six buttons, Bluetooth, Unifying receiver or wired USB-C, and a rechargeable battery rated at up to four months complete its file.

Fit check: two limits belong up front. Its clicks are ordinary audible clicks, not quiet clicks — in a shared room, that is a daily fact, not a footnote. And both movement checks are limited, so a programmer whose coding pain is distance, down a file or across a pane, will feel this mouse's ceiling every day. It is also right-hand only, and a minority of owners report button and scroll-wheel faults over time. Buy it when the large vertical shape is the decision for a long session; for scroll-led programming work, the MX Master 3S remains the answer. Our full review covers the rest.

The stationary conditional: Logitech ERGO M575S

The ERGO M575S answers check three by removing movement rather than reshaping it. It is a stationary thumb trackball: the sculpted body sits where you put it and never travels, the thumb rolls a ball to drive the cursor, and a long coding session asks no arm movement and no clear run of desk at all. That stillness is a genuine case at a small or crowded desk, or where sweeping the arm is the strain the session repeats. Its clicks are quiet, it has five buttons, it connects to a single device by Logi Bolt or Bluetooth, and a single AA battery is rated at up to 18 months. For a programmer whose files are narrow and whose editor is one pane, those facts can outrank the movement checks.

The trade is stated plainly, because on the other two checks this is the weakest mouse in the guide. Check two fails outright: its recorded specs state no horizontal scrolling, so wide lines and side-by-side panes get no wheel-based help at all. Check one is limited: a standard vertical scroll wheel with a middle click and no free-spin mode recorded, so long files are travelled at a standard wheel's pace. Its sensor runs up to 2,000 DPI, adjustable only in Logi Options+ with no DPI button on the mouse, and it serves one device at a time rather than the three-device switching the other three offer. That is a conditional pick for stillness, not a general programming pick.

Fit check: buy it for the desk or the arm that wants the mouse to stop moving — not for wide code, where its own recorded specs answer the sideways question no before the work starts. Our full review makes the stationary case in full.

When the mouse already on your desk is the right answer

Price the option already in front of you first. Programming is a real use case, but not automatically a purchase: if the mouse on your desk already travels a long file without wearing your finger out, gives you a comfortable way across wide lines and panes, and still feels right at the end of a long session, then no mouse here buys you anything you do not already have. The cases that earn a new mouse map onto the three checks — a wheel that makes long files slow, wide code that means constant scrollbar trips, a shape your hand stops wanting to hold, or arm movement a small desk cannot keep giving room to. Name your case in those terms before shopping: a desk with no failing check points at no purchase at all.

Before you buy: the short pre-buy test

Take one real working session — the longest file and the widest editor layout in your actual work — and answer three questions against it. First: jump from the top of the file to the last function you edited, and back. If that trip is the pain, the free-spin check decides, and only one mouse passes it fully. Second: cross a wide line and move between side-by-side panes without touching the keyboard. If that trip is the pain, the thumb wheel decides, and only one mouse has it. Third: code for an hour and notice what complains — the palm-down angle, a body the wrong size for your hand, or the arm travel itself. A handshake answer names one of the two verticals, split by hand size; an arm-travel answer names the trackball, with its wide-code cost accepted.

Programming mouse questions, answered honestly

Which mouse is best for programming?

Of the four mice we review, the Logitech MX Master 3S is the programming pick, because it passes the two movement checks outright and holds up on comfort for the hands it fits. Through a long file, its MagSpeed wheel free-spins at up to 1,000 lines per second and SmartShift switches modes on its own. Across wide code, it has a separate thumb wheel for horizontal movement — the only horizontal wheel recorded here. Its sculpted palm body suits medium-to-large hands. The two vertical mice are conditional picks bought for their handshake angle, and the Logitech ERGO M575S trackball is the stationary conditional pick, weakest on wide code.

Which of these mice scrolls fastest through a long file?

The Logitech MX Master 3S, and the gap is structural rather than small. Its MagSpeed electromagnetic wheel offers ratchet precision for short hops and a free-spin mode rated at up to 1,000 lines per second for long ones, with SmartShift switching between the modes automatically. The Logitech Lift is next, with a SmartWheel that offers precision and speed-scroll modes. The Logitech MX Vertical has a precision scroll wheel with a middle click only — no free-spin mode is recorded. The Logitech ERGO M575S has a standard scroll wheel with a middle click, and its recorded specs state no free-spin mode.

Do you need a thumb wheel for programming?

Only if your code runs wide or your editor runs side by side — and then it is the check that separates this set most cleanly. Only the Logitech MX Master 3S has a separate thumb wheel for horizontal movement in its recorded specs. The Logitech Lift and the Logitech MX Vertical record no horizontal wheel, and the Logitech ERGO M575S states in its recorded specs that it has no horizontal scrolling. A programmer whose files stay narrow and on one pane may never miss it. A programmer crossing wide lines and split panes all day should treat the thumb wheel as a first-order feature, because no other mouse here offers a wheel-based way across.

Is a vertical mouse good for long coding sessions?

A vertical mouse can be the right programming mouse when the shape is the reason for the change — the movement extras are what it trades away. The Logitech Lift, for small-to-medium hands, holds the hand at a 57-degree handshake angle and keeps a SmartWheel with precision and speed-scroll modes, but records no thumb or horizontal wheel, and its compact body crowds large hands. The Logitech MX Vertical, for medium-to-large hands, holds the same 57-degree angle in a larger rechargeable body, but its wheel is precision-only. Buy either when the handshake shape comes first for a long session, and accept the wide-code check as the cost.

Is the Logitech ERGO M575S trackball good for programming?

Conditionally — it is the stationary answer in this guide, and the weakest answer for wide code. The mouse itself never moves: the thumb rolls a ball to drive the cursor, so there is no arm movement and no desk travel through a long session, its clicks are quiet, and a single AA battery is rated at up to 18 months. Against that, its recorded specs state no horizontal scrolling, its standard wheel has no free-spin mode for long files, and its sensor runs up to 2,000 DPI adjustable only in Logi Options+, with no DPI button on the mouse. Buy it when stillness is the point, not when wide lines and split panes are the daily work.

Bottom Line

Down the file, across the code, through the session — in that order

Run the checks in the order your work runs them: down a long file, across wide code, and through the session. The programming answer is the Logitech MX Master 3S — the only mouse passing both movement checks, with the free-spinning MagSpeed wheel, the separate thumb wheel, and a sculpted body for medium-to-large hands. The angle answers: the Logitech Lift for small-to-medium hands and the Logitech MX Vertical for medium-to-large hands, each bought for its 57-degree shape with the wide-code check traded away.
