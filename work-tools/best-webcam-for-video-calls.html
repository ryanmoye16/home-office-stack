Buying Guide — Webcams

Best Webcam for Video Calls

The short answer first: a video call is decided by light, focus and framing — in that order — and resolution comes last, because the meeting app compresses whatever your camera sends. The four webcams we've reviewed split exactly on those first three things: one buys you low-light headroom and a choice of framing, one is the simplest fixed-focus upgrade that keeps your room out of the frame, one is the proven glass-lens camera that has been getting this job done for a decade, and one buys you 4K headroom for the recording and cropping jobs a meeting app itself will never show. Here's how to tell which problem is actually yours.

Run the light, focus and framing checks before the resolution check

Light first, because no sensor repeals it. Watch your last recorded call back: if your face is dim, grainy or lit from behind, that is a light problem before it is a camera problem. Cameras differ in how gracefully they cope — automatic low-light correction is a real, reviewable difference between the four below — but each one trades detail or smoothness to brighten a dark face. Light on your face, from in front of you, is the cheapest picture upgrade there is; our monitor light bar guide covers the desk-sized way to get it.

Focus second, because it follows how you sit. If you lean toward the screen, gesture, or hold a product, sketch or document up to the lens, autofocus keeps you sharp through the movement. If you sit still at a fixed distance — a monitor set back on the desk, a chair that doesn't travel — a fixed-focus lens set for exactly that seat never hunts, never breathes, and never focuses on the bookshelf instead of you. Neither is better in the abstract; they are answers to different chairs.

Framing third, because it decides what else is on the call. Field of view is measured in degrees, and the spread here is wide: about 59 degrees shows you and little else, 78 fits a second person beside you, and 95 takes in the desk and the wall behind you. Pick the view your calls actually need — a solo client call wants the room kept private, a pair-programming or show-your-work call wants the room in frame — and notice whether the camera fixes that choice at the factory or lets you change it later: two of the four below fix it, and two let you switch it in software.

For dim rooms and framing that changes: Anker PowerConf C200

The C200 is the pick when your calls happen in ordinary, imperfect light and you want the camera to carry more of that burden. Its 2K sensor (2560 × 1440 at 30 fps) sits behind an autofocus lens, and its automatic low-light correction is the feature reviewers praise first: faces stayed bright and readable in monitor-lit, after-dark rooms where laptop cameras fell apart. The second strength is the framing choice — 65, 78 or 95 degrees, switched in the AnkerWork software — so a face-only client call and a show-the-room presentation are the same camera. The narrower views are crops of the 2K sensor, which is exactly what the extra resolution is for. Dual stereo microphones carry noise cancellation with directional or omnidirectional pickup, and the built-in sliding privacy cover turns the camera off when it blocks the lens.

Fit check: the frame rate is 30 fps at every resolution, the body cannot swivel and there is no tracking or auto-framing to follow you around a room, and the manual controls live in software reviewers describe as having rough edges. Judge it as the fixed, meeting-first camera it is: strongest from one chair, in weak light, with framing needs that change between calls.

For the simplest upgrade that keeps the room private: Logitech Brio 101

The Brio 101 is the pick when the brief is a clear 1080p picture with the least possible machinery around it. It is fixed-focus — no autofocus motor, nothing to hunt — and its fixed 58.9-degree field of view is the narrowest of the three, which on a solo call is a privacy feature: the frame holds your face and shoulders and leaves most of the room out of it. RightLight 2 automatic light balance handles ordinary room light, the single built-in microphone is rated for pickup up to 4 feet, and the integrated sliding shutter covers the lens by hand. It is plug-and-play over its attached 5-foot USB-A cable, at 75 grams without the cable.

Fit check: fixed focus is a seat, not a compromise to apologise for — it suits a monitor set back on the desk, and it softens if you sit close or lean in. The shutter covers the lens but does not switch the camera off or mute the microphone, the clip tilts up and down only with no swivel and no tripod thread, and there is no 60 fps mode at any resolution. If your calls are seated, solo and still, those limits rarely fire; if they are not, they are the whole story.

For the proven all-rounder: Logitech C920s HD Pro

The C920s is the pick when a decade of other people's calls counts for more than a newer spec sheet. Its five-element glass lens delivers 1080p at 30 fps with autofocus, its fixed 78-degree view is wide enough for two people at one desk, and its dual stereo microphones are rated to 1 metre. The mount is the quiet differentiator: the universal clip is tripod-ready, so the camera can leave the top of the screen for a desk tripod when that angle is wrong — neither of the other two cameras here can. A detachable clip-on privacy shutter covers the lens, and RightLight 2 handles automatic light correction in reasonably lit rooms.

Fit check: its limits are printed in its age — 1080p at 30 fps is the ceiling, dim rooms cost it frame rate and detail sooner than the C200, and the stereo microphones are serviceable rather than strong, with no noise cancellation claimed. The shutter covers the lens but does not switch the camera off or mute the microphones. Buy it for ordinary calls in ordinary light, where its long record says it simply works.

For recording, cropping and headroom beyond the meeting: Logitech Brio 4K

The Brio 4K is the pick when the camera has a second job beyond the meeting itself. Its 13-megapixel sensor captures 4K (4096 × 2160) at 30 fps behind an autofocus glass lens, and that headroom is spent in three places a meeting app never shows: recording locally at full resolution, streaming, and cropping a sharp 1080p frame out of the wider shot — its 65, 78 and 90-degree fields of view are digital crops of the 4K sensor, which is why they hold detail better than a 1080p camera's crops, and its 5x digital zoom works the same way in Full HD. Its light handling is the widest-ranging of the four: RightLight 3 with HDR corrects exposure and contrast from low light through direct sunlight and backlight, and reviewers found its autofocus and HDR tracking movement and lamp changes seamlessly, producing a superior image at 1080p as well as 4K. It is also the only camera here that signs you in: an infrared sensor handles Windows Hello facial sign-in, and it is the only one that breaks the 30 fps ceiling, with 1080p at 60 fps and 720p at up to 90 fps for smoother motion at those resolutions. Dual omnidirectional microphones carry noise cancellation, rated to 1 metre.

Fit check: the autofocus has a documented finicky streak — reviewers found it locking close and struggling to refocus farther away, and some Mac owners report manual focus as the standing workaround, so its headline convenience can arrive with a manual step attached. The microphones are a fallback, not a reason to buy; the computer end of the cable is USB-A, and 4K needs a USB 3.0 port whatever the plug shape; the privacy shutter is attachable rather than built in, so it can be mislaid, and it covers the lens only without switching the camera off or muting the microphones. One owner found the camera heavy enough on a thin laptop lid to sink it slowly during a call. Buy it only if recording, streaming, cropping or face sign-in is in your actual week — for a plain meeting, the app compresses the 4K away and the plainer cameras above make the same call.

When none of these four is the right buy

Three of these cameras top out at 30 frames per second, and the fourth — the Brio 4K — reaches 60 fps only by stepping down to 1080p (90 fps at 720p). So if your real job needs smooth motion at 4K, none of the four is the tool — that work wants a different class of device entirely, and buying one of these for it ends in a return. The same honesty applies to sound: each camera's microphones are a ready-when-the-headset-is-not fallback, not a recording setup, so a voice-first job — podcasting, narration, teaching all day — should budget for a microphone before it budgets for a sharper lens.

And if your laptop camera already looks fine on the calls that matter — a recent laptop, a bright room, a still seat — the right buy may be no buy. Run the three checks at the top of this guide on a recorded call first; a webcam only earns its desk space by fixing a problem you can actually point to in the recording.

Webcam questions, answered honestly

Do I need a 2K or 4K webcam for Zoom and Teams calls?

For most calls, no. Meeting apps compress and often cap the video they send, so the other side rarely sees your camera's full resolution. What they do see is light, focus and framing. A higher-resolution sensor still helps in one real way: it leaves resolution to spare when a narrower field of view crops the picture, which is how the Anker C200's 65-degree view works — and the Logitech Brio 4K's crops hold detail better than any of the others because they are cut from a 4K sensor. Buy resolution for cropping headroom, not as the headline reason.

Is autofocus worth it on a webcam?

It depends on how much you move and how close you sit. Autofocus earns its place if you lean in, hold things up to the camera, or sit close enough that a fixed lens set for a farther seat softens your face. If you sit still at a monitor set back on the desk — the seat the Logitech Brio 101's fixed focus was designed around — a fixed lens is one less thing hunting in the background of every call.

Which field of view is best for video calls?

The narrowest one that still frames you naturally. A wide view — 78 degrees and up — fits two people or a whiteboard, but on a solo call it mostly shows your room. The Brio 101's fixed 58.9 degrees keeps a solo frame tight and private; the C920s' fixed 78 degrees suits a two-person seat; the C200 lets you switch between 65, 78 and 95 degrees in software, with the narrower views cropped from its 2K sensor; and the Brio 4K switches between 65, 78 and 90 degrees, cropped from its 4K sensor.

Can a better webcam fix a dark room?

It can soften the problem, not solve it. The C200's automatic low-light correction is the strongest low-light handling of the four cameras we review, and reviewers found faces stayed bright in monitor-lit rooms; the Brio 4K's RightLight 3 with HDR is the strongest of the four against backlight, where a bright window behind you would otherwise plunge your face into shadow. But every webcam trades something in the dark — detail, frame rate, or both — so light on your face still comes first. A monitor light bar or a lamp aimed at you does more for a dim call than any sensor upgrade.

Do these webcams work without extra software?

Yes — all four are plug-and-play over USB with Zoom, Teams, Google Meet and the other major apps. The optional software is where the adjustments live: the C200's field-of-view switch and microphone modes are in AnkerWork, and Logitech's apps hold brightness and picture controls for the Brio 101, C920s and Brio 4K. The cameras work fully without it; the software is for tuning, not for getting on a call.

When is a 4K webcam actually worth it?

When the camera does a second job beyond meetings: recording locally, streaming, or cropping a 1080p frame out of a wider shot without losing sharpness — the work the Logitech Brio 4K's sensor headroom exists for. Its infrared sensor also signs a Windows user in by face, which none of the other three cameras here offers. For meetings alone, a meeting app compresses 4K down to the same call the other cameras make, so the headroom never reaches the other side of the call.

Bottom Line

Check light, focus and framing — then pick

Watch a recorded call, name the problem you can see, and buy the camera that fixes that one: dim rooms and changing framing point to the Anker PowerConf C200; a still, solo seat that wants the simplest private frame points to the Logitech Brio 101; ordinary light, two-person framing and a mount that can move point to the Logitech C920s; recording, cropping, smoother motion below 4K or Windows Hello face sign-in point to the Logitech Brio 4K.
