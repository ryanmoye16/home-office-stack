Best Smart Plug for Energy Monitoring

Check one: what the monitoring actually reports

Energy monitoring is not one feature; it is three different answers wearing one label. The first answer is real-time draw — what the device is pulling right now, the number you watch while a heater cycles or a desktop wakes. The second is the history — how much energy it used across days, weeks and months, the record that turns a suspicion about standby draw into evidence. The third is cost estimates — the app translating energy used into money, which is only ever as good as the electricity rate someone typed in. Read any monitoring claim by asking which of the three answers it gives, because the useful purchase is usually the history, not the live number.

The two monitoring plugs here split that honestly. The Kasa EP25 records real-time and historical draw, in those terms, in the Kasa app. The Tapo P110M builds its case on the longer view: daily, monthly and yearly use in the Tapo app, plus cost estimates once you enter your electricity rate. A standby hunt — the job owners describe most — lives in history: a device drawing a little, all night, every night, is invisible in a single real-time glance and obvious across a week of records. Cost estimates then answer whether the draw you found is worth acting on, using a rate you supplied yourself.

The third plug gives none of the three answers. The Amazon Smart Plug records its energy monitoring specification as, literally, no. It reports nothing about power draw — no live figure, no history, no estimate. That is not a weaker version of monitoring; it is the absence of it, and it settles that plug's role here before any other feature is discussed. Keep it in this guide for one honest reason: a reader who only needs simple Alexa on and off control should be told plainly that this plug is that product, and that buying it for measurement would be buying the wrong tool.

Check two: where the data lives — the central caveat

For both monitoring plugs, the richest data lives in the vendor app, not the platform app. It is the caveat that decides whether monitoring stays useful after the first week. The Tapo P110M can be controlled across Alexa, Google Assistant, Siri, SmartThings and Apple Home over Matter, and the Kasa EP25 through Siri and Apple Home alongside Alexa, Google Assistant and SmartThings — but control travelling is not the same as data travelling. The history, the breakdowns and, for Tapo, the cost estimates sit in the Tapo and Kasa apps respectively. A household living inside one platform app will still open a second, vendor app every time the numbers are the point.

The Tapo version of that split has an extra wrinkle, recorded in its review. Energy data is richest in the Tapo app; what carries over into a Matter platform has depended on firmware and platform support. Some energy information may appear in a platform app, and what appears can change — which is why this guide does not promise platform-side monitoring for the P110M. If you buy the Tapo plug for monitoring, plan to read the monitoring in the Tapo app, and treat anything a platform app shows as a bonus rather than the record.

The Kasa version is simpler and just as firm. Energy history lives in the Kasa app, so checking consumption means opening Kasa even if day-to-day control happens in Apple Home or through a voice assistant. Owners live with that split in a pattern our EP25 review records: voice and the platform app for everyday switching, the vendor app reserved for energy history and schedule changes. Neither plug needs a hub or a subscription, which keeps the caveat about inconvenience rather than cost — but the second app is still there. If a second app is a deal-breaker, the honest conclusion is that neither monitoring plug removes it, whatever platform the box advertises.

Check three: load and control limits

All three plugs share the same electrical ceiling: 15A / 1800W. Load capacity never separates these picks — monitoring and platform do that — so there is no higher-rated option hiding elsewhere in this comparison. The ceiling also sets the boundary for the high-draw devices owners most often want to measure, space heaters and desktop equipment among them. The figure that matters is the connected device's own stated draw, read off that device's rating label. Judge the label against the ceiling and stay visibly under it. A device drawing at or over the rating does not belong on any of the three plugs, and a plug faithfully reporting an over-limit load does not make running it a sensible idea.

Control has a separate limit, and it is about behaviour, not wattage. A smart plug works by cutting and restoring power. It cannot press another device's power button or restore the state it was in before the power went. So a plug genuinely controls only devices that resume when power returns — the lamps, fans, space heaters and coffee makers with physical switches that fill owner reports — and it cannot wake a device that restores into standby and waits for its own button. That device will sit off when the schedule restores power, however dependable the plug and however accurate its monitoring. The check takes seconds: unplug the device while it is on, plug it back in, and see whether it resumes. That unplug and replug test is the whole compatibility question for control, just as the rating label is the whole question for load.

Coverage is the third limit, and it shapes the method below. One plug covers one outlet. It measures and controls the single device plugged into it — not a strip's worth of desk gear as separate devices, not the room, not the desk as a whole. A multi-device desk is measured one device at a time, moving a single plug from suspect to suspect, or with several plugs, each reporting its own device separately in the vendor app. Body size matters at that point: the EP25's slim 2.62 by 1.5 by 1.57 inch body stacks two plugs in one duplex outlet, the P110M's compact body is designed to avoid blocking the adjacent outlet though owners report two side by side can still feel tight, and the Amazon plug is the largest of the three at 3.2 by 1.5 by 2.2 inches.

The method: how owners actually hunt standby draw

Put the plug on one suspect device, and only one. The owner-reported method is boring in the way that works: choose the device you actually suspect — desk gear that stays warm, a TV that never fully sleeps, a heater whose running pattern nobody knows — plug that single device through the monitoring plug, and resist measuring everything at once. One device per plug is a limit from the last check; as a method, it is also what makes the history readable. A record belonging to one named device can be acted on. A merged record from a crowded strip can only be wondered at.

Then let the history build over ordinary days, not a single test hour. Standby draw is a pattern, not an event. It hides in the hours nobody watches — overnight, during the workday, across a weekend away — and a device can look innocent across one supervised hour while drawing steadily through every unobserved one. Ordinary days are what the history features were built for: the EP25's historical record in the Kasa app, the P110M's daily, monthly and yearly views in the Tapo app. Give the suspect its normal life, including the idle stretches, and read the record afterwards. Owners who skip this step conclude monitoring told them nothing; owners who take it end up naming specific devices, which is the entire point of buying a monitoring plug instead of a plain one.

Do the energy maths yourself before deciding what the finding is worth. A device's draw in watts, multiplied by the hours it draws for, divided by 1000, gives kilowatt-hours. Multiply that by the rate on your own bill and you have the running cost — the same basis the Tapo app's cost estimates use once you enter your rate there. This guide quotes no rate and no running-cost figures, deliberately: rates differ by household, and a cost built on a stranger's rate is a fiction with decimals. A small draw sounds trivial in watts and stops sounding trivial when the hours are every hour, all year — and a large draw on a heater used briefly can matter less than a modest draw that never stops. History supplies the hours; your bill supplies the rate; the product of the two is the finding.

Finally, act with the controls the plug actually has. A finding earns its keep only when it changes a schedule. Both monitoring plugs schedule in their vendor apps: the P110M adds Auto-Off and Away Mode, and the EP25 offers custom schedules alongside sunrise and sunset patterns. The typical actions follow directly from a standby finding — schedule the device off overnight, set an Auto-Off so a heater or fan cannot run on indefinitely, or cut power to a device whose idle draw bought it nothing. Apply the control check before automating anything: the device has to resume when power returns, or the morning version of the schedule is a device sitting off, waiting for its own button. Monitoring finds the waste; scheduling, applied to the right kind of device, removes it.

When none of these is the right buy

If the device you want to measure draws at or over the shared 15A / 1800W ceiling, none of these plugs belongs between it and the wall — that job needs a different class of meter. If your household runs Thread, Zigbee or Z-Wave rather than Wi-Fi and Matter over Wi-Fi, neither monitoring plug joins it: the EP25 has no such radio and the P110M is a Wi-Fi Matter plug. And if you will never open a vendor app to read a history, be honest about that before paying for monitoring — the data lives there, and unused monitoring is just a more complicated on and off switch. The simple plug above, or a plain timer, is the honest buy instead.

Energy monitoring questions, answered honestly

Where do I actually see the energy data from a monitoring smart plug?

In the vendor app, for both monitoring plugs we review. The Tapo app shows the P110M's daily, monthly and yearly use and its cost estimates, and the Kasa app shows the EP25's real-time and historical draw. What carries into a platform app — Apple Home, Alexa, Google Home or SmartThings — can be less, and for the Tapo plug it has depended on firmware and platform. Expect to open the vendor app whenever the numbers are the point.

Can a smart plug tell me what a device costs to run?

Partly. The Tapo app produces cost estimates for the P110M once you enter your own electricity rate, and the Kasa app's history for the EP25 shows how much energy a device used over time. The maths is yours to check either way: watts multiplied by the hours run, divided by 1000, gives kilowatt-hours, and that multiplied by the rate on your own electricity bill gives the running cost. We quote no rate here because yours is the only one that makes the answer true.

Can one monitoring plug measure my whole desk?

No. One plug covers one outlet, so it measures the one device plugged into it. A multi-device desk is measured one device at a time — move the plug from suspect to suspect and let a history build for each — or with several plugs, each reporting its own device separately in the vendor app. There is no setting that turns a single-outlet plug into a whole-desk meter.

Can a monitoring plug run a space heater or another high-draw device?

Only inside the shared ceiling. All three plugs we review are rated 15A / 1800W, and space heaters are among the devices owners most often monitor with the two monitoring plugs. The check is the heater's own rating label, not the plug's marketing: judge the device's stated draw against that ceiling and stay visibly under it. A device that draws at or over the rating does not belong on any of these plugs, and monitoring a load does not make an over-limit load safe.

Why does a device stay off when the plug turns the power back on?

Because a smart plug can only cut and restore power; it cannot press another device's own power button. It genuinely controls devices that resume when power returns — lamps, fans and heaters with physical switches are the common owner examples — and it cannot wake a device that restores into standby and waits for its own button. Test it before blaming the plug: unplug it while it is on, plug it back in, and see whether it resumes. That test is the whole control question.

Buy the history, and plan to read it in the vendor app
