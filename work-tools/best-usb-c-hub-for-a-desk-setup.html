Buying Guide — USB-C Hubs

Best USB-C Hub for a Desk Setup

A travel hub is judged in a bag. A desk hub is judged by what stays plugged into it for months — a network cable, external drives, one good screen, and the charger — because at a desk a hub's ceilings stop being trip tolerances and become permanent. Our two reviewed Anker hubs split that job cleanly: one was built for the desk, the other visits it. Here's the honest split.

Check 1: what stays connected all day

On the road, a hub's port list is a menu you pick from for a few hours. At a desk, it is infrastructure: the peripherals, drives and cables you plug in on Monday are still plugged in on Friday, and the hub's slowest port sets the pace of everything behind it, every day. That is why the desk decision starts with the two ports a trip barely notices.

Wired Ethernet. Only one of our two hubs has it: the Anker 555 builds in Gigabit Ethernet, and it is the feature its owners single out most — a wired connection stays stable on video calls when Wi-Fi does not. At a permanent desk the cable is run once and forgotten, which removes the only real argument against wired networking. The Anker 7-in-1 has no Ethernet port at all, and no adapter-free workaround exists.

Drive speed. The 555's USB-C data port and both USB-A ports run at 10Gbps, so an external SSD is not bottlenecked by the hub. The 7-in-1's data ports run at 5Gbps — half the ceiling. For a keyboard, a mouse and the occasional card import, 5Gbps is invisible. For a desk where an SSD lives permanently — backups, photo libraries, project drives — it is a toll paid on every large transfer, forever. Both hubs carry SD and microSD slots, and on the 7-in-1 owners can use both at the same time, a detail photographers single out when copying a camera card and a drone card in one session.

Check 2: the one screen it drives every day

Each hub has exactly one HDMI output, so each drives exactly one external display — that is the shared limit, and no desk arrangement changes it. What differs is the quality of that one screen. The 555 runs 4K at 60Hz from a DP 1.4 host (4K at 30Hz from a DP 1.2 host). The 7-in-1 caps 4K at 30Hz, with 1080p and 1440p running at a smooth 60Hz. Owners find 4K at 30Hz fine for documents, presentations and media, and noticeably less smooth for heavy window management — and at a desk, window management is the job, eight hours a day. On a 1080p or 1440p desk monitor the gap disappears entirely, which is the 7-in-1's honest desk case.

Neither hub's USB-C ports carry a video signal — Anker states this plainly for the 555, and the 7-in-1 is the same — so a USB-C-to-HDMI cable out of a data port buys you nothing. And if the desk you are building has two monitors, neither hub is the answer on any laptop: on a Mac the chip may cap you at one external display before the hub even matters (our MacBook hub guide runs those limits chip by chip), and on Windows a second screen needs a dock or a second port, not a second HDMI that a single-output hub does not have.

Check 3: power and placement, all day

Both hubs take up to 100W of PD input, reserve 15W for the hub itself, and deliver up to 85W to the laptop — full speed for a MacBook Air and most ultrabooks, and adequate for a 14-inch MacBook Pro while every other port stays active. Two conditions decide whether you actually get that 85W. First, feed the hub a 100W PD charger: pass-through power depends on the adapter, and Anker's guidance and owner reports agree that a weak adapter is the first suspect when an external display flickers. Second, know the exception: a 16-inch MacBook Pro that ships with a 140W adapter charges more slowly through either hub under sustained heavy load — give it its own power port and let the hub run data and video.

Placement is the check desk buyers skip and then live with. Both hubs hang off a short attached cable — on the 7-in-1 it is the most common owner complaint, because it keeps the hub close to the laptop, limits placement and can strain the port on some desk setups. Plan the desk around that reach: hub within cable distance of the laptop's port, on a hard surface with air around it. Warmth under full load is normal for this class, not a failure sign — Anker itself notes the 555's shell can reach about 122°F when every port is busy with a PD adapter attached, and reviewers report the 7-in-1's warmth as moderate rather than a problem. Just don't bury either hub under papers or a desk mat, and don't run it on fabric.

The desk pick: Anker 555 USB-C Hub (8-in-1)

The 555's own job description is a desk: one cable into the laptop, and the desk is connected — monitor, network, drives, cards and charging. It is the only hub here with Gigabit Ethernet, the only one whose data ports run at 10Gbps, and the only one that puts a 4K screen at 60Hz. Those are precisely the three capabilities a permanent setup uses daily and a trip uses rarely, which is why this guide's checks keep landing on it. Owner feedback is consistent with the spec sheet: the Ethernet connection is the reliability win, the 4K HDMI output is stable for office and media work, and 10Gbps transfers keep external SSDs at full speed.

Desk notes, honestly: it runs warm fully loaded — Anker's own figure is about 122°F at the shell with every port busy and a PD adapter attached — so give it a clear patch of desk, not a drawer. Its aluminum body is the larger of the two at 4.76 × 2.17 × 0.6 inches and 4.5 oz, which is irrelevant on a desk and was only ever a travel tax. And its one HDMI port is a hard ceiling: this is a one-screen desk hub, full stop.

The smaller desk: Anker 7-in-1 USB-C Hub

The 7-in-1 is the travel pick in Anker's lineup — that is its own review's verdict, and this guide isn't going to pretend otherwise. But a desk can honestly be a small job: a 1080p or 1440p monitor, solid Wi-Fi, a keyboard and mouse on USB-A, card imports through both slots at once, and no fast SSD living on the desk. That desk exists in plenty of home offices, and the 7-in-1 runs it completely — stable connections under load per owner feedback, both card slots reading simultaneously, and the same honest 85W pass-through charging as its bigger sibling.

Fit check: buy it for a desk only with its ceilings in view, because at a desk they don't reset after the trip. No Ethernet, ever. Data at 5Gbps, so the day a fast external SSD moves onto the desk permanently, the hub becomes its bottleneck. And 4K stays at 30Hz. If none of those touches your setup — and your monitor tops out at 1440p — the smaller hub is a legitimate desk hub. The moment one of them does, you are describing the 555, and buying the 7-in-1 first just rents the lesson.

Setting the desk up, in order

A desk hub earns its keep in the first ten minutes and then disappears, so set it up in the order the checks ran. Power first: connect a 100W PD charger to the hub's PD input before anything else, so the laptop's 85W is there from the first plug-in and a weak adapter never gets to introduce itself as a flickering screen. Network second: on the 555, run the Ethernet cable once, while the desk is apart — it is the one cable you should never have to touch again. Screen third: the single HDMI to your one monitor, at the refresh rate your host actually supports — 60Hz at 4K from a DP 1.4 host on the 555, 30Hz on the 7-in-1.

Drives and peripherals last: put the fastest drive on the fastest port — on the 555, the 10Gbps USB-C data port — and the permanent peripherals on USB-A, where they can stay. Keep the card slots reachable rather than buried behind the monitor; on a desk that imports photos, slot access is a daily ergonomic, and both hubs' slots sit on the body you place once. Then stop touching it. A desk hub that needs re-plugging every morning has failed its one job, and on the owner feedback behind both reviews, a correctly fed hub — right charger, clear surface, cables seated — is the one that holds its connections under load, day after day.

When neither hub is the right buy

Step up to different hardware if any of these is you:

• You need two external monitors at the desk — each hub has one HDMI output. Look at a Thunderbolt dock with independent video outputs; on a base M1/M2 Mac, add a DisplayLink adapter to the conversation, accepting its driver and its limits with protected video content.
• You run a 16-inch MacBook Pro hard all day and want full-speed one-cable charging — a powered Thunderbolt dock, or the laptop's own power port for charging plus a hub for everything else.
• You need 2.5GbE networking, several fast SSDs at full speed, or a 3.5mm audio jack — none of that is in either port list; that is dock territory, not hub territory.

We'd rather route you to the right category than sell you a hub whose ceiling your desk hits in the first week. Until a dock is reviewed here, buy on the dock's per-output video specs and its published power budget, not on a listing photo showing three glowing screens.

Desk hub questions, answered honestly

What makes a hub a desk hub instead of a travel hub?

Permanence. A travel hub is judged in a bag — packed size, the ports a trip uses, the charger you feed it. A desk hub is judged by what stays plugged into it for months: a network cable, external drives, a monitor, peripherals, and the charger. That flips the priorities. The ports a trip never misses — Gigabit Ethernet and 10Gbps data — are the ones a desk feels every day, and the ceilings a trip tolerates — 4K at 30Hz, 5Gbps transfers — become permanent at a desk. Neither hub in this guide is a different product at a desk; the same two hubs are simply being judged on the desk's checks instead of the road's.

Do I need Ethernet if my Wi-Fi works?

Not always — but a desk is where the case for it is strongest. Owners of the Anker 555 single out its built-in Gigabit Ethernet most: a wired connection stays stable on video calls when Wi-Fi does not, and at a permanent desk the cable is plugged in once and forgotten. If your Wi-Fi is genuinely solid all day, Ethernet is a nice-to-have rather than a need — that is the honest case for the smaller 7-in-1. If calls drop, the router is a room away, or your work network restricts Wi-Fi, Ethernet stops being optional, and only the 555 has it.

Is 85W pass-through charging enough for a desk setup?

For most laptops, yes. Both hubs take up to 100W of PD input, reserve 15W for the hub, and deliver up to 85W to the laptop — full speed for a MacBook Air and most ultrabooks, and adequate for a 14-inch MacBook Pro while every other port stays active. The exception is a 16-inch MacBook Pro that ships with a 140W adapter: it charges more slowly through either hub under sustained heavy load. Feed the hub with a 100W PD charger — a weaker adapter is the first suspect when a display flickers — or give a big laptop its own power port and let the hub handle data and video.

Where should a desk hub sit, and is warmth normal?

Within reach of the laptop's USB-C port — both hubs hang off a short attached cable, the most common owner complaint about the 7-in-1 — and on a hard surface with air around it. Warmth under load is normal, not a failure sign: Anker itself notes the 555's shell can reach about 122°F when every port is busy with a power adapter attached, and reviewers report the 7-in-1's warmth as moderate and manageable. Do not bury either hub under papers or a desk mat, and do not run it on a couch or bed; a clear patch of desk is the whole requirement.

Can either hub run two monitors on my desk?

No. Each hub has exactly one HDMI output, so each drives exactly one external display — on any laptop. On a Mac the chip may lower the ceiling further: base M1 and M2 Macs support one external display natively, and macOS does not extend a desktop across dual-HDMI hubs. Two desk monitors are a different product class — a Thunderbolt dock with independent video outputs, or a DisplayLink adapter on a Mac whose chip limits it to one. Buy a hub in this guide for one good screen plus ports and power, not for a second screen.

Bottom Line

Judge a desk hub by what never leaves it

For a permanent one-screen desk, the Anker 555 is the pick: it is the only reviewed hub with the desk's three daily capabilities — Gigabit Ethernet, 10Gbps data and 4K at 60Hz — on one cable with honest 85W charging. The Anker 7-in-1 honestly runs the smaller desk — a 1440p-or-under screen, solid Wi-Fi, peripherals and card slots, no resident fast SSD — and stays the pick to pack when that same laptop travels.
