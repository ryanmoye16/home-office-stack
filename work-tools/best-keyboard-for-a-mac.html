Buying Guide — Keyboards

Best Keyboard for a Mac

The short answer first: every keyboard we review lists macOS in its compatibility line, so "works with Mac" is not the question — the Mac experience is decided by three quieter checks. The login screen: FileVault can keep a Bluetooth keyboard from answering before you have logged in, and two of these boards carry that warning in their own documentation. The box: three boards include the USB receiver that answers the login problem, and the compact one that ships without one is the board whose manual advises a receiver most plainly. And what else Apple it serves: three boards list iPadOS, one lists iOS too, and the split ergonomic board lists neither. One board answers all three checks cleanly. One answers them in a smaller frame, for one extra purchase. One answers them with mechanical switches. And one answers them with a split frame, a two-year battery, and the oldest receiver standard here.

Check one: the login screen — FileVault is where Bluetooth can fail a Mac keyboard

Start before the desktop loads, because that is the one moment a Mac treats a wireless keyboard differently. With FileVault disk encryption enabled, a Bluetooth keyboard may not be able to enter your password at startup or reboot — the connection the board types on all day is not guaranteed at the login screen. This is not a forum rumour we are repeating: it is recorded in the documentation of two of the four boards on this page. The Logitech MX Mechanical's own specifications carry Logitech's note that on a FileVault-enabled Mac, Bluetooth may not enter your password at startup, with the included Logi Bolt receiver named as the fix for that step. The Logitech MX Keys Mini's manual advises the same receiver route, in the words its review records: FileVault can prevent Bluetooth devices from connecting before you have logged in. A Mac buyer who never enables FileVault never meets this check; a Mac buyer who does meets it at every reboot, with the built-in laptop keyboard as the fallback on a MacBook and no fallback at all on a desktop Mac.

So the receiver in the box stops being an accessory question and becomes a login question. The MX Keys S and the MX Mechanical each include a Logi Bolt receiver; the Ergo K860 includes a Logitech Unifying receiver; the MX Keys Mini includes no receiver at all — Bluetooth only in the box, working with a Bolt receiver bought separately, and not compatible with the older Unifying receivers many Logitech desks already run. Read that against check one and the Mini's shape changes: it is the board whose own manual points at a receiver, sold without one. That does not disqualify it — for a MacBook, whose built-in keyboard covers the rare login, Bluetooth-only may cost nothing — but on a desktop Mac with FileVault on, the receiver is part of the Mini's real purchase, and this guide prices it that way in the pick below.

Check two: legends and software — all four are labelled for Mac, and that is only the start

On legends, the four boards tie — honestly. Every keyboard here labels its key legends and function keys for both Windows and Mac: the MX Keys S, the MX Keys Mini, the MX Mechanical and the Ergo K860 alike. No board on this page asks a Mac user to guess which key is Command. Logitech also sells separate for Mac versions of the MX Keys S and the MX Keys Mini, with Mac-only legends under different models and ASINs; those are different listings we have not reviewed, and nothing in this guide — no figure, no verdict — carries across to them. The boards covered here are the dual-legend versions, which is itself a Mac-desk advantage for anyone who also keeps a Windows machine: one board, correctly labelled for both, switching between up to three devices on Easy-Switch keys.

Software is where the tie breaks. Full customisation on these boards runs through Logitech's Options software on Windows or Mac — the K860's review records function-key and shortcut customising in Options on either system — and the MX Keys S makes the app effectively required rather than optional: its F row defaults to its printed system actions instead of F1–F12, and setting the row to standard function keys is done in Logi Options+, as its review's most-asked question records. The same software layer carries Flow on the MX boards, moving the pointer and copy-paste between computers — including between a Windows machine and a Mac, as the MX Keys S's owner evidence describes. Judge the software check plainly: a Mac buyer gets the full feature set on macOS with all four boards, but a buyer who refuses vendor software on principle should know the F-row relearn and the customisation layer live in that app, not on the board.

Check three: the rest of the Apple desk — iPad coverage and USB-C power

A Mac keyboard often moonlights, and the compatibility lines split on what else it can serve. The MX Keys S lists iPadOS alongside macOS; the MX Mechanical lists iPadOS too; the MX Keys Mini lists iPadOS and iOS — the widest Apple coverage of the four, fitting the board built to move. The Ergo K860's recorded compatibility is Windows, macOS, Chrome OS and Linux, with no iPadOS listed. All four switch across three devices, so the switching is not the limit — the printed compatibility is. A reader whose second screen is an iPad on a stand has a real check here, and the split board fails it on paper.

Power, by contrast, suits a Mac desk on three boards and sidesteps it on the fourth. The MX Keys S, MX Keys Mini and MX Mechanical each recharge over USB-C — the cable a current MacBook already charges with — with backlight-driven ratings: up to 10 days backlit or 5 months dark for the two MX Keys boards, up to 15 days backlit or 10 months dark for the Mechanical, the longest backlit rating here. The Ergo K860 takes two AAA batteries rated for up to 24 months instead: no cable ever, at the cost of disposable cells, and no backlight at any setting — the trade its two-year rating is bought with. One port note closes the check: the receivers here are USB-A, and a current MacBook offers USB-C ports only, so a receiver on a MacBook needs an adapter or a hub — our USB-C hubs for MacBook guide covers the two hubs we review. The Mac pick: Logitech MX Keys S

The MX Keys S is the only board here that passes all three Mac checks with nothing added. Its recorded compatibility covers macOS and iPadOS, its legends are labelled for Windows and Mac, and the Logi Bolt receiver is in the box — so the receiver route that two sibling manuals point to for a FileVault login is available on day one, on Bluetooth or receiver as the desk prefers. It recharges over USB-C and keeps working while plugged in, its smart backlight wakes as your hands approach and adjusts to the room, and the typing itself is the feature reviewers and owners praise first: spherically dished scissor keys that centre each fingertip and stay quiet doing it, with Easy-Switch across three devices and Flow moving text and files between a Windows machine and a Mac once Options+ is configured.

Fit check: the backlight is a trade with steep terms — rated up to 10 days with it on against up to 5 months with it off, and owner reports match the arithmetic. The F row defaults to system actions rather than F1–F12 until Logi Options+ sets it, so the app is part of the purchase. Bolt is not compatible with Unifying, so an older Unifying mouse cannot share this receiver. And the receiver is USB-A, so a USB-C-only MacBook needs the adapter or hub from check three to use it. Inside those lines, it is the complete Mac desk keyboard of the four. Our full review records the whole picture.

The compact Mac pick: Logitech MX Keys Mini

The Mini carries the MX Keys typing feel — the same dished Perfect Stroke scissor keys, the same smart backlight, the same battery ratings — into an 11.65-inch frame with no numpad pushing the mouse arm wide, and its recorded compatibility is the widest Apple line here: macOS, iPadOS and iOS, with dual Windows/Mac legends. For a Mac desk that also serves an iPad, or a small desk where the mouse reach matters more than a numpad, it is the natural pick — the board our small-hands guide and travel guide keep arriving at from their own angles.

Fit check: one condition, stated as plainly as its own review states it. This listing is Bluetooth only in the box — no receiver is included, it works with a Logi Bolt receiver bought separately, and Unifying receivers cannot pair with it. Its manual advises that Bolt receiver for a FileVault login, so a desktop-Mac reader with FileVault on should count the receiver as part of the purchase, and every reader should know the box holds a keyboard, a USB-C charging cable and documentation, nothing more. There is also no wired typing mode: the cable charges only. With the receiver question answered honestly, the rest of its Mac case is the strongest per inch here. Our full review covers the rest.

The mechanical Mac pick: Logitech MX Mechanical

The MX Mechanical exists for the reader who misses a mechanical bump, and its Mac paperwork is the most explicit here: its recorded specifications are the ones that carry Logitech's FileVault note — Bluetooth may not enter your password at startup on a FileVault-enabled Mac; use the Bolt receiver for that step — and the Bolt receiver is included, so the documented problem arrives with its documented fix in the same box. Compatibility covers macOS and iPadOS with dual legends, the Tactile Quiet switches give a soft, rounded bump reviewers put near a high-quality laptop keyboard's sound, the backlight rating is the longest here at up to 15 days on or 10 months off, and it recharges over USB-C across three Easy-Switch devices.

Fit check: the switches are soldered, not hot-swappable — the Tactile Quiet feel of this listing is the feel for the board's life, and the Clicky and Linear versions are different listings chosen at purchase. The construction noise is the criticism that follows this board: reviewers note a slightly hollow spacebar and stabiliser rattle, and PCWorld criticised rattle in the construction outright, which is why its owner rating is the most divided in our keyboard coverage. The charging cable carries power only; typing stays wireless. Buy it when mechanical feel is the point and the rattle reports are read first. Our full review makes the full case.

Customising its function keys and shortcuts runs in Logitech's Options software on Mac as on Windows, it switches across three devices over Bluetooth or its included receiver, and its two AAA batteries are rated for up to 24 months — the board you can forget to think about for two years at a time. When the keyboard your Mac came with is the right answer

Price the option already on the desk first. None of the four boards here offers fingerprint sign-in, and none is the keyboard a MacBook already carries under your hands — for a laptop used at its own keyboard in decent light, the honest purchase may be no purchase, or a laptop stand that makes the built-in board sit at the right height, which our laptop stand for posture guide works through. The cases that do earn an external keyboard are specific: a desktop Mac or a closed-laptop setup that needs a board at the desk rather than at the screen, wrists that need a split frame or a wrist rest no laptop offers, a mechanical feel, a numpad a compact laptop board lacks, or one board switching between a Mac and the other machines on the desk. Name your case before you shop — each one points at a different pick above.

Before you buy: the two-minute Mac test

Two minutes in your Mac's settings tells you which version of this guide you are shopping in. First, the encryption test: check whether FileVault is on. If it is, the login check is live for you — favour a board with a receiver in the box, or count a Bolt receiver in the Mini's purchase, and plan the adapter a USB-A receiver needs on a USB-C-only MacBook. If FileVault is off, Bluetooth-only costs you nothing at login and the Mini's missing receiver is a footnote. Second, the household test: list what else the board must serve — an iPad, a Windows work laptop, a Bolt or Unifying mouse already on the desk. That list, read against the compatibility and receiver lines in the table above, usually names the pick by itself. Then buy that board, and on the day it arrives, reboot the Mac once with the new board as the only keyboard, inside the return window, while the login behaviour on your machine is still a fact you can act on.

Mac keyboard questions, answered honestly

Which keyboard is best for a Mac?

Of the four keyboards we review, the Logitech MX Keys S. Its recorded compatibility covers macOS with key legends labelled for both Windows and Mac, it includes a Logi Bolt USB receiver in the box — the receiver route Logitech's own manuals point to when FileVault blocks Bluetooth at the login screen — it recharges over USB-C, and its full-size layout with smart backlighting is the most complete Mac desk package here. Its honest costs are the backlight's battery trade (rated up to 10 days with backlighting on, up to 5 months with it off) and an F row that defaults to system actions until Logi Options+ sets it to standard function keys.

Does the MX Keys Mini work at a Mac login screen with FileVault on?

With one Logitech-documented caveat, recorded in its own review: FileVault can prevent Bluetooth devices from connecting before you have logged in, and Logitech's manual for this model advises a compatible Logi Bolt receiver in that setup. This listing includes no receiver — it is Bluetooth only in the box, and it is not compatible with older Unifying receivers — so a FileVault Mac user should treat a separately bought Bolt receiver as part of the purchase. The MX Mechanical's recorded specifications carry the same FileVault note, but its Bolt receiver is included.

Should I buy the 'for Mac' version of one of these keyboards instead?

The versions reviewed here are the dual-legend ones, and they are the ones this guide can speak for. Logitech sells separate for Mac versions of the MX Keys S and the MX Keys Mini with Mac-only legends, under different models and ASINs; we have not reviewed those listings, and their ratings and prices are their own. The dual-legend boards covered here list macOS in their recorded compatibility and label their keys for both Windows and Mac, so they serve a Mac desk and a mixed Mac-and-Windows desk alike. Choose a for Mac listing only with its own listing read on its own terms — do not carry this guide's figures across to it.

Will the receiver in the box plug straight into a current MacBook?

Not directly. A current MacBook offers USB-C ports only, and the receivers here — Logi Bolt on the MX Keys S and the MX Mechanical, Unifying on the Ergo K860 — are USB-A, so on a MacBook they need an adapter or a USB-C hub; our MacBook hub guide covers the two hubs we review. Bluetooth avoids the port question entirely for ordinary use, which is why the FileVault login check matters: the receiver earns its place at the one moment Bluetooth may not answer. On a Mac with a USB-A port, each included receiver plugs straight in.

Do all four keyboards work with an iPad as well as a Mac?

Three of the four list it and one does not. The MX Keys S and the MX Mechanical list iPadOS in their recorded compatibility, and the MX Keys Mini lists iPadOS and iOS — the widest Apple coverage here, fitting its role as the board that moves between machines. The Ergo K860's recorded compatibility is Windows, macOS, Chrome OS and Linux, with no iPadOS listed, so an iPad side desk is a check the split board fails on paper even though its Bluetooth pairing is the same kind. All four connect to up to three devices with Easy-Switch keys, so the machines a board can switch between are decided by that compatibility line, not by the switching itself.

Bottom Line

Run the login screen first — then let the shape choose

Run the checks in order: settle FileVault and the receiver, confirm the legends and software serve your machines, and check the iPad and port lines against your desk. The complete Mac answer: the Logitech MX Keys S, receiver included, dual-legend, USB-C powered. The compact answer: the Logitech MX Keys Mini, with the widest Apple compatibility here and a receiver to budget for if FileVault is on. The mechanical answer: the Logitech MX Mechanical, whose own specs name the login caveat and whose box answers it. And the ergonomic answer: the Logitech Ergo K860, split and two-year-batteried, on the older receiver standard.
