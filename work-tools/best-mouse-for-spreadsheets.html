Buying Guide — Mice

Best Mouse for Spreadsheets and Data Work

Spreadsheet work asks a mouse three very specific questions, and they are not the questions a general guide asks. Down: when a sheet runs to hundreds or thousands of rows, how fast does the wheel travel — and how cleanly does it stop? Across: when columns run wider than the screen, is there a wheel that moves sideways, or does every wide sheet become a drag on the scrollbar? And onto the cell: when the target is one small rectangle among thousands, how precisely can the sensor be set, and how quickly can that setting change? Four mice we review answer them very differently: one passes all three, two pass on the strength of their shape, and one is named plainly as the skip for this job — while remaining the right mouse for a different desk.

Check one: down a long sheet — the vertical scroll test

The Logitech MX Master 3S passes this check fully, and the reason is its MagSpeed electromagnetic wheel. In ratchet mode it moves with the stepped precision short hops need. Flick it harder and it free-spins at up to 1,000 lines per second — the mode for a sheet whose total sits far below the visible rows. SmartShift switches between those modes automatically, so the same wheel that creeps a few rows can be spinning freely a moment later without a settings trip. Its own review copy puts the point plainly: the MagSpeed wheel solves the friction in long documents, spreadsheets and code files, because a ratchet-only wheel makes the finger pay for every row of a long sheet.

The Logitech Lift passes partially. Its SmartWheel offers a precision mode and a speed-scroll mode, which is a real two-speed answer rather than a single fixed wheel — short moves stay controlled, longer runs move faster. It is not a free-spin wheel in the MX Master 3S sense, and its recorded specs give it no separate horizontal wheel to discuss later. The Logitech MX Vertical is ratchet-only on this check: a precision scroll wheel with a middle click, and no free-spin mode recorded. That precision suits short, deliberate movement, but a long sheet is travelled a notch at a time. The Logitech ERGO M575S sits at the same end: a standard vertical scroll wheel with a middle click, and no free-spin mode in its recorded specs. The order on check one is clear — full pass, partial pass, and two precision-only wheels.

Check two: across wide columns — the horizontal movement test

Only one mouse in this guide passes check two, and it is not close. The MX Master 3S has a separate thumb wheel, set apart from the main wheel, whose job is horizontal movement. Its review copy says the thumb wheel is what spreadsheet and timeline work feels immediately, and wide columns are exactly why: the thumb rolls sideways across the columns while the fingers stay where they were, instead of the whole hand leaving the work to find a scrollbar, drag it, and come back. For a sheet wider than it is tall in daily use — budgets with months across the top, trackers that keep adding columns — that wheel changes the work more than any sensor figure on this page.

The other three have no recorded horizontal wheel. The Lift's recorded specs describe its SmartWheel and no thumb or horizontal wheel. The MX Vertical's recorded wheel is its precision wheel with a middle click only — no horizontal wheel is recorded for it either. The M575S is the most explicit of the three: its recorded specs state that it has no horizontal tilt scrolling. None of that makes those mice unusable on a wide sheet — keyboards and scrollbars exist on every desk — but the mouse itself offers no wheel-based way across, and a reader whose pain is sideways should not buy any of the three hoping a sideways feature turns up in the box. One pass, three honest failures.

Check three: landing on the right cell — the precision test

Precision here is two things: how finely the sensor can be set, and how fast that setting can change mid-work. The MX Master 3S sets the top mark on range: a Darkfield sensor from 200 to 8,000 DPI, settable in 50 DPI increments, so the pointer speed can be tuned in small steps rather than a few coarse jumps. The same sensor tracks on glass at least 4mm thick — not a spreadsheet feature in itself, but the reason it keeps its precision where other sensors give up. Its seven buttons plus a gesture button, customisable per app in Logi Options+, let spreadsheet commands live under the hand, though the sensor figures are what pass this check.

The other three split the precision idea in instructive ways. The Lift's optical sensor runs up to 4,000 DPI and is fully adjustable — a solid, set-it-in-software answer with no instant switch recorded. The MX Vertical also runs from 400 to 4,000 DPI in 50 DPI steps, and then adds the one precision extra no other mouse here has: a cursor speed switch on top, for instant DPI changes on the mouse itself. That switch is genuinely useful in sheet work — fine settings for landing on one cell, a faster setting for crossing a big sheet, swapped in the moment rather than in an app. The M575S is the limited answer: its sensor runs up to 2,000 DPI, adjustable only in Logi Options+, with no DPI button on the mouse, and the pointing itself is done by the thumb on a ball, which carries a learning curve before small targets feel routine. Range, instant switching, or app-only adjustment — check three's winner depends on which half of precision your work stresses, though the MX Master 3S still leads on range.

The spreadsheet pick: Logitech MX Master 3S

The MX Master 3S is the spreadsheet pick because it is the only mouse here that does not ask the reader to choose which check to give up. Down a long sheet, the MagSpeed wheel covers both distances — ratchet precision for a few rows, free-spin at up to 1,000 lines per second for a few thousand, with SmartShift deciding between them as the flick changes. Across wide columns, the separate thumb wheel moves horizontally while the main wheel keeps its vertical job, which is the single most spreadsheet-specific control in this entire guide. Onto the cell, the Darkfield sensor's 200 to 8,000 DPI range, set in 50 DPI increments, gives the finest adjustment recorded here, and it keeps tracking on glass at least 4mm thick. Add seven buttons plus a gesture button, customisable per app in Logi Options+, Quiet Clicks rated 90 percent quieter than the previous generation, Bluetooth or the included Logi Bolt receiver across up to three devices with Flow, and a rechargeable battery rated at up to 70 days — and the pick is not close. It is the mouse built for the movement pattern spreadsheets demand.

Fit check: the limits are size, handedness and two honest owner notes. The sculpted body suits medium-to-large hands and is large and heavy for small hands, and it is right-hand only, with no left-hand version. Owner reliability reports split on the left click, and the soft coating wears over time — neither cancels the spreadsheet case, but both belong in the decision, and its full review records them in full. If your hand is small, read the Lift section as your starting point and accept the sideways trade with your eyes open.

The Lift is a conditional pick, and the condition is stated in its shape: a 57-degree vertical handshake angle, sized for small-to-medium hands, in the right-hand Graphite listing reviewed here. If that angle is why you are changing mice, the Lift is how you keep most of the spreadsheet story while you change. Check one passes partially — its SmartWheel offers precision and speed-scroll modes, a genuine two-speed wheel. Check three is solid — an optical sensor up to 4,000 DPI, fully adjustable, with six buttons and quiet clicks. The rest of its file travels well between sheets and machines: Bluetooth or the included Logi Bolt receiver, up to three devices with Flow, and a single AA battery rated at up to 24 months, so power simply leaves the list of things to manage.

Fit check: check two fails, and this guide will not soften it. No thumb wheel and no horizontal wheel are recorded for the Lift, so wide columns are worked by scrollbar and keys, not by a sideways wheel. Large hands will also crowd the smaller body — the MX Vertical below is the larger version of the same idea. Buy the Lift when the angle and the hand size come first; do not buy it as a spreadsheet specialist, because on the sideways check it is not one. Our full review makes its fit case in full.

The large-hand vertical conditional: Logitech MX Vertical

The MX Vertical is the same conditional logic at the larger scale: a 57-degree vertical body for medium-to-large right hands, bought because the forearm has chosen the angle. Its spreadsheet file is the most mixed in the guide. Check one is limited — a precision scroll wheel with a middle click only, with no free-spin mode recorded, so long sheets are travelled deliberately, notch by notch. Check two fails as it does for the Lift — no horizontal wheel is recorded. But check three carries a real extra: an optical sensor from 400 to 4,000 DPI in 50 DPI steps, plus a cursor speed switch on top for instant DPI changes. In sheet work that switch earns its place — one setting for landing precisely on a cell, another for crossing the sheet, changed on the mouse in the moment. Six buttons, connection by Bluetooth, the Unifying receiver or wired USB-C, and a rechargeable battery rated at up to four months complete a file that is stronger than its wheel lets it be.

And both wheel checks are limited, so a reader whose spreadsheet pain is distance, down or across, will feel this mouse's ceiling every day. Buy it when the large vertical shape is the decision and the cursor speed switch is a welcome extra; for scroll-led work, the MX Master 3S remains the answer. Our full review covers the rest.

The named skip for spreadsheet work: Logitech ERGO M575S

The ERGO M575S is named as the skip for spreadsheet work specifically, and the reasons stack across all three checks rather than resting on one. Check one: a standard vertical scroll wheel with a middle click, and recorded specs stating explicitly that there is no free-spin mode. When the mouse already on your desk is the right answer

Price the option already in front of you first. Spreadsheet work is a real use case, but not automatically a purchase: if the mouse on your desk already travels a long sheet without wearing your finger out, gives you a comfortable way across wide columns, and lands where you point it without overshooting the cell, then no mouse here buys you anything you do not already have. The cases that earn a new mouse are specific, and they map onto the three checks — a wheel that makes long sheets slow, wide sheets that mean constant scrollbar trips, or a pointer whose speed cannot be tuned finely enough for cell work, or changed fast enough between fine and fast. Name your case in those terms before shopping. A desk with no failing check points at no purchase at all.

Before you buy: the short pre-buy test

Take one real sheet — the longest, widest one in your actual work — and answer three questions against it. First: scroll from the top to the last row you use. If that trip is the pain, the free-spin check decides, and only one mouse passes it fully. Second: move from the first column to the last without touching the keyboard. If that trip is the pain, the thumb wheel decides, and only one mouse has it. Third: pick ten small, scattered cells in a row. If overshoot is the pain, sensor range and how DPI changes are made decide — in an app once, or on the mouse, in the moment. Read against the table above, those answers usually name the pick.

Spreadsheet mouse questions, answered honestly

Which mouse is best for spreadsheets and data work?

Of the four mice we review, the Logitech MX Master 3S is the spreadsheet pick, because it is the only one that passes all three checks in this guide. Down a long sheet, its MagSpeed wheel free-spins at up to 1,000 lines per second and SmartShift switches modes on its own. Across wide columns, it has a separate thumb wheel for horizontal movement — the only horizontal wheel recorded here. For landing on the right cell, its Darkfield sensor runs from 200 to 8,000 DPI, settable in 50 DPI increments. The two vertical mice are conditional picks bought for their angle, and the Logitech ERGO M575S trackball is the named skip for spreadsheet work.

Do you need a horizontal scroll wheel for spreadsheet work?

If your sheets run wide, it is the check that separates this set most cleanly. Only the Logitech MX Master 3S has a separate thumb wheel for horizontal movement in its recorded specs. The Logitech Lift and the Logitech MX Vertical record no horizontal wheel, and the Logitech ERGO M575S states explicitly that it has no horizontal tilt scrolling. A reader whose work stays inside narrow sheets may never miss it. A reader who crosses wide columns all day should treat the thumb wheel as a first-order feature, not a bonus, because no other mouse here offers a wheel-based way across.

Which of these mice scrolls fastest down a long sheet?

The Logitech MX Master 3S, and the gap is structural rather than small. Its MagSpeed electromagnetic wheel offers ratchet precision for short moves and a free-spin mode rated at up to 1,000 lines per second for long ones, with SmartShift switching between the modes automatically. The Logitech Lift is next, with a SmartWheel that offers precision and speed-scroll modes. The Logitech MX Vertical has a precision scroll wheel with a middle click only — no free-spin mode is recorded. The Logitech ERGO M575S has a standard scroll wheel with a middle click, and its recorded specs state explicitly that it has no free-spin mode.

Is a vertical mouse good for spreadsheets?

A vertical mouse can be the right spreadsheet mouse, but the angle is the reason to buy it — the spreadsheet extras are what it trades away. The Logitech Lift, for small-to-medium hands, keeps a SmartWheel with precision and speed-scroll modes, but records no thumb or horizontal wheel. The Logitech MX Vertical, for medium-to-large hands, is ratchet-only on vertical scroll and records no horizontal wheel either, though its cursor speed switch on top is genuinely useful for jumping between fine cell work and crossing a big sheet. Buy either when the 57-degree handshake shape comes first, and accept the sideways check as the cost.

Is the Logitech ERGO M575S trackball good for spreadsheets?

For spreadsheet work specifically, it is the named skip in this guide. Its recorded specs state explicitly that it has no horizontal tilt scrolling and no free-spin mode, its standard wheel covers vertical movement only, and its sensor runs up to 2,000 DPI adjustable only in Logi Options+, with no DPI button on the mouse — while thumb pointing itself carries a learning curve for precise cell work. That verdict is for this job, not for the mouse. At a desk with no room for a mouse to travel, its stationary body, quiet clicks and single AA battery rated at up to 18 months remain the right answer, and our other guides make that case.

Bottom Line

Down, across, and onto the cell — in that order

Run the three checks in the order your sheet runs them: how the wheel travels down, whether anything travels across, and how precisely the sensor lands. The spreadsheet answer is the Logitech MX Master 3S — the only mouse passing all three, with the free-spinning MagSpeed wheel, the separate thumb wheel, and the widest, finest DPI range recorded here. The angle answers: the Logitech Lift for small-to-medium hands and the Logitech MX Vertical for medium-to-large hands, each bought for its 57-degree shape with the sideways check traded away — the MX Vertical returning a cursor speed switch as its sheet extra. And the honest skip for this job: the Logitech ERGO M575S, which stays the right mouse for a desk with no room to travel. Pairing the typing half of the same desk?
