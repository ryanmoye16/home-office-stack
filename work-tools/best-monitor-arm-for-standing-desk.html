Buying Guide — Monitor Arms

Best Monitor Arm for a Standing Desk

A standing desk looks like the hardest test of a monitor arm — the screen rises and falls all day. In practice it reframes the job. A desktop-clamped arm travels with the desk, so the sit-stand movement is the desk's problem, not the arm's; what the arm has to survive is a clamp secure at full height, cables that travel the same distance, and a screen whose position relative to the desktop may never change at all. Here are the five checks a standing desk adds, then how the three arms we've reviewed split on them.

Five checks a standing desk adds

First check: the screen travels with the desk — so the clamp is the foundation. An arm clamped or bolted to the desktop moves through the desk's full height range with it. That changes what the arm is for: its job is setting the screen's height relative to the desktop once, after which the desk itself does all the sit-stand work. What gets tested every cycle is the mount: the clamp's bite on the desktop edge carries the arm and panel through every rise and stop, and any play shows up most at full height. So the check is clamp security on your desktop edge, followed by a re-check once installed: raise the desk to its full standing height, nudge the desk edge, and watch whether the screen shakes. That is your verification step, on your own setup.

Second check: how often the screen itself moves decides the mechanism. It is tempting to assume a moving desk needs a moving arm — a gas spring to match the motion. It does not follow: the desk's travel moves the arm and panel as one unit, and the arm's joints are not worked by a height change at all. The question that decides the mechanism is how often you reposition the screen relative to the desktop. If that height is set once at install and left — the common case — a set-and-forget friction arm is honestly enough, and its stiffness is a non-issue because you never feel it. A gas spring earns its place when the relative position changes often: a shared desk, or work that alternates between close and detail work. Then one-hand adjustment matters most at standing height, where repositioning a stiff friction joint means leaning across the desk to work it loose. Judge your frequency honestly, because it — not the desk's motion — is what you are paying the spring for.

Third check: the cables must travel too. Everything plugged in on the desktop travels with it — except the cables that leave. Any cable running to a wall outlet, a floor-standing PC or anything else that stays behind needs slack for the desk's full travel, or it becomes the travel limiter: the desk rises, the cable goes taut, and something gets pulled at exactly the height you work standing. The scale is not trivial: across the four standing desks we review, height travel runs from about 17 inches to about 25.7 inches — the distance a too-short cable has to find, every time you stand. The rule that solves it, and the one our standing desk setup guide is built around, is to mount the power strip under the desk so it travels too: everything on the desktop plugs into the strip, and only one cable — the strip's own — drops to the wall, with slack for the full rise.

Fourth check: panel weight against the band — both ends of it. This check it shares with every arm guide, including our general one. The ceiling is the familiar one: a panel over the arm's maximum sags or will not hold. The floor is the one buyers miss. Both gas-spring arms we review carry a minimum panel weight of 4.4 lb as well as a maximum — a panel lighter than that cannot balance the spring, can float up and refuse to stay lowered, and tension adjustment does not fix a panel below the printed floor. Weigh your panel without its stand and read the number against the whole band, not just the top. A friction arm has no spring and no such floor, a genuine argument in its favour for a light panel.

Fifth check: the arm and panel count against the desk itself. A standing desk's desktop has a stated load limit, and everything on it counts — the arm and its panel included. The desk's motors lift them on every cycle alongside everything else the surface carries. Of the four desks we review, the tightest stated limit is the SHW's, at 110 lb per its manual; the other three reviewed desks state 176 lb. On a desk at the generous end, an arm and a standard panel are a rounding error. At the tighter end — or on a desk already carrying a desktop PC or storage — the arm-plus-panel weight belongs in the running total.

The proven gas-spring pick: NB North Bayou F80

The F80 is the second gas-spring route, and its standing-desk argument is tunability. Its spring tension is adjustable, and its manual teaches the fix for the two classic symptoms directly: if the panel sinks, turn the tension toward "+"; if it floats, turn it toward "−". On a travelling desk that matters, because a screen that drifts relative to the desktop drifts at every height — a panel that slowly sinks while you work standing is one you re-lift all day, at the height where re-lifting is most awkward. An arm whose correction is printed in its own manual, dialled in against the panel's real weight, is the arm most likely to stay where the first check leaves it. It also adds a pan adjustment, and includes both C-clamp and grommet mounts, on VESA 75×75 and 100×100.

Read its band honestly, because it is the tightest of the three. The size range we publish is the manual's conservative 17 to 30 inches, and the weight band is 4.4 to 19.8 lb, the manual's 2.0 to 9.0 kg range. A panel under 17 inches is outside its printed spec, a panel over 19.8 lb is over its ceiling, and the same 4.4 lb floor applies at the bottom. Inside that window, though, this is the proven gas spring — the one whose owner track record in our review is built on holding a set position over long-term daily use, precisely the quality a travelling desk asks of an arm.

Fit check: buy it when your panel is 17 to 30 inches, weighs in comfortably inside 4.4–19.8 lb, and you want the gas spring whose tension you can tune yourself. Making the cables travel: the tray rule

Whichever arm you pick, the third check closes under the desk rather than on it. The arrangement our standing desk setup guide works through in full is a power strip carried under the desktop — the reviewed way we cover it is the clamp-on Litwaro under-desk cable tray, a tray of about 15 lb capacity that carries the strip beneath the surface. Everything on the desktop plugs into that travelling strip, and the monitor's cables drop from the arm into the same bundle. Then exactly one cable, the strip's, leaves the desk for the wall, and it alone needs slack for the full travel — about 17 to about 25.7 inches across the desks we review, plus margin.

Verify it the same way as the clamp: raise the desk slowly to full standing height and watch the cables, not the screen. Every cable should hang with slack to spare at the top — none straight, none tugging a plug partway out of the strip. Then lower it: the slack has to go somewhere at sitting height without pooling on the floor, which is what the tray is for.

When none of these three is the right buy

Sometimes a standing desk empties the field. Buy nothing on this page if:

• You run two monitors. All three arms we review are single-monitor arms; a dual setup is a different product class, and bolting on a second single spends desktop-edge room and load budget a travelling desk feels twice.
• Your panel is over 32 inches or an ultrawide. Every band here tops out at 32 inches; our ultrawide arm guide covers that end honestly.
• Your panel is over 22 lb without its stand — beyond the highest ceiling we review — or under 4.4 lb and you specifically want a gas spring, which the floor rules out.
• Your VESA pattern is 200×100 rather than 75×75 or 100×100 — none of these three arms fits it.
• The screen you want to raise is a laptop, not a monitor. That is a different fork entirely, and our laptop stand vs monitor arm guide works through which screen you own first.

We would rather hand you that list than sell you an arm that floats, sags, or strips its clamp at full height. Standing-desk arm questions, answered honestly

Does a monitor arm move with a standing desk?

Yes, if it is clamped or bolted to the desktop — and that is the point. A desktop-mounted arm rides the desk through its full height range, so the screen keeps the height relative to the desktop that you set once, sitting or standing. The desk does the sit-stand work; the arm's job is only the relative position. What needs checking is the mount: raise the desk to full standing height, nudge the desk edge, and watch whether the screen shakes. If it does, the clamp needs tightening or a sounder edge before the setup is trusted at height.

Do I need a gas-spring arm on a standing desk?

Only if you reposition the screen itself often. The sit-stand movement does not argue for a gas spring at all, because the desk moves the whole setup, arm included. If the screen's height relative to the desktop is set once and left, a friction arm like the Amazon Basics is honestly enough. A gas spring earns its place when the relative position changes often — a shared desk reset between people, or alternating between close work and detail work — because it repositions with one hand at standing height, without leaning across the desk to work a stiff joint loose.

How much cable slack does a standing desk need?

Enough for the desk's full travel, plus a margin. Across the four standing desks we review, height travel runs from about 17 inches to about 25.7 inches, and any cable that leaves the desk — to a wall outlet, a floor-standing PC, a dock on a shelf — must cover that whole rise without going taut at the top. A cable that was tidy at sitting height becomes the desk's travel limiter, or gets pulled, at full standing height. The fix most setups land on is a power strip mounted under the desk, so everything on the desktop plugs in there and only one cable to the wall needs the slack.

Can a monitor be too light for a gas-spring arm?

Yes. Both gas-spring arms we review — the HUANUO and the NB North Bayou F80 — carry a minimum panel weight of 4.4 lb as well as a maximum. A panel under that floor is too light for the spring to balance, so it can float up and refuse to stay lowered, and tension adjustment does not fix a panel below the printed minimum. Weigh your panel without its stand before choosing a gas spring. A friction arm like the Amazon Basics has no gas spring and no minimum weight recorded in our review, so the float-up failure does not apply to it.

Does a monitor arm count against a standing desk's weight limit?

Yes. The arm and the panel it carries sit on the desktop, so their combined weight counts against the desk's desktop load limit along with everything else up there — computer, books, the rest of the setup. Of the desks we review, the tightest stated limit is the SHW's, at 110 lb per its manual; the other three reviewed desks state 176 lb. On a desk at the tighter end, add the arm and panel to the running total before assuming there is headroom to spare, especially if a second screen or a desktop PC is also planned.

Bottom Line

Let the desk do the moving — pick the arm by how often the screen itself moves

Run the standing-desk checks in order: clamp security, verified at full standing height with a nudge to the desk edge; how often the screen moves relative to the desktop; cables with slack for the full travel, closed by the under-desk tray rule; panel weight against both ends of the band; and arm plus panel against the desk's own load limit. For the set-once setup most standing desks actually are, the default is the Amazon Basics arm — no gas spring, no minimum weight to fall under, and a hold that asks nothing once set. When the screen moves often, the HUANUO is the frequent-mover pick on the highest ceiling we review; when you want the proven gas spring with tension you can tune inside a 17-to-30-inch range, it is the NB North Bayou F80.
