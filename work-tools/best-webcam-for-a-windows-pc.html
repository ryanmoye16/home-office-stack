Best Webcam for a Windows PC

The short answer first: every webcam we review lists Windows in its compatibility line, so "works with Windows" is not the question — the Windows experience is decided by three quieter checks. The sign-in: only one of the four lists the infrared sensor Windows Hello needs, so facial sign-in exists on exactly one camera here. The version and the port: the Windows floors split the four from Windows 7 to Windows 10, all four end in USB-A at the computer, and the flagship's 4K needs a USB 3.0 port behind that plug. And the apps: the Teams certification lines differ, and the vendor software is optional everywhere. One camera fires every flagship feature on Windows. One is the complete package behind it. One is the proven driver-free buy. And one removes the focus question by having no focus to adjust — at the cost of the newest Windows floor here.

Check one: sign-in — Windows Hello exists on exactly one camera here

Start with the feature that only Windows can use, because it belongs to one camera and no other. The Logitech Brio 4K's recorded specifications list an infrared sensor, and that sensor provides Windows Hello facial sign-in — Windows only, by design. The recorded specifications for the Anker PowerConf C200, the Logitech C920s and the Logitech Brio 101 list no infrared sensor, stated exactly that way: facial sign-in is not part of what those three promise on paper. This is the mirror image of our Mac guide's point — on a Mac this feature never fires; on a Windows PC it is a real, daily reason to pay flagship money.

Weigh it as a yes-or-no question, not as a tiebreaker. If you sign in many times a day, or share a desk where locking the machine matters, Hello is a concrete return on the Brio 4K's price that none of the other three can match. If you type a PIN without thinking about it, Hello is worth nothing to you, and the rest of this guide — resolution, microphones, framing, the version floors below — should decide instead.

Check two: the Windows version, the port and the bandwidth behind it

The floors split the four, and the newest one can genuinely fail an older PC. The Anker PowerConf C200 and the Logitech C920s list Windows 7 or later — the widest welcome here. The Logitech Brio 4K lists Windows 8 or later, with Windows 8.1 or higher needed for resolutions above 1080p. And the Logitech Brio 101 lists Windows 10 or later: the newest Windows floor of the four, which means the simplest, cheapest-feeling camera on this page is the one an older Windows PC can actually fail. Check your own version before anything else — it is the only check here that can disqualify a camera outright rather than merely rank it lower.

The plug, by contrast, passes almost everywhere — the bandwidth is the real condition. All four cameras end in USB-A at the computer: the C200's port on the camera is USB-C but its included cable runs USB-C to USB-A, the Brio 4K's detachable cable is USB-A plug-and-play at the computer end (USB-C via a third-party adapter, not included), and the C920s and Brio 101 cables are attached USB-A at 5 ft. Most Windows desktops and laptops still offer USB-A natively, so the adapter problem that dominates the Mac version of this guide mostly evaporates here. What does not evaporate is the Brio 4K's other condition: 4K requires a USB 3.0 port and compatible software — plug it into an older USB 2.0 port and the headline resolution is not on offer.

Check three: apps and software on Windows — certification lines, optional tools, one focus record

The calling-platform lines are close but not identical, and Teams is where they separate. The Brio 101 is certified for Microsoft Teams, Zoom and Google Meet. The Brio 4K is certified for Skype for Business and Microsoft Teams, and works with Zoom, Webex and Google Meet. The C200 lists the apps it works with — Zoom, Teams, Google Meet, Skype for Business, Cisco Webex and FaceTime — and the C920s lists Skype, Google Hangouts, FaceTime, Zoom, Teams and Google Meet. Every camera here covers the mainstream Windows meeting day; the difference is whether your platform is named as a certification or as a works-with line, which is a difference in what is promised on paper, stated as exactly that.

The software layer is optional everywhere, and the focus record flips sides on Windows. Logi Tune and Logitech Camera Settings hold the Brio 4K's controls, AnkerWork selects the C200's fields of view, Logi Options+ is the Brio 101's optional control layer, and the C920s works fully without its optional camera-settings software — none of the four needs its vendor app to make a call. Focus is the other half: the Brio 4K's review records Windows reviewers praising its autofocus, while a Mac owner report describes manual focus as the workaround on macOS. That caveat is Mac-specific — a Windows buyer should not import it, nor the Mac guide's ranking, which charged the flagship for a feature only Windows can spend.

The attachable privacy shutter covers the lens only — it does not switch the camera off or mute it. The autofocus question belongs to the other platform: Windows reviewers praise it, and the manual-focus workaround in its review is a Mac owner report. Read both halves in our full review.

The proven Windows pick: Logitech C920s HD Pro

When the camera already in your Windows laptop is the right answer

Price the free option first: the camera built into your Windows laptop. For a weekly family call and the occasional meeting in decent light, it may already clear the bar, and an external camera bought for a problem that is actually light or framing will not fix either — our video call setup guide works through camera height, front light and framing in that order, on whatever camera you have. The cases that do earn an external camera are specific: a dim or backlit room, a desktop PC or closed-laptop setup with no camera in the right place, recording or cropping beyond what a meeting shows, a better microphone or frame than a laptop lid gives you — or Windows Hello facial sign-in, which on this page only the Brio 4K offers. Name your case before you shop.

Before you buy: the two-minute Windows test

Two minutes with the camera you already own tells you more than a spec sheet about your own setup. First, the version and port test: check your Windows version against the floors above, and look at your PC's ports — every pick here ends in USB-A, and the Brio 4K's 4K needs a USB 3.0 port specifically. Second, the app test: open Teams or your usual meeting app on your current camera and record a minute — lean in, sit back, speak at your normal distance — and watch and listen to it back. That playback names the problem you are actually buying against: soft focus, a frame too tight or too wide, a voice too far away, a face lost to backlight. Buy the camera whose recorded strengths match that named problem, and run the same recording the day it arrives, inside the return window.

Windows webcam questions, answered honestly

Which webcam is best for a Windows PC?

Of the four webcams we review, the Logitech Brio 4K. It is the only one whose recorded specifications list an infrared sensor, so it is the only one here that provides Windows Hello facial sign-in, and every other flagship feature fires on Windows too: 4K at 30 fps (which needs a USB 3.0 port and, above 1080p, Windows 8.1 or higher), fields of view selecting 90, 78 or 65 degrees in Logitech software, 5x digital zoom in Full HD, and RightLight 3 with HDR. It is certified for Skype for Business and Microsoft Teams, and Windows reviewers in the evidence behind its review praise its autofocus — the documented manual-focus workaround attached to it is a Mac owner report, not a Windows one.

Does Windows Hello work with these webcams?

With one of them. The Logitech Brio 4K is the only one of the four whose recorded specifications list an infrared sensor, and that sensor is what provides Windows Hello facial sign-in — a Windows-only feature. The recorded specifications for the Anker PowerConf C200, the Logitech C920s and the Logitech Brio 101 list no infrared sensor, so facial sign-in is not part of what they promise on paper. If signing in with your face is a reason you are shopping, it is a reason that points at exactly one camera on this page; if it is not, none of the other three asks you to pay for it.

Will these webcams plug into my Windows PC — and will they run on an older version of Windows?

The plug is the easy half: all four cameras end in USB-A at the computer, which most Windows desktops and laptops still offer natively — the C200's included cable is USB-C at the camera and USB-A at the computer, the Brio 4K's detachable cable is USB-A (USB-C via a third-party adapter, not included), and the C920s and Brio 101 cables are attached USB-A at 5 ft. The version is the half to check. The C200 and the C920s list Windows 7 or later, the Brio 4K lists Windows 8 or later with Windows 8.1 or higher needed above 1080p, and the Brio 101 lists Windows 10 or later — the newest floor of the four, and the one an older Windows PC can actually fail. The Brio 4K's 4K also needs a USB 3.0 port.

Does the Logitech Brio 4K's Mac autofocus problem apply on Windows?

On the record we have, no. The detailed report behind its review describes the autofocus consistently failing on macOS, with manual focus in Logitech's Camera Settings as the standing workaround — and that report is Mac-specific. The same review records Windows reviewers praising the Brio 4K's autofocus. That is stated as the scope of the evidence, not as a promise no Windows owner will ever meet a focus problem. The Windows conditions that do apply are the platform ones: 4K needs a USB 3.0 port and compatible software, and resolutions above 1080p need Windows 8.1 or higher.

Is a fixed-focus webcam a safer buy for a Windows PC?

It removes one whole failure mode and charges for it elsewhere. The Logitech Brio 101's focus is fixed, so there is no autofocus to misbehave on any operating system, and it is certified for Microsoft Teams, Zoom and Google Meet. In exchange you give up adjustment itself: the frame is a fixed, tight 58.9 degrees, the picture softens if you sit close or lean in, the single mono microphone is rated to 4 ft, there is no tripod thread, and its recorded compatibility starts at Windows 10 or later — the newest Windows floor on this page, so check an older PC against it. For a still, solo seat at a set-back monitor that trade is honest value; for anyone who moves, presents or shares the frame, an autofocus camera serves better.

Bottom Line

Run the sign-in and the version first — then let resolution choose

Run the checks in order: decide whether Windows Hello facial sign-in is worth flagship money to you, confirm your Windows version against each camera's floor and a USB 3.0 port if 4K is the point, and confirm the apps you use are the ones the camera names. The Windows answer: the Logitech Brio 4K, the only camera here whose recorded specifications list an infrared sensor, with its autofocus praised on this platform. The complete answer behind it: the Anker PowerConf C200, from Windows 7 up, with the cover that switches the camera off. The proven, driver-free answer: the Logitech C920s. And the simple answer: the Logitech Brio 101, whose fixed focus removes the question entirely, on a Windows 10-or-later machine.
