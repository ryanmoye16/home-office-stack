Buying Guide — Keyboards

Best Keyboard for Working From Home

The short answer first: a keyboard is a shape product before it is a features product. Split or straight is built into the frame — no setting changes it — so the geometry your shoulders and wrists live with decides the shortlist, and only then do key feel, backlight and receiver family separate the survivors. The four keyboards we've reviewed split exactly that way: one is the curved split board with the wrist rest built in, two are straight full-size boards that divide on whether your fingers want quiet scissor keys or a mechanical bump, and one is a compact straight board that drops the numpad so the mouse can sit closer. Here's how to tell which shape — and which size — is yours.

Run the shape, feel and receiver checks before the features check

Shape first, because it cannot be adjusted away. A split keyframe angles the two halves so your wrists do not bend sideways to meet a straight row, and a wrist rest holds your palms at the frame's height instead of letting them drop to the desk. Of the four boards below, only one changes the shape at all — and its split angle is fixed, in a single piece, with a rest that does not come off. If that one geometry does not match your shoulders, no other board on this page is a split board with different numbers; they are a different answer entirely. Size is part of the same check: three of the four are full-size boards with numpads, roughly 17 inches wide before the mouse zone, and one drops the numpad to 11.65 inches — a width difference your mouse shoulder feels on every reach.

Feel second, because you will notice it ten thousand times a day. Scissor keys are short-travel, quiet and close to the laptop typing most people already know. Low-profile mechanical switches add a bump you feel in the fingertip — and a little more sound, even in the quiet variant. Neither is better in the abstract; the honest question is whether you have ever missed mechanical feedback on a laptop board. If you haven't, you are shopping for a refinement of what you have, not a replacement for it.

Receiver third, because it decides what else on your desk still works. Logitech runs two incompatible wireless families: Unifying and Logi Bolt, and Logitech itself states Bolt is not compatible with Unifying technology. The split board below ships with Unifying; the two straight full-size boards ship with Bolt; and the compact board ships with no receiver at all — Bluetooth-only in the box, with Bolt a separate purchase and Unifying not compatible with it at all. All four also pair over Bluetooth to up to three devices, so nothing here strands a device — but a one-receiver desk only works if the keyboard and mouse are in the same family, and that fork is set at purchase.

For wrists that want the shape changed: Logitech Ergo K860

The K860 is the pick when a straight board is the problem you are solving. Instead of asking your wrists to bend sideways and flatten to meet it, the board splits the keys into two angled clusters on a curved frame and puts a three-layer pillowed rest — stain-resistant fabric over support foam over memory foam — under your palms. Front tilt legs lift the palm side at 0, -4 and -7 degrees, the negative tilt that keeps wrists from bending upward while you type. The keys themselves are deliberately ordinary: quiet Perfect Stroke scissor keys with scooped, matte caps, close to a good laptop board, so the shape is the only new thing your hands have to learn. It connects over Bluetooth or its included Unifying receiver to up to three devices, and the two included AAA batteries are rated for up to 24 months — the longest battery figure on this page by a wide margin, helped by the simplest lighting setup here: there is none.

Fit check: the geometry is fixed. The halves do not separate and the split angle does not adjust, the wrist rest does not come off, and the full footprint — 17.95 by 9.17 inches, rest included — needs real desk depth. The full-size numpad also pushes a right-handed mouse further out, which matters if your shoulder is the sensitive one. And there is no backlight, at any setting. Judge it as exactly what it is: the right buy when its one geometry matches your shoulders, your desk and your typing habits, and the wrong one when it does not.

For a familiar straight board, refined: Logitech MX Keys S

The MX Keys S is the pick when your hands already like a straight, low-profile board and you want the best version of it. The Perfect Stroke keys are spherically dished, so fingertips land centred instead of glancing off edges, and the typing is quiet scissor travel — the board reviewers and owners reach for when the room, or a call, rewards quiet. Its white backlight is the smart kind: it wakes as your hands approach, sets its brightness from the room's light, and can be tuned in Logi Options+. The metal-framed body is a solid 810 g that stays planted, the board sits at one fixed optimum angle, and it switches between up to three devices over Bluetooth or its included Logi Bolt receiver. The rechargeable battery is rated up to five months with the backlight off, and it keeps working while it charges over USB-C.

Fit check: straight means straight — no split, no tenting, no palm lift, and no wrist rest in the box, so wrists that need geometry changed get nothing here. The angle is fixed too, with no tilt feet to raise or lower it. The backlight's convenience is paid for in charging rhythm: with it on, the rating falls to up to 10 days, against five months unlit. And it is a full-size board — 430 mm wide before the mouse zone — so a small desk pays the numpad's reach cost here just as it does on the mechanical board; that cost is exactly what the MX Keys Mini below exists to remove, at the price of the numpad itself.

For fingers that miss mechanical feedback: Logitech MX Mechanical

The MX Mechanical is the pick when the MX Keys S sounds right in every way except the one your fingertips care about. It keeps the same formula — full-size low-profile frame, white smart backlight that wakes as your hands approach, Bluetooth or Logi Bolt switching across three devices — and swaps the scissor keys for low-profile mechanical switches. On this listing those are the Tactile Quiet variant: a bump you feel rather than hear, the quietest of the board's three switch options, with a sound reviewers put near a high-quality laptop keyboard's. Two flip-out feet at the rear give it the back-tilt adjustment the MX Keys S skips, and its backlit battery rating is the longest of the lit boards here — up to 15 days with the backlight on, up to 10 months with it off — recharging over USB-C.

Fit check: mechanical here does not mean customisable — the switches are not hot-swappable, the Clicky and Linear versions are different listings chosen at purchase, and there is no wired typing mode: the USB-C cable carries power only, and the board stays wireless while plugged in. It is also not the quietest board on this page: reviewers note a slightly hollow spacebar and a hint of stabiliser rattle in fast typing, so if absolute quiet outranks feel, the MX Keys S is the quieter buy. No wrist rest is included, the shape is straight like the Keys S, and on a FileVault-enabled Mac, Logitech's own note says Bluetooth may not enter your password at startup — use the included Bolt receiver for that step.

For a small desk, or a mouse shoulder that aches: Logitech MX Keys Mini

The MX Keys Mini is the pick when the board itself is not the problem — the width is. It keeps the MX Keys S's formula where your fingers can tell: the same spherically dished Perfect Stroke scissor keys, the same white smart backlight that wakes as your hands approach, the same three-device Easy-Switch switching and Logi Options+ Flow support. What it cuts is the numpad and the separate control cluster, bringing the board down to 11.65 inches wide. That cut is an ergonomic change before it is a tidiness one: with no numpad pushing the mouse out to the side, a right-handed mouse sits roughly five inches closer in, inside the shoulder's comfort arc, instead of at the end of a reach repeated all day. At 506.4 g the metal-topped body stays planted while typing and is light enough to double as a travel board, and the rechargeable battery matches the MX Keys S's ratings — up to 10 days with the backlight on, up to 5 months with it off.

Fit check: the cut is permanent — there is no numpad and no separate pad in the box, the function row is a single shared media-first row, and the typing angle is fixed with no tilt feet and no wrist rest. The omission buyers miss is the receiver: this listing is Bluetooth-only in the box, a Logi Bolt receiver is a separate purchase, and Unifying receivers do not work with it at all — and owner reports on connectivity are the most divided theme on the listing, with a minority describing Bluetooth wake lag that a Bolt receiver fixed. Judge it as the compact MX board it is: the right buy when desk width and mouse reach decide, the wrong one when numbers, a receiver in the box, or an adjustable angle are requirements.

When none of these four is the right buy

The four boards above cover the split shape, the refined straight board, the mechanical feel and the compact width — between them, most working-from-home desks. What they do not cover is just as specific: if you want a split board whose halves separate and tent independently, that is enthusiast split-board territory, and the K860's fixed single-piece geometry deliberately is not it; if you need a wired typing connection, none of these offers one — the rechargeable boards' USB-C cables carry power only, and the K860 runs on batteries; and if you enter numbers all day on a small desk, the Mini's missing numpad is a daily tax, not a saving — that desk wants the full-size MX Keys S and a mouse placed deliberately, not a compact board.

And if your current keyboard actually fits — wrists neutral, shoulders relaxed, no reach that aches by afternoon — the right buy may be no buy, or a smaller one: our ergonomic desk setup guide treats the keyboard as one part of the screen-and-surface layers, and the layer that is actually wrong is often the screen height or the chair, not the keys. Persistent wrist, hand or shoulder pain, in particular, is not a gear specification — it deserves a clinician before it deserves a checkout.

Keyboard questions, answered honestly

Which keyboard should I buy if I want an ergonomic split shape?

Of the four keyboards we review, only the Logitech Ergo K860 changes the shape: a curved, split keyframe in a single piece, an integrated three-layer pillowed wrist rest, and front palm-lift legs at 0, -4 and -7 degrees. The split angle is fixed and the halves do not separate, so it fits shoulders that match that one geometry — it cannot be widened, narrowed or tented the way a two-piece enthusiast board can. The MX Keys S, MX Mechanical and MX Keys Mini are all straight boards with no split and no wrist rest in the box. And a shape change is not a pain treatment: persistent wrist, hand or shoulder pain deserves a clinician before it deserves a checkout.

Is a mechanical keyboard better than a scissor keyboard for typing all day?

It is a feel preference, not an upgrade. The MX Mechanical's low-profile Tactile Quiet switches give a bump you feel rather than hear, and reviewers put its sound near a high-quality laptop keyboard's — but the switches are not hot-swappable, so the feel you buy is the feel you keep. The K860, MX Keys S and MX Keys Mini use Logitech's Perfect Stroke scissor keys: quieter, shorter-travel, and closer to the laptop typing most people already know. If you have never missed mechanical feedback, scissor keys are not a compromise; if you have, the MX Mechanical is the only one of the four that answers it.

Which of these keyboards has a backlight, and what does it cost in battery life?

The MX Keys S, MX Mechanical and MX Keys Mini all have a white smart backlight that wakes as your hands approach and adjusts to ambient light; the Ergo K860 has no backlight at all. The battery trade is explicit in Logitech's ratings: the MX Keys S and the MX Keys Mini each run up to 10 days with the backlight on against up to 5 months with it off, and the MX Mechanical up to 15 days on against up to 10 months off. The unlit K860 sidesteps the trade entirely — its two AAA batteries are rated for up to 24 months.

Can these keyboards share a receiver with my Logitech mouse?

Only within the same receiver family — and one of these boards ships with no receiver at all. The Ergo K860 ships with a Logitech Unifying receiver; the MX Keys S and MX Mechanical ship with Logi Bolt, and Logitech states Bolt is not compatible with Unifying technology. The MX Keys Mini is Bluetooth-only in the box: a Logi Bolt receiver is a separate purchase for it, and Unifying receivers do not work with it at all. So a Bolt mouse (the Lift or the MX Master 3S in our reviews) can share a receiver family with the two full-size MX boards — and with the Mini once a Bolt receiver is added — but not with the K860, and a Unifying mouse (our MX Vertical review) pairs the other way round. All four keyboards also pair over Bluetooth to up to three devices, which is the receiver-free route on any desk.

Which of these keyboards fits a small desk?

The Logitech MX Keys Mini — it is the compact board on this page: 11.65 inches wide with no numpad, against roughly 17 inches for the other three, so a right-handed mouse sits about five inches closer in, inside the shoulder's comfort arc. It keeps the MX Keys S's dished scissor keys and smart backlight; what it gives up is the numpad itself, a separate control cluster, and any receiver in the box. Depth barely splits the straight boards — the Mini, MX Keys S and MX Mechanical are all about 5.19 inches deep — while the Ergo K860 is 9.17 inches deep because its pillowed wrist rest is built in and does not come off, so if depth is your binding constraint, measure the K860's full footprint, rest included, before anything else on this page.

Bottom Line

Check shape, feel and receiver — then pick

Name the shape your wrists need, the size your desk can spare, the feel your fingers actually miss, and the receiver family your desk already runs, and buy the keyboard that matches all four: a changed, split shape with the rest built in points to the Ergo K860; a familiar straight board at its quietest and most refined points to the MX Keys S; mechanical feedback on that same straight frame points to the MX Mechanical; and a compact board that pulls the mouse in close — the MX Keys S's keys and backlight, minus the numpad and any in-box receiver — points to the MX Keys Mini.
