Buying Guide — Monitor Arms

Best Monitor Arm for a Curved Monitor

A curved monitor does not need a special category of arm — a curved panel between 27 and 32 inches sits inside the size range of the arms we review. What the curve changes is the three checks underneath the size. The weight: curved panels tend to run heavy for their diagonal, and the only figure that counts is the panel's weight without its stand. The tilt hold: a deeper curve puts more mass in front of the mounting plate, so the tilt joint works harder than the same weight in a flat panel would ask. And the VESA fit: curved backs often hide their mounting holes in a recessed well. Three arms we review split on all three checks — one on headroom, one on a tilt tension you can tune, and one on being set once and left alone.

Check one: the weight — the panel without its stand, judged with headroom

Start with the figure most listings bury: the panel's weight without its stand, taken from the maker's own specification page. That is the load the arm actually carries — the stand stays in its box — and it is rarely the number in large type, which is usually the weight with the stand attached or the shipping weight of everything in the carton. An arm judged against the wrong figure can look comfortable on paper and arrive already at its limit. Every ceiling in this guide is read against that stand-free figure, and the gas-spring floors are too: a panel under 4.4 pounds is below the range the two gas-spring arms here are rated to balance.

Curved panels make this check sharper, because they skew heavy for their size. The curved glass, the deeper back shell that houses it, and the bracing that keeps a bent panel rigid all add mass that a flat panel of the same diagonal does not carry. So a 32-inch curved panel should never be assumed to weigh what a 32-inch flat panel weighs, and a 27-inch curved panel can sit closer to an arm's ceiling than its size suggests. Read the three ceilings here as a ladder: 22 pounds for the HUANUO Single Monitor Arm, 20 pounds for the Amazon Basics Single Monitor Arm, and 19.8 pounds for the NB North Bayou F80. The gaps look small — two pounds, then a fifth of a pound — but a heavy curved panel can land between two rungs of that ladder, passing one arm and ruling out the next.

Judge the result with headroom, not at the line. A panel a pound or two under a ceiling leaves room for the ordinary untidiness of real setups: a cable bundle pulling down at the back, a light bar clipped to the top edge later, a spec figure that turns out to have been rounded in the maker's favour. A panel sitting exactly on a ceiling has none of that room, and a panel over it is not a judgement call at all — it is outside the arm. Size draws the same kind of line, by the way: 27- to 30-inch curved panels are inside all three arms' published ranges, while a 32-inch curved panel is inside two of them and past the 30-inch manual cap we publish for the F80. Check two: the tilt hold — a curve moves the mass forward of the plate

An arm does not hold a panel's weight in the abstract; it holds that weight wherever the panel puts it. On a flat monitor, the mass sits close to the plane of the VESA plate at the back. Curve the panel and its edges — and a share of its mass — swing forward of that plane, so the tilt joint at the plate is no longer just supporting the panel, it is resisting a forward lean built into the panel's shape. The joint's job is leverage, and a curved panel hands it a longer lever than a flat panel of identical weight. This is why two panels of the same stand-free weight can behave differently on the same arm.

The depth of the curve sets how much harder the joint works. Curve radii are written as numbers like 1800R, 1500R and 1000R, and the smaller number is the deeper curve. A gentle 1800R panel sits only modestly forward of its plate; a deep 1000R panel wraps noticeably toward the viewer, and correspondingly more of its mass hangs in front of the mount. Nothing about the radius changes the arm's size or weight gates — check one still decides those — but a deep curve is a reason to prefer an arm whose hold you can adjust over one whose hold you simply accept, and to be suspicious of a setup that passes check one with nothing to spare.

A panel that slowly nods down is a tension report first, and a verdict on the arm only later. Owner feedback on gas-spring arms returns to the same sequence: the screen is set level, hours or days pass, and the panel has drifted a few degrees toward the desk. On a curved panel that drift is the forward-shifted mass winning slowly against a joint set for a lighter or flatter load. The F80's manual answers it directly, in the maker's own directions: if the panel sinks, turn the tension adjustment toward the "+" sign; if the panel floats upward, turn it toward the "−" sign — small turns, re-set the angle, and let it sit before judging. Gas-spring arms in general are built around that adjustability: the spring is meant to be tuned to the panel it carries. Treat a nod as a setting to correct once, properly, before treating it as a reason to replace anything.

Check three: the VESA fit — a pattern match against a curved back

On pattern, all three arms say the same thing: VESA 75x75 and 100x100, and nothing else. That covers a great many curved panels in the 27- to 32-inch range, whose mounting patterns are usually one of those two squares. It also draws a clean outside line. A panel drilled for VESA 200x100 — a pattern that shows up on some larger and heavier screens — fits none of the arms in this guide, and no adjustment, adapter claim or optimistic reading changes a hole pattern. Read your panel's pattern off its specification page alongside its weight, because either figure on its own can pass while the other fails the panel completely.

The pattern is only half of the fit, because a curved back is not a flat mounting surface. Curved panels commonly sink their VESA holes into a recessed well moulded into the back shell, so the plate that sits flat against a flat monitor may have to reach into a pocket, clear the well's rim, or bridge a surface that curves away from the screws. Whether a given panel's well is deep enough to need spacers or standoffs — and which ones — is the panel maker's specification question, answered in that panel's manual, not a property of the arm. We state it as a check rather than a claim: put the panel's manual next to the arm's plate dimensions before ordering, and treat a well you cannot account for as an unanswered question.

The mount at the desk end, by contrast, is settled in this guide: all three arms include both a C-clamp and a grommet mount, so the choice between clamping to the desk edge and bolting through a desk hole is yours on every arm here, and no curved case changes it.

The curved pick: HUANUO Single Monitor Arm

The HUANUO is the curved pick because check one is where curved panels are most often lost, and it holds the most headroom there. Its gas spring is rated from 4.4 to 22 pounds for panels from 13 to 32 inches — the widest size range and the highest ceiling of the three arms we review — so the full 27- to 32-inch curved band is inside its size specification, and a curved panel that runs heavy for its diagonal still lands with margin under the ceiling instead of against it. That margin is the room check two spends: a deep curve asks the tilt joint to hold forward-shifted mass, and an arm working well inside its rating holds an angle more calmly than one at its edge.

On check three it matches the group: VESA 75x75 and 100x100 only, both mounts in the box, and height, tilt, swivel and rotation. Its honest costs are in its own review: owners call the assembly instructions unclear, and it carries the 4.4-pound gas-spring floor. Neither is a curved-panel problem in particular — for a panel inside the gates, this arm asks the fewest apologies of the three.

Fit check: buy it when your curved panel weighs, without its stand, somewhere in the middle-to-upper part of the ranges here — the territory where the other two ceilings start to press — or when a deep curve makes you want headroom in reserve for the tilt joint. The tunable-tension conditional: NB North Bayou F80

The F80 is the conditional pick for the reader whose central worry is check two: a curved panel that will not keep the angle it was left at. It is a gas-spring arm with tension the owner tunes to the panel, and its manual gives the adjustment as a plain rule — a panel that sinks gets a turn toward the "+" sign, a panel that floats gets a turn toward the "−" sign. For a deep-curved panel whose mass sits well forward of the plate, that documented, reversible adjustment is the most direct answer in this guide to the slow nod, because it treats the nod as the balance problem it usually is. Its movement set is also the fullest here: height, tilt, swivel, rotation and pan, with both mounts included.

The conditions are the strictest in the guide, and they are stated in its review as plainly as here. We publish the F80's manual size range, 17 to 30 inches, so a 32-inch curved panel is outside this arm on the range we stand behind, whatever a catalogue field elsewhere says. Its weight band is 4.4 to 19.8 pounds — the lowest ceiling of the three — so the heavy-for-its-size curved panel that motivates check one can rule the F80 out before its tension advantage ever comes into play. Owners also call its assembly instructions minimal, and its review recommends a helper for the final attachment.

Fit check: buy it when your curved panel is 30 inches or under, weighs comfortably inside 4.4 to 19.8 pounds without its stand, and you expect to fine-tune tilt hold — or already know a drifting panel is your problem. The set-and-forget conditional: Amazon Basics Single Monitor Arm

The Amazon Basics arm is the conditional pick for a curved panel that will be positioned once and then left where it was put. It is rated for monitors up to 32 inches and up to 20 pounds, accepts VESA 75 and 100 patterns, and includes both C-clamp and grommet mounts, with height, tilt, swivel and rotation adjustment and an integrated cable channel. That middle ceiling — a pound and four-fifths under the HUANUO's, a fifth of a pound over the F80's — passes a typical 27- to 32-inch curved panel judged with headroom, and stops, as it should, at the genuinely heavy ones.

Its condition is its adjustment, and owner reports are consistent about it: the mechanisms feel stiff, particularly at first, and the arm's pattern in owner feedback is set-and-forget — set the screen height and angle deliberately, then stop moving it. On a curved panel that stiffness cuts both ways, and this guide will say so plainly: a stiff tilt joint, once set, is not prone to wandering, but it also offers no spring tension to tune if a deep curve's forward mass starts a slow nod — the correction is effort at the joint, not a dial.

Fit check: buy it when your curved panel is under 20 pounds without its stand, inside 32 inches and on a 75 or 100 pattern — and when your honest habit is to set a monitor once and work. When none of these arms is the right answer

Three curved cases close this guide rather than bending it. The first is size past the band: a 34-inch curved ultrawide is outside every size range published here — 32 inches on two arms, 30 on the F80's manual range — and it belongs to a different set of numbers, which our monitor arm guide for ultrawide monitors works through on weight and headroom against the same three arms. The second is weight past the highest ceiling: a panel over 22 pounds without its stand exceeds the HUANUO, the Amazon Basics and the F80 alike, and no reading of check one rescues it — that panel needs an arm from a heavier class than anything we currently review. The third is the pattern: a panel that needs VESA 200x100 fits none of these arms, because all three stop at 75x75 and 100x100. A panel under 4.4 pounds is likewise below both gas-spring arms' floor. In each case the honest move is to stop at the gate rather than argue with it.

Before you buy: the short pre-buy test

Three lookups and one look at the back of the panel, all before anything is ordered. First: open the panel maker's specification page and write down the weight without the stand — not the headline figure — and hold it against 22, 20 and 19.8 pounds, keeping the arm whose ceiling leaves real headroom. Second: read the same page for the VESA pattern; 75x75 or 100x100 keeps all three arms in play, and 200x100 closes the guide. Third: note the size against the ranges — 32 inches closes the F80 on its manual cap, 34 closes everything here. Then turn the panel, or its manual's diagram, over: find the mounting holes, judge whether they sit in a recessed well, and settle the spacer or standoff question from the panel maker's own documentation. Buy against the check that actually narrowed your list.

Curved monitor arm questions, answered honestly

Which monitor arm is best for a curved monitor?

Of the three arms we review, the HUANUO Single Monitor Arm is the curved pick for most 27- to 32-inch panels, because it leaves the most room under the ceiling. It is a gas-spring arm rated for panels from 13 to 32 inches and from 4.4 to 22 pounds, with VESA 75x75 and 100x100 mounting and both C-clamp and grommet mounts included. Curved panels tend to weigh more than flat panels of the same size, so that 22-pound ceiling is the margin that matters. Judge it on your panel's weight without its stand, from the maker's specification page, and leave headroom under the ceiling rather than planning to sit exactly on it.

How do I know if my curved monitor is too heavy for an arm?

Use the panel's weight without its stand, from the manufacturer's specification page — not the shipping weight, and not the weight with the stand attached, which is the figure most listings lead with. Then compare it with each arm's ceiling, leaving headroom: 22 pounds for the HUANUO Single Monitor Arm, 20 pounds for the Amazon Basics Single Monitor Arm, and 19.8 pounds for the NB North Bayou F80. A panel at or over a ceiling is outside that arm, not a close call. The two gas-spring arms also have a floor: a panel under 4.4 pounds is below the range the HUANUO and the F80 are rated to balance.

Why does my curved monitor slowly tilt down on its arm?

Start with tension, not with a replacement. A curved panel carries more of its mass forward of the VESA plate than a flat panel of the same weight, and the deeper the curve, the further forward that mass sits, so the tilt joint works harder to hold the angle you set. A panel that slowly nods down over a day is usually reporting that the joint needs more hold, not that the arm is wrong. On the NB North Bayou F80 the fix is in the manual: if the panel sinks, turn the tension adjustment toward the plus sign; if it floats up, turn it toward the minus sign. Re-set the angle after adjusting, and judge the arm only once the tension matches the panel.

Does the curve radius — 1000R, 1500R or 1800R — change which arm I need?

It changes the tilt question more than the size question. The radius describes how deep the curve is: a 1000R panel curves more deeply than a 1500R panel, which curves more deeply than a 1800R panel, so at the same diagonal and weight the deeper panel puts more of its mass in front of the mounting plate. That is an argument for headroom and for an arm whose tilt hold you can adjust, rather than for a different size class. It does not change the gates: the panel still has to sit inside the arm's size range, under its weight ceiling, and on a VESA pattern the arm accepts.

Will these arms fit the VESA mount on a curved monitor?

All three arms accept VESA 75x75 and 100x100 patterns only, so a curved panel drilled for one of those two patterns passes the pattern check and a panel that needs VESA 200x100 fits none of them. The curved-back complication is separate: the mounting holes on curved panels often sit in a recessed well in the back shell, so whether the plate sits flush, and whether spacers or standoffs are needed to reach it, is a question for the panel maker's own specification and manual. Check the pattern and the well before ordering; neither can be settled from the arm's box.

Can any of these arms hold a 34-inch curved ultrawide?

Not on the size specification published for them. The HUANUO Single Monitor Arm and the Amazon Basics Single Monitor Arm are rated to 32 inches, and the NB North Bayou F80's manual range runs to 30 inches, so a 34-inch panel is outside all three before weight or VESA are even considered. That case is a different guide on this site: our ultrawide monitor arm guide works through the same three arms against 34-inch panels on weight and headroom, and reaches its own honest limits there. Staying inside this guide means a curved panel of 32 inches or under — 30 inches or under for the F80.

Bottom Line

The weight, the tilt, the pattern — in that order

Run the checks in the order a curved panel runs them: the stand-free weight with headroom, the tilt hold against mass shifted forward of the plate, and the VESA pattern against a curved back. The curved answer is the HUANUO Single Monitor Arm — the highest ceiling here at 22 pounds across the full 13- to 32-inch range, so a heavy curved panel keeps its margin. The tunable answer is the NB North Bayou F80, whose manual tension adjustment answers the slow nod directly, inside its 17- to 30-inch manual range and its 19.8-pound ceiling. The set-once answer is the Amazon Basics Single Monitor Arm, rated to 32 inches and 20 pounds, with the stiffer adjustment owners describe, for a panel positioned once and left alone. The panel past these gates — a 34-inch curved ultrawide, a stand-free weight over 22 pounds, a 200x100 pattern — is not a failure of shopping; it is a different search, and it starts from the gates above — or browse all our monitor arm reviews.
