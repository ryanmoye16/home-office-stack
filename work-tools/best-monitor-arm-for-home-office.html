Buying Guide — Monitor Arms

Best Monitor Arm for Home Office

The short answer first: for a standard home office monitor — 24 to 27 inches, comfortably under 20 lb without its stand — every arm we've reviewed passes the fit checks, so the real choice is how the screen moves. Two of the three are gas springs that adjust with one hand; the third is a set-and-forget arm that costs its stiffness in daily adjustment and repays it in simplicity. But the weight check comes first, because it is the one buyers get wrong: the number that counts is your panel's weight without its factory stand. Here are the three checks in order, then how the three arms split.

Check one: your panel's weight, without its stand

Every monitor arm failure story starts with the wrong weight. The figure printed on your monitor's box and in most listings is the weight with the factory stand attached — and the stand comes off the moment the arm goes on. The figure an arm is rated against is the panel alone, and the only honest source for it is the manufacturer's specification page for your exact model. A panel that reads comfortably under an arm's limit with its stand on can sit much closer to that limit than its owner expects once the stand is off. Weigh the right number, then leave headroom under the ceiling rather than buying at the line — headroom is what absorbs a deeper curved panel's tilt torque and a heavier replacement screen later.

Gas springs add a second number: a floor. Both gas-spring arms we review are tensioned for panels from 4.4 lb upward. A very light panel below that floor can refuse to stay lowered, floating up against a spring balanced for more weight. So the weight check is a band, not a ceiling: your panel needs to sit inside it, with room at the top end. The three reviewed bands are 4.4 to 22 lb (HUANUO), 4.4 to 19.8 lb (NB F80, per its manual's 2.0 to 9.0 kg range) and up to 20 lb with no spring floor (Amazon Basics). If you'd rather not do this arithmetic by hand, our monitor arm checker takes your screen size, panel weight and VESA pattern and returns a strict-spec verdict on each of the three — comfortable fit, tight headroom, or outside spec — with no buying link on a fit that fails.

Size is a separate limit, and it is not negotiable either. All three arms state a maximum monitor size — 32 inches on the HUANUO and the Amazon Basics, and 17 to 30 inches in the F80's manual — because a wider panel loads the arm differently from a heavier one. A 34-inch ultrawide can weigh less than a 27-inch panel and still sit outside every arm here on size alone; that case has its own page, our ultrawide monitor arm guide, and it ends honestly for most 34-inch panels.

Check two: how often the screen actually moves

This is the check that splits the three arms, and owner feedback is unusually consistent about it. Gas-spring owners describe adjusting the screen during the day — up for focused document work, down and angled for a video call — because height moves with one hand once the spring tension is set. Standard-arm owners describe the opposite pattern: set the screen once, at install, and live with it. Neither pattern is wrong. What is wrong is buying against your own pattern: a gas spring you never move buys you nothing over the simpler arm, and a stiff arm you fight through three adjustments a day taxes every working day you own it.

Be honest about which worker you are. If your day is one posture — one chair height, one task, screen at eye level from nine to five — the set-and-forget pattern is not a compromise, it is the fit. If you alternate between deep-focus upright work and leaning-back calls, or share the desk with someone of a different height, the screen will move daily whether the arm makes it easy or not, and the gas spring is the honest buy. The third check is quicker: all three arms fit VESA 75x75 and 100x100 and include both a C-clamp and a grommet mount in the box, so mount and pattern only rule you out if your panel carries VESA 200x100, which none of the three accepts.

A heavier 27-inch panel, the kind with a metal back and a built-in hub, can sit close enough to a 20 lb ceiling to make the arithmetic uncomfortable; on the HUANUO the same panel sits mid-band, with room left for the tilt loads a deep curve adds. The gas spring adjusts height smoothly with one hand once tension is set, per owner reports, and owners describe actually using that adjustment through the day rather than admiring it at install.

The proven gas-spring pick: NB North Bayou F80

The F80 is the arm in this guide with the long owner track record, and its feedback centres on the same point as the HUANUO's: a gas spring that takes little force to move and holds its position once set, on an arm owners consistently describe as not feeling like a budget product in daily use. It adds one adjustment the other two do not list — pan, the fine sideways angle — alongside height, tilt, swivel and rotation, which makes it the easiest of the three to square to a desk that does not sit perfectly straight to the chair. Its hold is tunable rather than fixed: the manual rates the spring for 2.0 to 9.0 kg and gives the tension screw a direction for each symptom — toward plus for a panel that sinks, toward minus for one that floats — so the arm can be dialled to the panel it actually carries.

Fit check: the F80 also carries the tightest band of the three — 4.4 to 19.8 lb, and 17 to 30 inches on its manual's figures — so the weight check matters most here. A panel above 19.8 lb is outside the rating outright, and a panel under the 4.4 lb floor sits below what the spring is tensioned to balance. Assembly instructions are minimal, per owners, who recommend a helper for the final monitor attachment, and the included plastic cable clips feel flimsy to many — Velcro ties are the common upgrade. Inside its band, though, this is the gas-spring pick for a standard monitor; our full review has the tension procedure and the rest.

The set-and-forget pick: Amazon Basics

The Amazon Basics arm is the straightforward pick for the worker check two describes: one posture, screen set at install, no daily adjustment to make easy. It covers monitors up to 32 inches and 20 lb with the four standard adjustments — height, tilt, swivel and rotation — an integrated cable channel, and both mounts in the box. Owners most often praise exactly what the design promises: a straightforward install with clear instructions and all hardware included, and a steady hold with no wobble at normal typing speeds once mounted. Mounting any of these arms also returns the factory stand's footprint to the desk — the pole sits at the desk edge — and owners setting up small desks cite that reclaimed surface among the changes they notice most.

Fit check: the trade is in the adjustment itself. The mechanisms are slightly stiff out of the box, per owners — they loosen after a few days of use, but this arm will never move like a gas spring, and if your screen changes height daily that stiffness is a daily cost, not a break-in period. Build quality is functional rather than premium, in line with owner expectations for the line. And the 20 lb ceiling needs the same measuring discipline as the springs' bands: panel weight without the stand, from the manufacturer's page, with headroom left over. When none of these three is the right buy

A panel above 22 lb, or wider than 32 inches, sits outside everything we review — the ultrawide case is worked through in our ultrawide monitor arm guide, and heavier professional displays need an arm from the heavy-duty class, shopped on the same discipline: panel weight without the stand, headroom under the ceiling, VESA pattern confirmed. A VESA 200x100 panel rules out all three arms here regardless of weight. And two screens rule out all three as well: every arm we review is a single-monitor arm, and we will not stretch one into a dual recommendation — a dual setup needs a dual arm or two singles, judged on the same checks per screen.

The other honest exit is no arm at all. If your screen is a laptop, the choice is a different one — our laptop stand vs monitor arm guide runs that fork, and our laptop stands for posture guide covers the stand side. If your monitor's own stand already puts the screen at eye level on a deep desk and never needs to move, an arm buys you the reclaimed footprint and nothing else — a real benefit on a small desk, a cosmetic one on a large one. Run your exact panel through the monitor arm checker before ordering any of the three; it applies these same specs strictly, and it will tell you when the honest answer is none of them.

Home office monitor arm questions, answered honestly

Which monitor arm is best for a typical home office monitor?

For the standard home office panel — 24 to 27 inches, comfortably under 20 lb without its stand — all three arms we review pass the weight and size checks, so the choice is how the screen moves. The HUANUO and the NB North Bayou F80 are gas springs: height adjusts with one hand once tension is set, which suits anyone who repositions during the day. The Amazon Basics arm is the set-and-forget pick: straightforward install, steady hold at normal typing speeds, stiffer adjustment. Pick by movement first, then confirm the weight — not the other way round.

How much weight headroom should I leave under an arm's rating?

Judge capacity on your panel's weight without its factory stand, taken from the manufacturer's specification page — not the weight with the stand attached, and not a guess from the screen size. Leave headroom rather than buying at the line: capacity is best judged with room to spare, because a panel sitting at an arm's ceiling leaves nothing for the tilt torque of a deep curve or a heavier replacement panel later. The three reviewed ceilings are 22 lb (HUANUO), 20 lb (Amazon Basics) and 19.8 lb (NB F80).

Do gas-spring arms have a minimum weight as well as a maximum?

Yes — and it is the limit buyers miss. Both gas springs we review are tensioned for panels from 4.4 lb up: the HUANUO's stated range is 4.4 to 22 lb and the F80's manual rates its spring at 2.0 to 9.0 kg, the same 4.4 to 19.8 lb. A very light panel below that floor can sit above what the spring is balanced to hold down, so it may not stay lowered the way a mid-weight panel does. The F80's tension is adjustable — the manual turns the screw toward plus for a panel that sinks and toward minus for one that floats — which widens the usable band but does not remove the floor.

Gas-spring or standard arm — which should I buy?

Buy the mechanism that matches how often the screen moves. Owner feedback splits cleanly on this: gas-spring owners describe adjusting during the day — up for focused document work, down and angled for a video call — because height moves with one hand. Standard-arm owners describe setting the screen once and living with it, which is a perfectly good outcome if your work is one posture all day; the Amazon Basics arm's adjustment is slightly stiff out of the box and loosens after a few days of use, per owners. Paying for a gas spring you never move buys nothing; saving on a standard arm you fight daily costs more than money.

What VESA pattern do these arms fit, and what if mine is different?

All three reviewed arms accept VESA 75x75mm and 100x100mm, which covers the large majority of home office monitors. None of the three accepts VESA 200x100, so a panel with that pattern — some larger and older displays — is outside all three regardless of its weight, and needs an arm specced for it or a VESA adapter plate rated for the panel's weight. Check the pattern on the back of your monitor or in its manual before ordering; it is printed there, and guessing it is the most common avoidable return in this category.

Can one of these arms hold a 34-inch ultrawide?

Not on spec. All three reviewed arms state a maximum monitor size of 32 inches or below — the F80's manual range is 17 to 30 inches — so a 34-inch ultrawide sits outside their printed size range even when its weight without the stand would fit under a ceiling. Weight is not the only limit: a wide, deep-curved panel also loads the tilt joint differently from a flat 27-inch. We ran the full numbers in our ultrawide monitor arm guide, and our monitor arm checker will give a strict-spec verdict on your exact panel before you order.

Do I need a grommet hole in my desk to use one of these arms?

No. All three reviewed arms include both mounting options in the box — a C-clamp that grips the desk edge and a grommet mount that bolts through a hole — so a desk without a grommet hole clamps, and a desk with one can bolt for the tidier install. The clamp is the usual choice: it is reversible, moves when the desk does, and leaves no hole behind. Whichever you use, the mount goes at the desk edge, which is also what returns the factory stand's footprint to you as usable desk surface.

Bottom Line

Weigh the panel, then pick by movement

Run the checks in order: panel weight without its stand, inside the band with headroom; then how often the screen moves. Heavier standard panel, or the most headroom you can get: the HUANUO, 22 lb ceiling on a gas spring. A standard 17 to 30 inch panel and a long owner track record with tunable tension: the NB North Bayou F80. One posture, set it at install and forget it: the Amazon Basics arm. Above 22 lb, beyond 32 inches, on VESA 200x100, or running two screens, buy nothing here — the checker and the ultrawide guide above will point you at the right class instead.
