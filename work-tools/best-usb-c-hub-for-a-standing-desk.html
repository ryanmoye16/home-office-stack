Buying Guide — USB-C Hubs

Best USB-C Hub for a Standing Desk

A fixed desk forgives a hub placed anywhere. A standing desk does not: the surface rises and falls, and every cable that leaves it is pulled through that travel twice a day. So a standing-desk hub is judged by where it lives and what crosses the moving joint — not just by its port list. Our two reviewed Anker hubs split that job cleanly: one was built for the desk, the other can honestly run it on Wi-Fi. Here are the three checks standing runs.

Three checks decide it, and standing runs all of them

1. What travels with the desk. The hub belongs ON or UNDER the moving desktop — never on the floor or the wall side. Everything plugged into it then rises and falls with the surface, and only ONE slack run should cross the moving joint: the charger's cable to the wall, sized to stay slack at full height. That is the same one-run rule our standing-desk cable guides solve for the power strip, applied to the hub. It comes with a placement consequence buyers miss: both hubs in this guide hang off a short attached cable, so the laptop also lives on the desktop, near the hub. A laptop parked on a fixed side shelf breaks the one-cable routine — the hub cannot follow the desk and stay plugged into a laptop that does not move.

2. The one-cable sit/stand routine. Done right, the routine is this: sit down, plug one USB-C cable into the laptop, and everything is already on the desk — the display, Ethernet on the 555, your drives and card slots, and charging at up to 85W pass-through, fed by a 100W charger plugged into the hub. Nothing gets re-plugged when the desk changes height, because nothing but the charger's own wall run crosses the joint. One condition decides whether you actually get that 85W, and it is a recorded owner note for both hubs: pass-through charging depends on the charger you feed the hub. A weak adapter is the first suspect when charging disappoints or a display flickers.

3. Fixed ports vs moving ports. Things wired to the hub travel with it — which is the point — but two of those things have a fixed end somewhere else. A monitor mounted to the desk or an arm travels with the surface, yet its cable run still needs checking at full height. And the Ethernet drop is the honest complication on the 555: the Ethernet cable comes from the wall, so on that hub it is a second moving run, and it needs its own slack at full height. Say it plainly: if the desk has no slack-managed Ethernet drop, the 555's Ethernet advantage shrinks, and Wi-Fi plus the 7-in-1's simpler single run is the honest alternative. Heat closes the check: the 555 runs warm fully loaded — Anker notes its shell can reach about 122°F with every port busy — so mount it ventilated on a hard surface, not buried, exactly as our cable management guide for standing desks treats everything else that lives under a moving desktop.

The standing-desk pick: Anker 555 USB-C Hub (8-in-1)

The 555 leads a standing-desk guide because it is the only reviewed hub that completes the one-cable routine with the desk's hardest jobs included. Mounted on or under the desktop, it carries Gigabit Ethernet, a single HDMI running 4K at 60Hz from a DP 1.4 host (4K at 30Hz from a DP 1.2 host), two USB-A ports and a USB-C data port at 10Gbps, SD and microSD slots, and up to 85W of pass-through charging from a 100W PD input — the hub reserves 15W for itself. Owner feedback behind our full review is consistent with that spec sheet: the Ethernet connection is the reliability win on calls, the 4K HDMI output is stable for office and media work, and 10Gbps transfers keep external SSDs at full speed. Its aluminum body and braided cable are the larger build here — 4.76 × 2.17 × 0.6 inches and 4.5 oz — which is a travel tax and a desk virtue: planted on a moving surface, weight is stability.

Standing notes, honestly: two conditions ride along. Second, heat: the shell can reach about 122°F fully loaded, so keep it on a hard surface with air around it — on the desktop or in a ventilated under-desk mount, never buried under papers or a desk mat that the desk's movement can shift onto it.

What it carries on the desktop is still a complete small desk: two USB-A 3.0 ports, a USB-C data port, SD and microSD slots that owners can use simultaneously, one HDMI at 4K@30Hz (1080p runs at 60Hz), data at 5Gbps, and the same honest charging figures as its sibling — 100W PD input, up to 85W pass-through, the hub reserving 15W. At 4.5 × 2.1 × 0.6 inches and 2.8 oz it is the smaller body to mount, and owner feedback reports its connections holding under load, with warmth described as moderate rather than a problem.

Fit check: buy it for a standing desk only with its ceilings in view. No Ethernet, ever — the day wired networking matters, this hub cannot grow it. Video comes only from the HDMI port; the USB-C ports carry no display signal. Data runs at 5Gbps, half the 555's ceiling. And the attached cable is short — the most common owner complaint in its catalog copy — which limits placement and keeps the laptop close, exactly the constraint the first check warns about. If none of that touches your desk, the smaller hub honestly runs it; the moment one point does, you are describing the 555.

When neither hub is the right buy

Two requirements close this guide, and both point past hubs entirely:

• You need two external monitors — each hub has exactly one HDMI output, and on a Mac neither hub does dual external monitors in any configuration. That is Thunderbolt dock territory, with independent video outputs per screen.
• You run a 16-inch MacBook Pro and want full-speed one-cable charging — its full-speed charging tops the 85W both hubs pass through, so it charges more slowly through either hub under sustained heavy load. Give it its own power port, or step up to a powered Thunderbolt dock.

We would rather route you to the right category than sell you a hub whose ceiling a moving desk hits in the first week. A standing desk also cannot rescue a hub choice made for a fixed desk: if your current hub already runs your desk on one cable and survives the full-height test below, standing by itself does not create a hub problem.

Before you buy: the full-height slack test

Run this test with the hub and cables you already have — or with a borrowed hub — before any order. Raise the desk to its full height and check every run's slack, one at a time. The charger's cable to the wall: slack at the top, never taut, never lifting. The monitor run: if the monitor is mounted to the desk or an arm it travels with the surface, but confirm its cable is not the thing quietly crossing to a fixed point. The Ethernet drop, if you are planning on the 555: treat it as the second moving run it is, and confirm the route from the wall can stay loose at full height — if it cannot, that result is the 7-in-1's case made in person. Then check the two placements: the laptop sitting on the desktop within the hub's short attached cable, and the hub itself on a hard, ventilated spot that stays clear when the desk moves. Run the desk back down and up once more and watch every cable through the whole travel. A setup that passes at full height passes everywhere; a setup that only works at sitting height is not a standing setup yet.

Standing desk hub questions, answered honestly

What is the best USB-C hub for a standing desk?

Of the two hubs we review, the Anker 555 USB-C Hub (8-in-1) is the standing-desk pick. It is the only one with Gigabit Ethernet, the only one whose USB-A and USB-C data ports run at 10Gbps, and the only one that drives a 4K display at 60Hz from a DP 1.4 host — and mounted on or under the moving desktop, it turns sitting down into a one-cable routine: display, wired network, drives and up to 85W of pass-through charging are already on the desk. The honest conditions are slack and heat: its Ethernet cable from the wall is a second moving run that needs slack for the desk's full height, and its shell can reach about 122°F fully loaded, so it belongs on a hard, ventilated surface, not buried. If your desk has no slack-managed Ethernet drop and your Wi-Fi is solid, the smaller Anker 7-in-1 in this guide is the honest alternative.

Where should a hub sit on a standing desk?

On or under the moving desktop — never on the floor or the wall side. Everything plugged into the hub travels with the desk, so the only run that should cross the moving joint is the charger's cable to the wall, sized to stay slack at full height. Both hubs in this guide hang off a short attached cable, so the laptop lives on the desktop near the hub; a laptop parked on a fixed side shelf breaks the routine, because the hub's cable cannot follow the desk and the laptop at the same time. Mount the hub where its ports stay reachable and its body stays ventilated.

Does a standing desk need a second slack run for Ethernet?

On the Anker 555, yes — honestly. The hub travels with the desk, but the Ethernet cable plugged into it comes from the wall, so it is a second moving run alongside the charger's cable, and it needs the same treatment: enough slack to stay loose at full height, routed so it never goes taut or pulls at either port. If the desk has no slack-managed Ethernet drop, the 555's Ethernet advantage shrinks, and Wi-Fi plus the 7-in-1's simpler single run is the honest alternative. The 7-in-1 has no Ethernet port at all, so on that hub the question never arises.

Is 85W pass-through charging enough at a standing desk?

For most laptops, yes. Both hubs take up to 100W of PD input, reserve 15W for the hub, and deliver up to 85W to the laptop — full speed for a MacBook Air and most ultrabooks, and adequate for a 14-inch MacBook Pro while every other port stays active. Pass-through depends on the charger you feed the hub: a 100W PD charger is what delivers the full 85W, and owner reports for both hubs agree a weaker adapter is the first suspect when charging disappoints or a display flickers. The exception is a 16-inch MacBook Pro under sustained heavy load, whose full-speed charging tops 85W — give it its own power port and let the hub run data and video.

Can either hub run two monitors on a standing desk?

No. Each hub has exactly one HDMI output, so each drives exactly one external display — and on a Mac, neither hub does dual external monitors in any configuration, because macOS does not extend a desktop across these hubs. A monitor mounted to the desk or an arm travels with the surface and still needs its own slack at full height, but a second monitor is a different product class: a Thunderbolt dock with independent video outputs. Buy a hub in this guide for one good screen plus ports and power, not for a second screen.

How warm does the Anker 555 get, and where should it be mounted?

Warm enough to plan around. Anker notes the 555's shell can reach about 122°F when every port is busy with a PD adapter attached, and owner feedback treats that warmth under full load as normal rather than a failure sign. At a standing desk that means mounting it on a hard surface with air around it — on the desktop or in a ventilated under-desk mount — and never buried under papers, a desk mat or fabric, where the desk's own movement can also shift the covering onto it. The 7-in-1's warmth is reported as moderate by comparison, but the same clear-surface rule applies to both.

Bottom Line

Put the hub on the desk that moves — then count the runs that leave it

For a standing desk with a slack-managed Ethernet drop, the Anker 555 is the pick: Gigabit Ethernet, 10Gbps data and one 4K screen at 60Hz on a single cable, with honest 85W charging — at the stated cost of a second moving run and a ventilated mount. On a Wi-Fi desk, the Anker 7-in-1 honestly runs the simpler version of the same routine. Building the fixed version of this desk instead? See our USB-C hub guide for a desk setup.
