Buying Guide — USB-C Hubs

Best USB-C Hub for Travel

The short answer first: pack the smaller hub. Of the two Anker hubs we review, the 7-in-1 is the one built for a bag — its own review verdict names it the travel pick in Anker's lineup — and the larger 555 earns a place in luggage only when a trip turns into a desk transplant. The decision comes down to three checks a desk never runs: what the hub weighs in a bag you carry all day, which of its ports a trip actually uses, and whether the charger you packed can feed it. Here is the honest split.

Three checks a desk never runs

First check: packed size and weight. At a desk, a hub's dimensions are trivia; in a bag carried through an airport, a hotel and a client site, they are the product. The Anker 7-in-1 measures 4.5 × 2.1 × 0.6 inches and weighs 2.8 oz — smaller than a deck of cards, in its review's phrase. The Anker 555 measures 4.76 × 2.17 × 0.6 inches and weighs 4.5 oz: a slightly larger footprint, and a little over half again the weight. Neither is heavy, and the honest reading is about what the extra ounces buy rather than the ounces themselves — the 555's size carries the Ethernet port, faster data and the stronger video output described below. If none of those three is part of your trip, the smaller hub wins this check outright, every day it is carried.

Second check: the ports a trip actually uses. Away from your own desk, the ports that earn their place are the unglamorous ones. An HDMI output for the screen you meet — a meeting-room display, a hotel television — and here the travel hub's ceiling is honest: the 7-in-1 runs 4K at 30Hz, which owners find fine for documents, presentations and media, and 1080p at a smooth 60Hz. Both SD and microSD slots, usable at the same time — the detail photographers single out when copying a camera card and a drone card in one session. Two USB-A ports for whatever receiver, drive or borrowed peripheral the day produces, plus a USB-C data port. The 555 carries all of that too, and adds the three capabilities its size pays for: Gigabit Ethernet, 10Gbps data ports, and 4K at 60Hz from a DP 1.4 host. So this check is a question about your itinerary, not about the hubs: screens, cards and borrowed peripherals point to the 7-in-1; a wired network port, a large drive offload or a borrowed 4K desk point to the 555.

Third check: the charger you feed it. On the road, the hub is only half the charging system — the other half is the adapter in your bag. Both hubs take up to 100W of PD input, reserve 15W for the hub itself, and pass up to 85W to the laptop: full speed for a MacBook Air and most ultrabooks while every other port stays active. But that figure is delivered by a 100W PD charger feeding the hub, and Anker's own guidance and owner reports agree on the failure mode: a weak adapter is the first suspect when a display flickers or charging disappoints. Pack the charger your laptop shipped with, or a PD adapter of at least that class, and either hub charges as rated. Pack a phone-class adapter and no hub can rescue the arrangement. Two smaller travel notes ride along with this check. The 7-in-1's attached cable is short — the most common owner complaint in its feedback — which keeps the hub close to the laptop; at a hotel desk that is a nuisance, in a bag it is nothing. And a working hub runs warm: Anker notes the 555's shell can reach about 122°F with every port busy, and the 7-in-1's feedback reports moderate warmth under load with the same advice — do not cover the hub while it works.

The limits both hubs share, stated before anything is sold: each has exactly one HDMI port, so neither adds a second external display — in a hotel room any more than at a desk — and video only ever comes out of that HDMI port, because the USB-C ports on both hubs carry data, not a display signal. And neither hub's recorded port list includes a 3.5mm audio jack, so wired headphones belong in the laptop's own jack. Those boundaries are the category's, not a defect in either pick below — but they are the two most common reasons a travel hub disappoints, so they belong at the top of the guide.

The travel pick: Anker 7-in-1 USB-C Hub

The 7-in-1 is the hub to pack because its port list reads like a trip. Two USB-A ports for legacy devices, a USB-C data port, HDMI for an external display, and both SD and microSD slots — which owners can use at the same time, a detail photographers single out when copying a camera card and a drone card in one session. Having all of that in a device smaller than a deck of cards is the appeal, and owner feedback on stability is consistent: roundup summaries of owner reviews report connections holding under load, with no disconnects or transfer failures even on large transfers, and reviewers describe the build as compact and sturdy enough to live in a laptop bag. This is the hub that covers the realistic travel list — the presentation, the card import, the borrowed peripheral, the charge overnight — without asking for space in the bag.

Charging, honestly: the PD port takes up to 100W in, reserves 15W for the hub, and delivers up to 85W to the laptop — full speed for a MacBook Air and most ultrabooks while every other port stays active, as long as the charger feeding it is strong enough; Anker's guidance and owner reports agree a weak adapter is the first suspect when a display flickers. Heat comes up in its feedback as moderate rather than a problem: reviewers report the body staying manageable under extended use with a display and drives attached, and Anker itself says warmth during charging or fast transfers is normal — just don't cover the hub under load, which on a hotel nightstand means leaving it in the open rather than under the itinerary printout.

Fit check: know the three limits before it goes in the bag, because they are the three checks read in reverse. There is no Ethernet port, so a room or office where Wi-Fi cannot be trusted is beyond it. Its data ports run at 5Gbps, and its card slots read at UHS-I speeds — dependable for casual photo and clip offloads, not the tool for dumping a huge shoot in a hurry, where a dedicated UHS-II reader is faster. And its HDMI output caps 4K at 30Hz, where 1080p runs at 60Hz. The smaller print matters too: the attached cable is short, the most common owner complaint, and there is no 3.5mm audio jack. The extended-stay pick: Anker 555 USB-C Hub (8-in-1)

The 555 earns its larger place in the bag when a trip stops being a trip and becomes a desk transplant — a week in a rented apartment, a long client engagement, a conference room that is yours until Friday. It is the only hub here with Gigabit Ethernet, and owner feedback singles that connection out as its reliability win: a wired line stays stable on video calls when hotel or venue Wi-Fi does not, and many hotels still offer a wired port the 7-in-1 cannot use at all. Its data ports run at 10Gbps, so the large offload at the end of a shooting or recording day is not bottlenecked by the hub. And its single HDMI output runs a 4K screen at 60Hz from a DP 1.4 host, so a borrowed 4K monitor at the far desk behaves like your own. Around those three, the rest matches the travel pick: two USB-A ports, a USB-C data port, both card slots, and the same 85W pass-through charging.

Charging, honestly: identical figures to the 7-in-1 — up to 100W in, 15W reserved, up to 85W to the laptop — with the same condition that a 100W PD charger is what delivers the full figure. Anker also states plainly that the USB-C data port carries no video signal, so there is no guessing about which port drives a display: HDMI does. One warmth note is this hub's own: Anker notes the shell can reach about 122°F when every port is busy with a PD adapter attached, and owners report it as normal — keep it on a hard surface with air around it, not on a bed.

Fit check: three conditions, all decidable before packing. The trip actually contains one of its three advantages — a wired connection you expect to use, large files moving on an external SSD, or a 4K screen fed by a DP 1.4 laptop (a DP 1.2 host gets 4K at 30Hz from this same hub, which dissolves the display advantage). Your display plan is one external screen, because one HDMI port is the whole video story. And you accept the size honestly: 4.76 × 2.17 × 0.6 inches at 4.5 oz is still small — it is the 7-in-1's margin that is smaller, and a hub carried daily and used for none of its three advantages is weight without a return. Packing the hub, honestly

Three habits decide whether a travel hub works on the road, and none of them is in the hub. First, match the charger to the laptop, not to the hub: the 85W pass-through figure is a ceiling set by what you feed the hub, so the adapter that shipped with your laptop — or a PD adapter of the same class — is part of the hub purchase whether it is in the bag or not. Second, expect the short attached cable: both hubs keep themselves close to the laptop by design, which is tidiness in a bag and a placement constraint on a deep hotel desk, where the laptop may need to sit nearer the screen than you planned. Third, give a working hub air: warmth under full load is documented for both, and a hub buried under clothes in a packed bag while it charges is the one arrangement both hubs' feedback warns against.

The checks also run in the other direction. If your travel laptop lives on Wi-Fi, presents at 1080p, and offloads a card a day, the smaller hub is not the compromise choice — it is the correct one, and the larger hub's advantages are features you would carry without using. The right travel hub is the one whose limits your itinerary never touches.

When neither hub is the right buy

Step up to different hardware, or solve a different problem, if any of these is you:

• You need two external monitors on one connection — each hub here has exactly one HDMI port and supplies one display output, in a hotel room just as at a desk. A two-monitor setup needs a Thunderbolt or USB4 dock with separate video outputs, or a DisplayLink dock or adapter that drives the extra display through its own driver.
• You travel with a gaming or workstation laptop with a higher-wattage adapter and want full-speed charging through the same cable — 85W of pass-through is a ceiling both hubs share, so plan on the laptop's own adapter for power and treat the hub as a data and video device.
• Your trips end with a huge shoot to offload in a hurry — the 7-in-1's card slots read at UHS-I speeds, and a dedicated UHS-II reader is the faster tool; the 555's 10Gbps ports speed an external SSD, not the card slots.
• You need wired audio through the hub — neither hub's recorded port list includes a 3.5mm jack. Wired headphones belong in the laptop's own jack, or a separate audio adapter is part of the packing list.

We would rather route you to the right category than sell you a hub that leaves a second monitor dark in a hotel room. Docks are a category we are expanding our reviews into; until one is reviewed here, buy on your laptop's published port specifications and the dock's per-output figures, not on a listing photograph showing several glowing screens.

Travel hub questions, answered honestly

Which of these two hubs should I pack?

For most trips, the Anker 7-in-1: it is the smaller, lighter hub (4.5 × 2.1 × 0.6 inches, 2.8 oz), it keeps both SD and microSD slots, two USB-A ports, an HDMI output and 85W pass-through charging, and its own review verdict names it the travel pick in Anker's lineup. Pack the Anker 555 instead when the trip is a desk transplant — you expect to use a wired Ethernet connection, move large files on an external SSD at 10Gbps, or drive a 4K screen at 60Hz from a DP 1.4 laptop — and accept its larger, heavier body (4.76 × 2.17 × 0.6 inches, 4.5 oz).

Will the 7-in-1's HDMI output work on a hotel TV or a meeting-room screen?

Yes. The 7-in-1 drives one display over its HDMI port, at 4K at 30Hz or 1080p at 60Hz — 30Hz is fine for documents, presentations and media, and 1080p at 60Hz is smooth. Video only ever comes out of the HDMI port: the hub's USB-C ports carry data, not a display signal, so a USB-C-to-HDMI cable plugged into them shows nothing.

Can I charge my laptop through the hub while travelling?

Both hubs take up to 100W of input, reserve 15W for the hub itself, and pass up to 85W to the laptop — full speed for a MacBook Air or a typical ultrabook while every other port stays active. What you actually get depends on the charger feeding the hub: a 100W PD charger delivers the full 85W, and owner reports and Anker's own guidance agree a weak adapter is the first suspect when a display flickers. A laptop that ships with a higher-wattage adapter charges more slowly through either hub.

Can the 7-in-1 read an SD card and a microSD card at the same time?

Yes — owners single this out, using both slots together, for example copying a camera card and a drone card in one session. Transfers run at UHS-I speeds: dependable for casual photo and clip offloads, but not the tool for dumping a huge shoot in a hurry, where a dedicated UHS-II reader is faster.

Does either hub give me a second monitor or wired internet on the road?

Wired internet, only from one of them: the Anker 555 has Gigabit Ethernet built in; the 7-in-1 has no Ethernet port at all. A second monitor, from neither: each hub has exactly one HDMI port and supplies exactly one external display. A two-monitor setup needs a Thunderbolt or USB4 dock with separate video outputs, or a DisplayLink dock or adapter.

Bottom Line

Pack the hub whose limits your trip never touches

For most trips that is the Anker 7-in-1: the smaller, lighter body, both card slots at once, an HDMI output for the screen you meet, and honest 85W pass-through charging. When the trip becomes a desk transplant — wired Ethernet, 10Gbps offloads, a 4K screen at 60Hz — pack the Anker 555 and accept its size as the price of those three.
