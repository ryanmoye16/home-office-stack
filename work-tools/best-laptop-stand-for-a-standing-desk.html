Buying Guide — Laptop Stands

Best Laptop Stand for a Standing Desk

A laptop stand on an ordinary desk has one job: lift the screen. On a standing desk the job changes, because the desk itself does most of the lifting — raise the desk and everything on it rises too. What is left for the stand is narrower: close the gap between the desk surface and your eye level without disturbing the keyboard height, change height as fast as your day does, and stay steady at height. This guide runs the four stands we have reviewed through those three checks — and of the four, only one reaches true standing height on a fixed desk.

Three checks decide a laptop stand for a standing desk

First check: how much lift is actually needed above the desk surface. Seated on a standing desk and standing at one are different jobs. A stand does not need to lift a laptop from the floor to your eyes; it only needs to close the gap between the desk surface and eye level. Seated, that gap is the familiar one, and a seated-range stand covers it. When you stand at a height-adjustable desk, the desk rises to meet your hands and the laptop rises with it, so the remaining gap above the surface is again a small one — the desk, not the stand, supplies the standing lift. The hard case is a fixed desk, where the surface never moves and the standing gap is the full distance from a sitting-height surface to standing eyes; of the four stands reviewed here, only one has the range for it. One condition guards all of it: the stand must not force the desk lower or higher than the keyboard position allows — the desk is set for your hands first, and the stand's range has to fit what is left.

Second check: adjustment speed, because a standing desk moves and the stand gets reset with it. A sit-stand day is a rhythm of small resets, and a stand that takes two hands and a minute of loosening will quietly stop being adjusted at all. The four stands here change height four different ways: a front slider, a continuous knob that trades speed for range, stiff two-hand hinges, and preset steps. On a standing desk, where the correct height changes every time the desk does, it decides whether the stand tracks your day or fights it.

Third check: stability at height — and the external keyboard it assumes. Every inch of lift is leverage. A stand that feels planted at seated height can wobble or bounce near the top of its range, and the failure mode is usually the same: typing on the raised laptop itself. Hands on a raised built-in keyboard push on the exact structure the lift made tall, so movement that would be invisible at desk height becomes sway at standing height. The honest reading of every stand in this guide is that a raised laptop is a screen, not a keyboard. An external keyboard and mouse stay on the desk at the height the desk was set for, your hands never touch the tall structure, and the stand only has to hold a screen still. If your plan is to type on the laptop at standing height, no stand in this guide makes that plan steady; that is a limit of the shape, not of one model. The standing pick: Lifelong Upryze

The Upryze is the standing pick for one reason that outweighs everything else in this guide: range. It lifts laptops from 10 to 17 inches up to 20 inches, in an aluminum and steel frame. On a fixed desk, that range is the difference between a screen at standing eye level and one stranded well below it. It is the only one of the four stands we have reviewed that reaches true standing height without the desk's help — if your desk does not move, this section is the answer. On a height-adjustable desk the same range covers any gap the desk leaves, though there it is more stand than the job strictly needs.

On the adjustment check it is the slow one, and the slowness is structural. Height is continuous, set by a knob: loosen, reposition, re-tighten. That buys any height in the range rather than a set of steps. It costs the rhythm a sit-stand day wants — repositioning takes effort, more so with the laptop in place — so owners tend to set a height and work at it rather than chase every posture change.

Owner feedback and roundups both note more wobble at or near maximum height if you type on the laptop itself — at 20 inches of lift, keystrokes are leverage. With an external keyboard and mouse on the desk, the same feedback describes the frame as sturdy and the laptop held securely once the knob is tightened — treat the external keyboard as practically mandatory at standing height. The stand partially folds — legs fold, but not completely flat — and its open platform leaves the laptop's underside exposed to airflow.

The seated-range pick on an adjustable desk: Lamicall

The Lamicall's case rests entirely on the first check's distinction: this is a seated-range stand, adjusting from 5.7 to 8.1 inches in height with a 45 to 180 degree angle range, for laptops from 10 to 17.3 inches. On a fixed desk it does not reach standing height, and nothing here claims otherwise. On a height-adjustable desk, the arithmetic flips. The desk rises to your standing hand height and carries the stand with it, so the stand only has to close the remaining gap between the raised surface and your eyes — a gap in the same few-inches family as the seated one. For that job its range is a sensible one, and the Lamicall becomes the pick for adjustable-desk owners who do not need the Upryze's fixed-desk range.

What it brings to that job is a planted base. At 2.1 pounds, with a large weighted aluminum base, owner feedback and roundups single out how settled it feels compared with lighter folding stands — the base stays put, and ventilation holes in the tray keep air moving under the machine. It folds flat, too, though it is heavier and bulkier folded than a minimalist travel stand.

Its two honest costs land on this guide's second and third checks. Adjustment is stiff by design: the hinges hold their set angle because they are tight, and changing height or angle is a two-hand job that takes real force. And at its taller settings, typing on the laptop itself can feel bouncy, especially with heavier machines. Neither cost is fatal here: set the Lamicall deliberately when the desk moves, type on an external keyboard, and its range and base do the rest.

Fit check: buy it when your standing desk is height-adjustable, the gap above the surface is within its 5.7 to 8.1 inch range, and a weighted, planted base matters more than one-hand adjustment. Our full review records the details behind this section.

The fastest adjustment: Rain Design iLevel 2

The iLevel 2 wins the second check outright, and on a standing desk that check is worth more than it first appears. Its front slider adjusts height and tilt together, with the laptop still on the stand: slide the knob on the front, and the platform resets in seconds. On a height-adjustable desk — where the right stand height changes each time the desk does — the slider is the difference between a stand that tracks the desk all day and one set in the morning and quietly wrong by afternoon. Owner feedback centres on exactly this: it moves smoothly, stays where it is set, and gets changed because changing it is easy.

Its range is a seated range: it raises a laptop toward seated eye level, and on top of a height-adjustable desk that is enough, because the desk supplies the standing lift. On a fixed desk it does not reach standing height at all — if the desk cannot rise, the Upryze section above is your section. It also does not fold: a fixed, desk-only stand in solid aluminum, with an open design that conducts heat away from the laptop and gives cables somewhere to hide.

One more limit belongs in a standing-desk guide: there is no VESA mount, so the iLevel 2 will not integrate with a monitor-arm setup later. The trade is coherent: it gives up range and folding to be the one stand here that adjusts at the speed a sit-stand day moves.

For standing use, this is the plainly-labelled skip. As a seated riser it has a real case — owner feedback praises its steady hold at normal typing speeds. But run it through this guide's three checks and the standing case collapses. On lift, 6 inches at its top step is a seated gap, not a standing one: on a fixed desk it is nowhere near standing eye level, and on an adjustable desk it adds the least lift of the four.

On adjustment, its preset steps are the coarsest mechanism here: your ideal height can fall between two levels, and changing takes two hands. On stability, owner feedback is consistent that it feels least planted at its highest setting — exactly the setting a standing-desk shopper would live on. When a monitor arm or an external monitor is the better answer

A laptop stand, however tall, still lifts a laptop: a screen of laptop size, at laptop distance, with its keyboard attached underneath it. Some standing-desk setups make that the wrong tool. The first is screen size and distance: if the work needs a larger display, or one that can be pulled closer and pushed back independently of everything else on the desk, that is a separate monitor's job. The second is repositioning: arms swing and re-aim a screen in one motion, all day, in a way no stand here matches. The third is the setup where the laptop is not really the screen at all — closed on the desk, driving an external display — where the screen question belongs entirely to the monitor and its mount.

If that fork sounds like your desk, start with fit: our monitor arm checker tests whether an arm fits your monitor and desk; our general monitor arm guide covers how the arms we have reviewed split. The deeper comparison is the whole subject of our laptop stand vs monitor arm guide. A stand remains the better answer in the common case: the laptop is the only screen, an external keyboard is already on the desk, and one of the four ranges above covers the gap. The arm is the better answer when the problem is the screen itself.

The no-stand case, and the conflict it hides

There is one more honest case: the desk raised so high that the laptop screen, sitting flat on the surface, is already at eye level — no stand needed. It works, but only by spending the one currency a laptop cannot split. A laptop's screen and keyboard are fixed to each other, so a single desk height cannot be right for both at once: set the desk for the screen and the built-in keyboard rises to a height your hands did not choose; set it for comfortable typing and the screen drops below eye level by the same amount. That is bearable for a short standing spell of reading or a call. As an all-day arrangement it asks your hands to work at screen height — the compromise every stand here exists to avoid, because the stand's real product is not lift but the separation of screen height from keyboard height.

Standing-desk laptop stand questions, answered honestly

Do I need a laptop stand on a standing desk at all?

Sometimes, and the desk decides which times. A height-adjustable desk does most of the lifting: raise the desk and the laptop on it rises too. What the desk cannot do is raise the screen and the keyboard by different amounts — on a laptop they are one piece of hardware. If the desk height that puts the screen at eye level leaves the built-in keyboard too high to type on comfortably, a stand closes the remaining gap — the desk is set for your hands, and the stand lifts the screen the last few inches. Worked that way, an external keyboard and mouse are part of the setup, not an accessory. And if your desk, chair and height already line the screen up while you sit, a stand may add nothing at all.

Which laptop stand reaches standing height on a fixed desk?

Of the four stands reviewed here, only the Lifelong Upryze. It lifts laptops from 10 to 17 inches up to 20 inches — enough to bring a laptop screen toward standing eye level on a fixed desk. The other three are seated-range stands: the Lamicall adjusts from 5.7 to 8.1 inches, the Rain Design iLevel 2 works in a seated range, and the Amazon Basics offers six preset levels from 3.5 to 6 inches. None of those three closes a standing gap on its own: on a fixed desk, standing height is the Upryze's job or a separate monitor's job.

Can a seated-range stand work on a height-adjustable standing desk?

Yes — that is the case where seated-range stands make the most sense. On a height-adjustable desk the desk itself supplies the standing lift, so the stand only has to close the smaller gap between the desk surface and your eye level, and that gap is broadly similar sitting and standing. The Lamicall's 5.7 to 8.1 inches, and the iLevel 2's seated range, can be enough on top of a desk that rises with them. The condition is that the stand must not force the desk to a height that breaks your keyboard position: set the desk for the external keyboard first, then check whether the stand's range covers what is left.

Do I need an external keyboard and mouse with a stand on a standing desk?

At any height where the stand is doing real work, yes. A stand lifts the screen and the built-in keyboard together, so the higher the screen sits, the higher your hands are asked to work — and at height, typing on the raised laptop is also where stability gives out: the Upryze can wobble at maximum height if you type on the laptop itself, and the Lamicall feels bouncy at its taller settings. An external keyboard and mouse stay on the desk, at the height the desk was set for, while the screen rises independently — that separation is the entire reason a stand helps on a standing desk.

When is a monitor arm or an external monitor the better answer?

When the screen you need at standing height is bigger than a laptop screen, when you reposition the screen often, or when no stand's range fits your desk and your height together. A monitor arm lifts a separate monitor, so screen height and keyboard height were never linked, and the laptop can stay closed or sit beside the monitor as a second screen. A stand remains the simpler answer when the laptop is the only screen you own and one of the four ranges in this guide covers your gap. The arm is the better answer when the gap itself is the wrong shape for a stand.

Bottom Line

Let the desk do the lifting it can do — then close the gap that is left

Start by naming your desk. On a fixed desk, standing height has exactly one answer here: the Lifelong Upryze, with up to 20 inches of lift, a slow continuous knob, and an external keyboard treated as mandatory at height. On a height-adjustable desk the choice opens up: the Lamicall brings a weighted, planted base and 5.7 to 8.1 inches of lift, while the Rain Design iLevel 2 brings the front slider for desks that move all day. The Amazon Basics is the honest skip for standing on either desk — a seated riser whose top step is its least planted.
