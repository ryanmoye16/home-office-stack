Buying Guide — Monitor Light Bars

Best Monitor Light Bar for Dual Monitors

The short answer first: a dual-monitor setup is not a wider version of the single-screen problem — it is a different one. Two 24-inch monitors span about 43 to 44 inches, and two 27-inch monitors span about 48 to 49 inches and typically sit angled inward. Both monitor light bars we have reviewed are rated for a single monitor up to 32 inches, so one bar stretched across a dual setup is being asked to cover far more than its stated fit — and centred over the pair, it sits over the bezel seam in the middle, lighting the centre best and the outer edges least. The even-coverage answer is one bar per monitor, and that answer doubles everything else: two controls to live with and two USB ports to find. So this guide runs three dual checks: coverage width, control per bar, and power per bar.

Check one: coverage width — the setup is wider than either bar's fit

Start with the arithmetic, because on a dual setup the arithmetic decides most of the answer before taste gets a vote. A monitor's headline size is its diagonal; what a light bar has to cover is the width of the desk in front of the screens. As our standing desk coverage works through, a 24-inch panel is roughly 21.5 to 22 inches across including its bezel, so a pair spans about 43 to 44 inches side by side. A 27-inch panel is roughly 24 to 24.5 inches across, so a pair spans about 48 to 49 inches — and because few desks let two 27-inch screens sit flat in a straight line, dual 27-inch setups typically sit angled inward, each screen turned slightly toward you. Either way, the working surface in front of the screens is a 43-to-49-inch zone. That is the width the light has to serve.

Now set that width against what either bar is actually rated to do. The Quntis Monitor Light Bar PRO+ fits monitors 15 to 32 inches. The Quntis Monitor Light Bar (Standard) fits monitors up to 32 inches. Those are single-monitor fits, stated by the manufacturer and recorded in our reviews — not suggestions a determined buyer can stretch by eleven to seventeen inches. A single bar centred over a dual setup is therefore outside its stated fit across most of the desk it is trying to light. That does not make the light useless; it makes the coverage uneven by construction, strongest where the bar is and weakest furthest from it.

Owner feedback already shows what that gap looks like on a smaller mismatch. Owners of 34-inch and larger ultrawide monitors report uneven coverage at the edges from both bars — on one single screen, only just past the 32-inch fit. A dual setup is wider than that ultrawide, so the edge problem owners report there is the mild version of what a 43-to-49-inch span asks of one bar. The centre lights; the wide edges do not, evenly.

Centring the one bar creates a second, geometric problem: it sits over the seam. Two monitors meet in the middle, bezel to bezel, exactly where a centred bar would mount. Instead of sitting over the middle of a screen, the bar straddles the join, its light falling most directly on the inner edges of both screens and the desk in front of them. If your work is centred there, that pool may be all you ask for. If your work spreads across both screens, the middle-best distribution works against you all day.

So check one resolves into a plain rule: one bar per monitor is the even-coverage route. A bar on each screen sits inside its stated fit, aims its asymmetric optic at the desk in front of its own screen, and covers its half of the desk the way it covers a single-monitor desk — the job owners praise both bars for, glare-free light on the desk and keyboard rather than on the screen. Angled dual 27-inch setups make the same point: a bar mounted on each screen follows that screen's angle, while a single centred bar cannot follow both. Everything after this check is about what living with two bars costs.

Check two: control per bar — two bars means two controls

Doubling the bars doubles the controls, judged bar by bar, not as a system. There is no shared controller here and no single dial that runs a pair. Each bar is adjusted on its own terms: its own control, its own settings, its own ambient sensor. That belongs in the decision up front, because the control difference between these bars, modest on one screen, is paid twice on a dual setup.

On the PRO+, control per bar means a wireless rotary remote per bar on the desk. Each PRO+ comes with its own wireless rotary remote, so a dual setup has two remotes. Be precise about what that buys: a remote adjusts its own bar, from the desk, without reaching up to a monitor. It does not adjust the other bar, and this guide makes no claim that one remote controls two bars, because that is not a sourced feature. What two remotes preserve is the PRO+'s core convenience, per screen — each change made from where your hands already rest.

The remote's running cost also doubles, and it is small but real. Each PRO+ remote runs on a coin-cell battery, included, which will need replacing over time — two remotes mean two batteries on two schedules. Auto-dimming softens it in practice: each bar follows its own ambient sensor, so routine brightness changes mostly happen without either remote being touched.

On the Standard, control per bar means reaching up to each bar's own touch controls. The Standard has no remote; its controls are touch controls on the bar itself. On a single screen that is an occasional reach. On a dual setup it is two reaches, to two different bars, every time you want both sides changed — up to the left bar for the left screen, up to the right bar for the right screen. If your pattern is set-and-forget — choose a setting per side when the desk is arranged, then let each bar's auto-dimming handle the day — that reach is rare enough to be a non-issue. If you adjust warmth and brightness often, and on both screens, the reach is the recurring cost of the simpler pick, paid in duplicate.

One part of control does scale gracefully: each bar dims itself. Both bars carry an ambient light sensor and auto-dimming, and in a dual setup each sensor adjusts its own bar. Owners report the sensor keeping the desk evenly lit as room light changes, without repeated manual tweaks — and with a bar per screen, that covers the whole dual desk rather than one pool in the middle. Two honest limits carry over: the sensors react to room light, not the clock, so overhead lights left on keep both bars bright; and auto-dimming manages brightness only, while warmer or cooler light — both bars cover 2700K to 6500K — remains a setting you choose on each bar in turn.

Check three: power per bar — each bar needs its own nearby USB port

Power is the check dual buyers most often run last and regret first. Each bar is USB powered — the PRO+ is listed at 5W — and each needs its own connection. A dual setup therefore needs two nearby USB ports, not one port and good intentions. Desks accumulate devices faster than reachable ports, and a second bar ordered on the assumption that power will sort itself out ends up lighting only when something else is unplugged.

The constraint that makes this a planning job rather than a footnote is the cable. Owners of both bars describe the fixed USB cable as relatively short — it is the recurring complaint in our reviews of each model. Fixed means it cannot be swapped for a longer one, and short means the port has to come to the bar rather than the bar reaching the port. On a single-monitor desk, one nearby port somewhere behind the screen usually exists. On a dual desk, the two bars sit at opposite ends of a 43-to-49-inch span, so the question has to be asked twice, once per side: is there a port within that short cable's reach of the left bar, and separately of the right bar?

Work through the port sources in the order most dual desks actually have them. Monitors themselves often carry USB ports, and a port on each screen is the shortest run for the bar sitting on that screen — check your own monitors' layouts, because that is a fact about your screens, not about these bars. Next, the computer's ports: a tower at one end of the desk may serve the near bar and strand the far one. Finally, a wall adapter per side is the fallback that always works geometrically, with no dependence on the computer being awake. The right route is the one your desk's ports and cable reaches actually support, checked side by side.

Before you order: the two-minute dual check

Two minutes settles which version of this guide you are shopping in. First, the width test: add your two panels' full widths, bezels included — about 43 to 44 inches for a pair of 24-inch monitors, about 48 to 49 inches for a pair of 27-inch monitors — and compare that span with the 32-inch fit each bar here carries. That comparison is the whole argument for one bar per monitor. Second, the seam test: notice where your work actually spends the day. Work spread across both screens points at a bar per screen; work concentrated in the middle leaves the single-bar compromise open. Third, the port test: name the specific USB port the left bar will use and the specific port the right bar will use, and check the short fixed cable's reach to each. A port you cannot name is a port you do not have yet.

When one bar is the compromise, and when neither bar is enough

The single-bar compromise, stated plainly: one bar, centred over the pair, needs one port and one control, and lights the middle of a dual desk — the seam zone — better than anything else. For a desk where one screen does the real work and the second holds reference material, that can be a rational buy rather than a mistake. Take it knowingly: the bar sits over the seam rather than over either screen, coverage falls away toward both outer edges, and the setup is outside the bar's single-monitor fit across most of its span. It is a centre light, not dual coverage.

Past dual, honesty points off this page. A very wide triple setup multiplies every problem in check one: three screens span far beyond any reading of a 32-inch fit, centre-seam compromises multiply, and the port and control counts rise to three of everything. A curved ultrawide poses the fit question in its sharpest form, and owners' reports of uneven edge coverage on flat 34-inch and larger screens are the only evidence needed to route it elsewhere. For those desks, judge lighting by stated fit for the actual screen or span first — and consider whether a desk lamp or a room light, tools not limited to one screen's width, serves the desk better than any screen bar. We name those criteria rather than new products here, because no bar we review is rated for those setups.

Dual monitor light bar questions, answered honestly

Can one monitor light bar cover two monitors?

Not evenly, on the evidence recorded in our reviews. A dual setup spans about 43 to 44 inches with two 24-inch monitors and about 48 to 49 inches with two 27-inch monitors, while the Quntis Monitor Light Bar PRO+ is rated for monitors 15 to 32 inches and the Quntis Monitor Light Bar (Standard) for monitors up to 32 inches. Owners of a single 34-inch and larger ultrawide already report uneven coverage at the edges from both bars. One bar centred over a dual setup also sits over the centre bezel seam, so it lights the middle best and the outer edges least. The even-coverage route is one bar per monitor.

Do I need two monitor light bars for dual monitors?

If you want even light across both screens and the desk in front of them, yes — one bar per monitor is the route this guide recommends, because each bar then covers a screen inside its stated fit. A single centred bar is a workable compromise when most of your work lives on one screen and the second screen is a reference display, but take it knowingly: the middle will be lit best, the outer edges least, and the bar will sit over the seam between the screens rather than over either screen itself.

Can one PRO+ remote control two bars?

This guide makes no such claim, because it is not a sourced feature of the bar. Plan on one remote per bar instead: each Quntis Monitor Light Bar PRO+ comes with its own wireless rotary remote, so a two-bar dual setup puts two remotes on the desk, one for each screen. Each bar also dims itself from its own ambient light sensor, so day-to-day brightness can follow the room on each side without either remote being touched. The Quntis Monitor Light Bar (Standard) has no remote at all — its controls are touch controls on each bar itself.

How do I power two monitor light bars?

Plan two nearby USB ports, one for each bar. Each bar is USB powered — the PRO+ is listed at 5W — and owners of both bars describe the fixed USB cable as relatively short, so reach is the real check on a dual setup. Work through the ports you actually have before ordering: USB ports on the monitors themselves, ports on the computer, or a wall adapter on each side. Check the cable reach per side, left and right separately, because a port that serves the left bar comfortably may not reach the right one.

Will either Quntis bar cover a triple setup or a curved ultrawide?

Treat both as outside what these bars are rated for. Each bar here is rated to 32 inches at most, owners of 34-inch and larger ultrawides report uneven coverage at the edges from a single bar, and a triple setup is wider again than the dual setups this guide covers. For a very wide triple layout or a curved ultrawide, judge any light by its stated fit for that screen first — and consider honestly whether a desk lamp or room light, which is not limited to one screen width, serves that desk better than a screen bar does.

Bottom Line

One bar per monitor, controlled and powered per bar

Run the checks in order: measure the span, then count the controls, then name the ports. For even coverage across both screens, buy a bar per monitor — the Quntis PRO+ is the dual pick, with a wireless rotary remote per bar keeping every adjustment on the desk and a stated 500 lux at 30cm per bar. Set the light once per side and let auto-dimming handle the day? The Quntis Standard covers the same core job per screen with touch controls on each bar and nothing extra to power or lose. A single centred bar remains the budget compromise for a centre-weighted desk, seam caveat understood. Either way, plan two nearby USB ports for the short fixed cables, and if either screen is wider than 32 inches, shop by stated fit instead. Choosing for a single screen instead?
