Buying Guide — Laptop Stands and Monitor Arms

Best Laptop Stand and Monitor Arm Setup for Dual Screens

A laptop stand and a monitor arm are usually bought one at a time — and that is how a laptop ends up raised beside a monitor it does not line up with. The panel passes every check on its arm while the laptop quietly fails the stand underneath it, and the arm clamp and the stand base arrive competing for the same stretch of desk. Using both together, so a laptop screen sits beside an external monitor as a true dual-screen desk, is a matching problem, not two separate purchases, so this guide runs three checks a single-product guide can skip. Alignment: can the tops be brought into one line? Separate gates: does each screen pass its own fit checks, against its own hardware? And one desk: can the arm's back-edge mount and the stand's base share a single desk without crowding out the keyboard and mouse the raised laptop now needs? One pair fits the widest laptops and heaviest panels, one is built to be set once and left, one is built to be adjusted on the fly — and one stand here is honestly wrong for a seated desk, because its lift was built for standing.

Check one: screen-top alignment — the stand sets the rough height, the arm finishes it

A dual-screen desk works when the two screens read as one surface, decided at the top edge. When the tops line up, moving between screens is a sideways glance. When they do not — the laptop screen sitting a step below the monitor, as it does on a desk with no stand at all — every crossing is a small drop and a small refocus, repeated all day. Alignment therefore splits into two jobs done by two different pieces of hardware. The stand sets the laptop's rough height — the band the laptop screen starts in. The arm does the fine alignment, nudging the panel the last stretch until the top edges meet. Both halves adjusting gives you two chances to land the line; one half moving in steps gives you one.

The stands set that starting band very differently, and the recorded lift figures are the whole story. The Lamicall lifts across 5.7 to 8.1 inches, with an angle range of 45 to 180 degrees alongside it, so height and tilt are both in play while you align. The Amazon Basics stand works in six preset levels from 3.5 to 6 inches: honest steps, but steps — wherever the line falls between two steps, the arm covers the difference. The iLevel 2 takes a third route: its patented front slider adjusts height and tilt together, with the laptop still in place, so the rough height can be revisited in seconds. No numeric height range is recorded for it, and this guide will not invent one; the mechanism is what alignment runs on.

The arms finish the job, and adjustment type — not any invented figure — decides how fine that finish can be. The two gas-spring arms here, the HUANUO Single Monitor Arm and the NB North Bayou F80, adjust continuously: the panel can be eased up or down in small increments and held where it stops, which is exactly what chasing a top edge asks for. The F80 adds pan, fine-tuning the panel's angle toward the laptop screen as well as its height. The Amazon Basics Single Monitor Arm adjusts in the same four directions — height, tilt, swivel and rotation — but owners consistently describe its adjustment as stiff out of the box, a set-and-forget character: it aligns perfectly well, once, and then prefers to be left where it was set. No arm here carries a recorded numeric height or pole range, so this guide claims nothing in inches about arm travel. The honest claim is about control: continuous gas-spring movement for a line you expect to tune, stiff adjustment for a line you expect to set and keep.

Check two: each screen passes its own gate — the laptop against the stand, the panel against the arm

The common dual-screen mistake is checking the setup once, as if it were one product. It is two products holding two screens, and each screen passes against the hardware that holds it — separately. The laptop is checked against the stand: size against the stated maximum, weight against the maximum where one is recorded. The panel is checked against the arm: weight without its factory stand, size against the stated range, and VESA pattern against the 75x75 and 100x100 patterns all three arms accept. Passing one gate says nothing about the other. A laptop that fits its stand can sit beside a panel too heavy for its arm, and the reverse fails just as quietly.

On the laptop side, the gates are size first, then weight where a weight exists. The Lamicall takes the widest laptops here, 10 to 17.3 inches, and records no separate weight ceiling — size is its gate. The Amazon Basics stand takes laptops up to 15.6 inches and adds the strictest weight gate here: a maximum of 10 lb, which rules out heavy 16-inch machines. The iLevel 2 takes laptops up to 17 inches, and its manufacturer specifies no maximum weight at all — the honest gate is size alone, stated plainly rather than filled in. The Upryze takes 10 to 17 inch laptops; its problem for this guide is not fit but height.

On the panel side, weight without the stand is the figure that counts, and the three ceilings are close but not equal: HUANUO up to 22 lb across 13 to 32 inches, the Amazon Basics arm up to 20 lb across panels up to 32 inches, the F80 up to 19.8 lb across the 17 to 30 inch range this site publishes from its manual. All three accept VESA 75x75 and 100x100 — but the pattern check still has to be run on your own panel, because a panel with no VESA pattern, or a larger one, fits none of them. Judge each ceiling as a ceiling to stay visibly under, with the panel-only figure, not a target to land on: an arm at its limit holds least comfortably exactly where a dual-screen desk asks it to hold stillest.

Check three: one desk edge, one surface — the clamp behind, the base in front, and input gear the raised laptop demands

A dual-screen setup mounts in two places at once, and they must not be planned as one. The arm takes the desk's back edge: all three arms here offer a C-clamp that grips the edge, a grommet mount that bolts through the desk, or both in the box — the F80 and the Amazon Basics arm include both mounts, and the HUANUO offers C-clamp or grommet fitting. That grip needs a clear stretch of edge solid enough to carry arm and panel together. The stand takes the surface: its base sits flat in front of that edge, under the raised laptop. Edge and surface are different budgets — a desk can have plenty of surface and still fail at the edge, where a back rail or cable tray sits exactly where the clamp wants to close.

The surface budget is where the stands differ most. The Lamicall's large weighted base is its recorded footprint trade: owners praise the planted feel it gives the raised laptop, and the same base is the largest claim on surface here, at 2.1 lb of stand altogether. Plan the desk around it: base first, then the space in front of it. The Amazon Basics stand and the Lamicall both fold flat, returning the surface when the laptop travels; the iLevel 2 does not fold at all, so its footprint is a permanent resident of its desk. The Upryze only partially folds — another stand built to stay set up.

One more surface claim is easy to forget until the laptop is already raised: a laptop at monitor height cannot be typed on. Lift any laptop here to sit beside a monitor and its keyboard and trackpad lift with it, out of working reach — and on the Lamicall the taller settings are bouncier if you type on the laptop itself, which settles the question anyway. A dual-screen desk therefore needs an external keyboard and mouse in front of the stand, as part of the setup, and that space belongs in the surface budget from the start. Choosing that pair is its own matching problem, and our keyboard and mouse pair guide for working from home runs it properly; this guide simply reserves the room and makes no separate picks here.

When the stand or arm already in your room is the right answer

A stand and an arm bought separately are not automatically mismatched: if the two top edges already line up, each screen already passes its own gate, and the clamp, the base and your keyboard and mouse already share the desk without a fight, no pair here buys you anything new. The cases that earn a rethink are specific — a laptop screen sitting stubbornly below its neighbour, a panel riding at its arm's ceiling, a stand base that has evicted the keyboard. Name your case in the terms of the three checks, and buy only the half that fails.

Before you buy: the five-minute setup test

Five minutes of checking tells you which pair you are shopping for. First, the alignment test: look at the step between your laptop's top edge and your monitor's, then decide whether you will tune that line often — continuous adjustment on both sides — or once, which the set-and-forget pair was built for. Second, the two gates, run separately: your laptop's size against the stand's maximum — 17.3 inches for the Lamicall, 17 for the iLevel 2 and Upryze, 15.6 for the Amazon Basics stand, with its 10 lb weight ceiling alongside — and your panel's weight without its stand, its size and its VESA pattern against the arm's, at 22 lb for the HUANUO, 20 lb for the Amazon Basics arm and 19.8 lb for the F80. Third, the desk test: a clear stretch of back edge for the clamp or a grommet route through the desk, surface in front of it for the stand's base — the Lamicall's weighted base first if that is your pair — and surface in front of that for the external keyboard and mouse the raised laptop requires. Those answers usually name the pair.

Laptop stand and monitor arm setup questions, answered honestly

What is the best laptop stand and monitor arm setup for dual screens?

Of the products we review, it depends on how you adjust. The widest-fit pair is the Lamicall Adjustable Laptop Stand with the HUANUO Single Monitor Arm: the stand takes laptops to 17.3 inches and the arm takes panels to 22 lb, and both adjust continuously, so alignment can be set on either side. The set-and-forget pair is the Amazon Basics Laptop Stand with the Amazon Basics Single Monitor Arm: six preset steps on the stand and stiff adjustment on the arm, for a desk set once and left. The adjust-on-the-fly pair is the Rain Design iLevel 2 with the NB North Bayou F80: the slider moves with the laptop in place and the gas spring fine-tunes the angle with pan.

How do I align the tops of a laptop screen and an external monitor?

Set the rough height with the stand first, then finish with the arm. The stand decides where the laptop screen starts: the Lamicall lifts 5.7 to 8.1 inches and the Amazon Basics stand steps 3.5 to 6 inches across six levels. The arm then does the fine alignment, because a gas-spring arm adjusts continuously and can be nudged until the two top edges meet. A stiff arm like the Amazon Basics arm still aligns, but it suits setting the line once and leaving it rather than nudging it daily.

Do I check the laptop and the monitor against the same limits?

No — each screen passes its own gate separately. The laptop is checked against the stand: its size against the stand's stated maximum, and its weight where a maximum is recorded, as with the Amazon Basics stand's 10 lb ceiling. The external panel is checked against the arm: its weight without its factory stand, its size, and its VESA 75x75 or 100x100 pattern. Passing one gate says nothing about the other, so a laptop that fits its stand can still sit beside a panel that is too heavy for its arm.

Can a laptop stand and a monitor arm share one desk?

Yes, if the edge and the surface are planned separately. The arm's clamp or grommet goes on the back edge of the desk, while the stand's base occupies surface in front of it — the Lamicall's large weighted base is the recorded footprint to plan around. Keep the clamp's grip clear of the stand's base, leave room for the raised laptop's cables, and remember that a laptop lifted to monitor height needs an external keyboard and mouse on the surface as well.

Is the Lifelong Upryze right for a seated dual-screen desk?

No — for a seated dual-screen desk it overshoots. The Upryze lifts 10 to 17 inch laptops up to 20 inches on continuous knob adjustment, a range built for standing setups, so at a seated desk the laptop screen sits well above the external monitor's top edge instead of beside it. Readers who work standing should use our standing-desk laptop stand guide instead, where that lift is the point rather than the problem.
