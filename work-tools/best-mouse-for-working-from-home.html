Buying Guide — Mice

Best Mouse for Working From Home

The short answer first: a mouse is an angle product before it is a features product. Palm-down or 57-degree handshake is built into the body — no setting changes it — so the posture your forearm lives in decides the shortlist, and only then do hand size, scroll wheel and receiver family separate the survivors. The four mice we've reviewed split exactly that way: two are vertical mice at the same fixed angle, divided by the hand size each body is built for, one is the conventional shape at its most refined, for work where scrolling matters more than angle, and one never moves at all — a stationary trackball for desks with no room to mouse. Here's how to tell which posture is yours.

Run the angle, hand-size and scroll checks before the features check

Angle first, because it cannot be adjusted away. A conventional mouse asks your forearm to rest twisted, palm-down, for the whole working day. A vertical mouse stands the hand up at what Logitech calls the natural handshake position — on both vertical mice below, a fixed 57 degrees. That angle is the product: it does not flatten for gaming, steepen toward fully upright, or adjust between users. If the palm-down twist is the strain you feel by mid-afternoon, the vertical pair is your shortlist; if it isn't, the conventional pick below refines the shape you already use instead of replacing it. And there is a third answer that changes neither angle nor shape: remove the movement. A trackball sits still and your thumb does the pointing, which is why it belongs on the shortlist whenever the real constraint is desk space or an arm that should travel less.

Hand size second, because on the vertical pair it is a hard gate, not a preference. The two verticals share the angle and divide on scale: one body is built for small-to-medium hands and crowds large ones, the other is built for medium-to-large hands and crowds small ones from the opposite direction. Measure your hand against your current mouse before ordering either — a vertical mouse in the wrong size is not a compromise fit, it is the wrong mouse. The trackball sidesteps the size gate differently: its sculpted body is right-hand only and sized for a resting palm, so the check there is handedness, not hand length.

Scroll and receiver third, because they decide your daily friction. If your day is long documents, wide spreadsheets and timelines, the wheel is the feature you will touch ten thousand times — and the four wheels below are genuinely different tools. And Logitech runs two incompatible wireless families, Unifying and Logi Bolt: three of these mice ship with Bolt, one ships with Unifying, and a one-receiver desk only works when the mouse and keyboard are in the same family. Three of the four also pair over Bluetooth to up to three devices; the trackball connects to one device at a time.

For smaller hands that want the angle changed: Logitech Lift Vertical

The Lift is the pick when the palm-down twist is the problem and your hands are small-to-medium. Instead of asking your forearm to flatten to meet the desk, it stands the hand at a fixed 57-degree handshake angle on a compact body with a softly textured grip and a snug thumb rest — a design Logitech describes as ergo-certified against criteria set out by leading ergonomists. Reviewers who live on vertical mice found it easier to grasp than any other vertical they had used, with side buttons easier to reach than its larger rivals, and long-term switchers describe working longer before needing a wrist stretch once they learned to move from the forearm. The clicks are genuinely quiet, the SmartWheel covers precision and speed scrolling, it switches between up to three devices over Bluetooth or its included Logi Bolt receiver, and the single AA battery is rated for up to two years — the longest battery figure on this page by a wide margin.

Fit check: hand size is a hard gate. Large hands crowd the compact body — owners with a claw grip describe fingertips extending past the buttons — and Logitech's larger vertical below is the honest alternative rather than this mouse in hope. The 57-degree angle is fixed, there is no rechargeable battery, and this listing is the right-hand Graphite version; the left-hand Lift is a different listing. It is a work mouse, not a gaming or fast-precision tool. Judge it as the fixed-angle vertical for the hands the category usually skips, because that is exactly what it is.

For larger hands that want the same angle: Logitech MX Vertical

The MX Vertical is the pick when the Lift's idea is right and its body is too small. It holds the hand at the same ergonomist-tested 57-degree handshake angle in a larger body sized for medium-to-large right hands, with a textured rubber surface and thumb rest owners describe simply resting on between tasks — on its listing, comfort is the most-mentioned theme by a wide margin. It is rechargeable over USB-C, rated for up to four months per charge with about three hours of use from a one-minute quick charge, and it keeps working while plugged in. A cursor-speed switch on the top ridge changes DPI instantly across its 400–4,000 range, and it switches between up to three devices over Bluetooth, its included Unifying receiver, or the cable, with Logitech Flow across computers.

Fit check: it is right-hand only, completely — there is no left-hand version — and small-to-medium hands crowd it from the other direction; the Lift is the same angle in the body shaped for them. Its clicks are ordinary and audible, not the Lift's quiet clicks or the 3S's Quiet Clicks, so a shared room hears it. The receiver is the older Unifying standard, not Logi Bolt, and the mouse has no storage compartment for it. A minority of owners also report button and scroll-wheel faults developing over time, which our full review records rather than smooths over.

For scroll-heavy work in the familiar shape: Logitech MX Master 3S

The MX Master 3S is the pick when your posture is not the complaint and your workload is: long documents, wide spreadsheets, multi-monitor pointing. It keeps the conventional palm-down shape and perfects it — a sculpted body with a thumb rest, Quiet Clicks, and the best scroll system in this comparison: a machined-steel MagSpeed wheel that runs ratcheted for precision or free-spins at up to 1,000 lines per second, switching modes by how hard you flick, plus a separate thumb wheel for horizontal movement. Its Darkfield sensor reaches 8,000 DPI and tracks on glass, it switches between up to three devices over Bluetooth or its included Logi Bolt receiver with Flow across computers, and the rechargeable battery is rated for up to 70 days, with three hours of use from a one-minute charge.

Fit check: it refines the palm-down posture but cannot change it — if the twist is your strain, a better conventional mouse is still a conventional mouse, and one of the verticals above is the honest answer. It is a large, right-hand-only body that smaller hands overflow, with no left-hand version at all. Owners also split on durability in ways our review records plainly: the soft-touch coating can discolour and wear, and the owner reliability record divides on the left click. Buy it for the wheels and the shape, with those limits named, not despite them.

For desks with no room to mouse: Logitech ERGO M575S

The M575S is the pick when the problem is not the angle but the travel. The mouse never moves on the desk — your thumb rolls a ball to move the cursor while the sculpted body sits where you put it — so it needs no mouse pad, no clear run of desk, and no arm movement, in a shape Logitech says is certified by United States Ergonomics for 25 percent less forearm muscle strain than a standard Logitech mouse. It is the 2024 update of the long-running M575, and the update is specific: the clicks are now quiet clicks, the older Unifying receiver is replaced by an encrypted Logi Bolt receiver that stores inside the battery compartment, and the single AA battery is rated for up to 18 months. On its listing, owners praise exactly the things the design promises — comfort, desk-space savings and smooth thumb control.

Fit check: the trade is a trackball's trade. There is a real learning curve measured in days while your thumb learns the pointing, and thumb-done precision has a ceiling the MX Master 3S does not. It is right-hand only with no left version, connects to one device at a time rather than switching between three, has no DPI button (sensitivity adjusts in Logi Options+, up to 2,000 DPI), no horizontal scrolling and no free-spin wheel mode, and the ball socket wants a brief clean every few weeks. Buy it when desk space or arm movement is the problem it solves; buy a vertical or the 3S when it is not.

When none of these four is the right buy

If you mouse left-handed, this page mostly routes you elsewhere: the 3S, MX Vertical and M575S have no left version at all, and the left-hand Lift is a separate listing from the right-hand one reviewed here — we will not pretend a listing we have not reviewed as a listing is the same pick. The same honesty applies to gaming: all four are office mice, and a fast, light gaming mouse is a category we have not reviewed, so we will not name one we haven't done the work on. And if you want an adjustable angle — flatter than 57 degrees or steeper — none of these bodies adjusts; that is a different category by design.

And if your current mouse actually fits — forearm comfortable, hand sized to the body, no strain by afternoon — the right buy may be no buy, or a placement fix: our ergonomic desk setup guide puts the mouse at elbow height, close enough that your shoulder stays down and your elbow stays near your side, and a mouse used at the wrong height strains at any angle. Persistent wrist, forearm or shoulder pain, in particular, is not a gear specification — it deserves a clinician before it deserves a checkout.

Mouse questions, answered honestly

Should I buy a vertical mouse or a conventional mouse?

That depends on where the strain is. A vertical mouse stands the hand at a handshake angle instead of asking the forearm to twist palm-down: both vertical mice we review hold the hand at 57 degrees, and reviewers and owners describe working longer before needing a wrist stretch once they learn to move from the forearm. A conventional mouse like the MX Master 3S refines the palm-down shape instead — a sculpted body, better wheels and quieter clicks — without changing the angle. If the palm-down twist is what you feel by mid-afternoon, go vertical; if your posture is comfortable and your work is scroll-heavy, the conventional pick is the more capable tool. There is also a third answer to the angle question: the ERGO M575S trackball removes the movement instead of changing the angle — the mouse never travels, your thumb rolls a ball, and it is the pick when desk space or arm travel is the problem.

Which vertical mouse fits my hand size?

Hand size is the whole fork between the two verticals. The Logitech Lift is built for small-to-medium hands — a compact 4.25-inch body reviewers found easy to grasp, and large hands crowd it. The Logitech MX Vertical is the same 57-degree angle in a larger, roomier body sized for medium-to-large hands, and small hands crowd it from the other direction. The other differences follow from the generations: the Lift runs up to 24 months on a single AA battery and uses the Logi Bolt receiver, while the MX Vertical is rechargeable over USB-C for up to four months and uses the older Unifying receiver.

Which of these mice is best for scrolling long documents and spreadsheets?

The Logitech MX Master 3S, and it is not close. Its MagSpeed electromagnetic wheel switches between a ratcheted precision mode and a free-spin mode Logitech rates at up to 1,000 lines per second, and it adds a separate thumb wheel for horizontal movement through wide spreadsheets and timelines. The Lift's SmartWheel covers precision and speed-scroll modes in a smaller body, and the MX Vertical and the ERGO M575S trackball have plain precision wheels with a middle click — capable, but scrolling is not what either is for.

Can these mice share a receiver with my Logitech keyboard?

Only within the same receiver family. The Lift, the MX Master 3S and the ERGO M575S ship with Logi Bolt receivers — the M575S receiver stores inside its battery compartment; the MX Vertical ships with the older Unifying receiver, and Logitech states Bolt is not compatible with Unifying technology. So a Bolt keyboard from our reviews (the MX Keys S or MX Mechanical) shares a family with the Lift, the 3S and the M575S, while the Ergo K860's Unifying receiver pairs the other way round, with the MX Vertical. The Lift, MX Vertical and 3S also pair over Bluetooth to up to three devices; the M575S connects to one device at a time.

Is there a left-handed option, and are any of these good for gaming?

Left-handed: of these four listings, none is a left-hand mouse — the MX Master 3S, MX Vertical and ERGO M575S are right-hand only with no left version, and this Lift listing is the right-hand Graphite version; Logitech sells a separate left-hand Lift listing, which is a different ASIN we have not reviewed as a listing. Gaming: no. All four are office mice — sensors up to 2,000 DPI on the trackball, 4,000 DPI on the verticals and 8,000 DPI on the 3S, tuned for documents, browsers, code and design placement, not fast lifts, flicks or gaming polling. A gaming mouse is a different purchase.

What if I have almost no desk space for a mouse?

That is the case the trackball exists for. The Logitech ERGO M575S never moves on the desk — it needs no mouse pad and no clear run of surface, so it works on a shallow desk, a tray table or a crowded corner where a travelling mouse has nowhere to go. The trade is a learning curve measured in days while your thumb learns the pointing, a precision ceiling below a good conventional mouse, and a standard wheel with no horizontal scrolling.

Bottom Line

Check angle, hand size, movement and scroll — then pick

Name the posture your forearm needs, the hand size the body must match, whether the mouse should move at all, and the wheel your workload actually uses, and buy the mouse that matches all four: a changed, handshake angle in a compact body points to the Logitech Lift; the same angle at a larger scale, rechargeable, points to the MX Vertical; the familiar palm-down shape at its scroll-heavy best points to the MX Master 3S; and no movement at all — a stationary thumb trackball for a desk with no room to mouse — points to the Logitech ERGO M575S.
