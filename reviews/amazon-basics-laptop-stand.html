What Could Be Better

The fit ceiling is explicit: laptops up to 15.6 inches and 10 lbs, which rules out heavy 16-inch machines. And like every riser, it lifts the built-in keyboard along with the screen, so long sessions still call for an external keyboard and mouse at elbow height.

The height ceiling deserves its own check. Six inches of maximum lift is enough for many seated desks, but it is a hard ceiling: if your seated eye level sits well above your desk — a taller chair, a taller torso, a low desk — even the top setting can leave the screen below eye level. That is a fit question you can answer with a tape measure before ordering, using the height check below, and it is the main reason to look at a taller stand instead of this one.

Will It Fit Your Laptop? The Size and Weight Check

This stand carries two hard limits, both printed in its specifications: laptops up to 15.6 inches, and up to 10 lbs. Everything else about fit follows from those two numbers, so check them against the laptop you actually own — the size printed on the box it came in, and the weight from its manufacturer's specification page — before anything else on this page matters.

Weight is rarely the binding limit at the smaller sizes and often is at the larger ones. How Much Lift Do You Actually Need?

Ergonomic guidance for seated computer work — the OSHA workstation guidance our laptop stand posture guide is built on — puts the top of the screen at or just below eye level, the screen roughly 20 to 40 inches from your eyes, and your gaze angled slightly down. A laptop on a desk fails that by construction: the screen sits several inches too low, so your neck does the difference all day. A stand exists to close exactly that gap, no more and no less.

You can measure your own gap in about a minute. Sit in your normal chair at your desk, look straight ahead, and measure from the desktop up to your eye level. Then measure from the desktop to the centre of your laptop screen with the laptop sitting flat. Subtract the second number from the first: that difference is the lift you need. If it lands between 3.5 and 6 inches, one of this stand's six levels covers it. Work through the levels from the middle out — set a level, sit back, and check the top of the screen against your eye level rather than judging by feel alone.

If your gap is larger than 6 inches, this stand alone will not close it, and no honest review should pretend a setting it does not have will. The fixes are a taller stand — the Lamicall lifts to 8.1 inches and the Lifelong Upryze to as much as 20 inches — or a different architecture altogether, an external monitor on an arm, which is the fork our laptop stand vs monitor arm guide works through. If your gap is smaller than 3.5 inches, even the lowest level overshoots slightly; the angled screen is still an improvement over flat on the desk, but a fixed low riser may suit you better.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will the Amazon Basics laptop stand fit a 16-inch laptop?

No — not within its rating. The stand is specified for laptops up to 15.6 inches and 10 lbs, so a 16-inch machine is outside the stated maximum even when it weighs less than 10 lbs. For a 16-inch laptop, look at a stand rated for larger machines, such as the Lamicall adjustable stand (rated to 17.3 inches) or the Rain Design iLevel 2 (rated to 17 inches).

Do I still need an external keyboard and mouse with this stand?

For long work sessions, yes. Raising the laptop lifts its built-in keyboard along with the screen, so typing on the raised machine puts your hands well above elbow height. Ergonomic guidance puts the keyboard at or just below elbow height, which is why the owners who stay happy with any laptop stand long-term are the ones who pair it with an external keyboard and mouse and let the laptop be the screen only.

Which height setting should I start with?

Start with a middle level, sit back in your normal working position, and check the top of the screen against your eye level — ergonomic guidance puts it at or just below eye level, roughly 20 to 40 inches from your eyes. Move one level at a time from there. Changing levels takes two hands, so set the laptop down first rather than adjusting with the machine on the stand.

Is the stand stable at its highest setting?

Owner feedback is consistent that stability is best at the lower and middle settings on a flat, solid surface, and that the stand feels least planted at its highest setting, where vigorous typing is more noticeable. If your work is typing-heavy all day, judge the stand at the setting you will actually live on, not the lowest one.

Does the open design help keep a laptop cool?

Owners credit the open frame with keeping air moving under the laptop and with cooler-running machines, and the raised, open design does leave the underside vents unobstructed compared with sitting flat on a desk. It is passive — there is no fan — so treat it as removing a barrier to airflow, not as a fix for a laptop that overheats for other reasons.

Does the Amazon Basics laptop stand fold flat for travel?

Yes. The aluminum frame folds flat with no tools and no loose parts, which is why owners describe the same stand moving between a home desk, a drawer, and a laptop bag. That portability is its clearest advantage over fixed-frame stands such as the Rain Design iLevel 2, which does not fold at all.

Bottom Line

The Amazon Basics laptop stand is the simple default for laptops up to 15.6 inches: six preset heights, an open airflow design, and a fold-flat aluminum frame, with its limits — preset steps and a 10 lb ceiling — stated plainly. Pair it with an external keyboard and mouse and it does the posture job most desks need.
