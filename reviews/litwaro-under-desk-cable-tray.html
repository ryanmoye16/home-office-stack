Our Verdict

4.6 out of 5 — Great

Litwaro cable tray review: a clamp-on carbon-steel mesh basket, 13.4 inches long, that lifts a power strip and its cable slack off the floor with no drilling. That is the whole product, and for the most common desk-cable problem it is the right shape of answer. Most cable mess is not a routing problem along a wall; it is one power strip, two or three power bricks and a coil of slack sitting on the floor under the desk, gathering dust and finding feet. This tray takes exactly that pile, puts it in a ventilated steel basket at the back edge of the desk, and keeps it there with a clamp instead of the adhesive strips whose failures fill the owner reviews of the raceway style. The listing's own figures are modest and checkable: carbon steel, 13.38 by 4.72 inches, 1.63 lb, about 15 lb of capacity, two pass-through holes on each side, rubber pads where the clamp meets the desk, and a clamp range of 0.4 to 2 inches of flat edge. Amazon's listing for this model (BJXCJ_01, the Black version) carries 4.6 stars across 2,555 reviews as of January 2026, and the roundups that test clamp trays against the alternatives keep landing it in the same place — the no-drill tray pick — for the same reasons owners give: it installs in minutes, it does not sag under a heavy strip, and the mesh lets power bricks breathe instead of cooking in a closed box. The honest limits are size and edge, and they are both printed on the listing. Thirteen inches is one ordinary power strip with little to spare, the 4.72-inch width makes bulky bricks a tight fit that reviewers flag, and a clamp is a binary thing: a flat edge in range and it is the sturdiest mount in the category; a beveled, curved or out-of-range edge and this product simply does not fit your desk. Judge it as what it is — the compact, reversible way to get one strip and its slack off the floor — and it earns its place as the first review in this category.

What We Liked

The clamp is the reason to buy this tray over the adhesive alternatives, and it is the point reviewers keep returning to. Adhesive raceways and trays live or die on a bond that heat, dust and cable weight all work against; a clamp is mechanical, reversible and indifferent to all three. Roundup testing of this tray describes the clamp system as surprisingly sturdy — no budging through sit-stand height changes, no sagging under a heavy surge protector — and the soft rubber pads on the clamp faces protect the desk surface it grips, which is the detail that makes a clamp acceptable on furniture you care about. It also comes off in minutes when the desk moves or the setup changes, leaving nothing behind: no holes, no residue, no peeled finish.

The basket itself is the right material for the job it does. A power strip and two power bricks are a small, warm pile of electronics, and the carbon-steel mesh gives them air on every side instead of sealing them into a plastic box; the listing itself notes the mesh does not accumulate dust the way a solid tray does. The two holes on each side are placed where cables actually need to go — down to the floor outlet at one end, up to the desk surface at the other — and the open top means adding or removing a cable is a reach, not a disassembly. Owners and roundups converge on the same summary: the strip and its slack disappear from the floor, cables stay reachable from the sides, and the floor under the desk becomes vacuumable again.

The size, which the limits section will treat as a constraint, is also a genuine fit for the desks this site mostly serves. A 13.4-inch tray covers the strip zone of a small or medium desk without spanning the whole rear edge — it leaves room beside it for the cable drop to the floor and does not fight a monitor arm's clamp or a desk frame's crossbar for the same inches of edge as often as a 25-inch tray does. For a one-strip desk, which is most home-office desks, the compact footprint is the correct amount of tray.

What Could Be Better

Size is the first limit, and it is a hard one. At 13.38 inches long and 4.72 inches wide, the basket fits one ordinary power strip lengthwise with little spare; a long strip, a wide strip with side-facing outlets, or a strip plus two bulky power bricks is a tight fit that reviewers of this tray name directly — larger strips need careful positioning, and the tray's depth can limit bulkier adapters. Bricks that will not fit inside still have to live somewhere, and a brick dangling from a short cord beside the tray recreates a smaller version of the mess the tray was bought to end. Measure your strip before ordering; the figures above are the listing's, and they are not generous.

The clamp's fit is binary in a second way: the desk edge itself. The listing is explicit that the mount suits flat edges from 0.4 to 2 inches thick. A beveled or waterfall edge gives the clamp a slope instead of a face; a curved or bullnose edge gives it a line instead of a surface; a top thicker than 2 inches is simply outside the stated range, whatever one roundup's 2.4-inch figure claims for the line. Desks with a rear crossbar, a modesty panel or a cable spine where the tray needs to sit can also block the mount or the basket — the inward/outward choice solves some of those conflicts, not all. None of this is a defect; it is the price of a mechanical mount, and it is checkable with a tape measure in under a minute, before the box is opened.

A tray also only solves the gathering point. It does nothing for the run from the tray to a wall outlet, nothing for cables that travel along the desk to a printer or a second device across the room, and nothing for the single charging cable you want held at the desk's front edge — those are raceway, sleeve and clip jobs, and this site's format comparison below says so plainly. Standing-desk owners get one more caveat, and it is about their cables, not the tray: everything in the basket rises with the desk, so the drop from tray to floor outlet needs a service loop with enough slack for full height, tied so it cannot snag — a taut cable is the first thing a rising desk pulls out of a socket.

Performance in Daily Use

Across the roundups that test this tray, the behaviour reported is consistent: clamp it on, load the strip and bricks, and it stays where it was put. Testing notes describe assembly in as little as three minutes, a clamp that does not loosen through repeated sit-stand cycles, and no visible sag with a heavy surge protector in the basket — the three failure modes of the category (slow installs, wandering clamps, drooping trays) are the three things this design is reported not to do. The mesh's ventilation is the quieter performance point: power bricks in a closed box heat the box; bricks in an open steel mesh sit in room air, which is where their own cooling assumes they are.

Owner feedback at scale points the same way the roundups do. The listing's 4.6-star average across 2,555 reviews (January 2026) is a strong pool for a product this simple, and the themes in owner reports are the practical ones — cables off the floor, the strip reachable but out of sight, the under-desk area cleanable — with the recurring caveats being the ones this review prints above: strips and bricks at the edge of the size, and desks whose edges are outside the clamp's range. PCWorld's editor, writing about the under-desk tray method generally, landed on the clamp variety as the preference and on two trays — one for the strip, one for bricks — as the grown-up configuration for heavier setups; that is method advice, not a claim about this model, but it is the right frame for its size: one tray per pile, not one tray stretched across two.

Who This Is For

Remote workers whose under-desk problem is a pile, not a network: one power strip on the floor, its slack coiled around it, a laptop brick or two in the tangle, and feet, a vacuum or a pet finding all of it. If your desk has a flat rear edge between 0.4 and 2 inches thick, this tray is the compact mechanical fix — and it is the reviewed way to do the single most useful step in our pet-proofing your desk guide: get the strip and its cords up off the floor and out of reach, in a basket a pet cannot pull down the way it can worry a loose cable.

Skip it if your desk edge is beveled, curved, thicker than 2 inches or thinner than 0.4, or if a frame crossbar sits exactly where the tray needs to hang — the clamp has no fallback mount, and forcing it is how desks get marked. Skip it if your strip is longer than about 13 inches or your bricks are bulky and numerous; the honest answers there are a longer tray (Litwaro sells 25-inch versions as separate listings, which this review does not cover) or two trays run side by side. And skip it if your cables' problem is distance rather than gathering — a long run along a wall or desk edge wants an adhesive raceway, and a single wandering charging cable wants a clip; the format table below sorts the jobs before you spend on the wrong one.

Will It Fit Your Desk and Your Strip? The Edge, Strip and Slack Check

This tray carries three fit facts, and they matter more than anything else on the page: the clamp needs a flat edge between 0.4 and 2 inches thick, the basket is 13.38 inches long and 4.72 inches wide, and everything it holds travels with the desk. Check those three against your desk, your strip and your outlet before anything else here matters.

The edge check comes first because it is binary. Run a finger along the back edge of your desktop: flat and square is a fit; beveled, curved or rounded is not, however close the thickness is. Then measure the strip, not the cable pile — its body length against the tray's 13.38 inches, and your bulkiest brick against the 4.72-inch width. A strip that fits with an inch to spare and bricks that sit inside the basket is the setup reviewers describe working; a strip at the line and bricks perched on the rim is the setup they describe fighting.

Tray, Raceway or Box? The Three Cable Formats, Sorted by Job

Cable products fail when they are bought for the wrong job, so this category opens with the sort. A tray gathers: it takes the pile where cables already collect — the strip, the bricks, the slack — and lifts it. A raceway routes: it carries a line of cables along an edge or wall and hides the run. A box conceals: it sits on the floor or desk and puts a lid over a strip you would rather not see but do not need to lift. Most tidy desks end up using a tray for the pile plus something else for any run that leaves it; buying one format to do another's job is how cable money gets wasted.

The short version: take this Litwaro tray when the problem is the under-desk pile on a desk with a clampable edge. Take an adhesive J-channel raceway when cables travel together along a desk edge or down a wall — and accept that its mount is a bond, with the surface preparation and the failure mode that implies. Take a lidded box when the strip stays in view on a desk or the floor (no clampable edge, a desk against a glass wall) and concealment matters more than lifting. Frequently Asked Questions

What desks does the Litwaro tray fit?

Desks with a flat rear edge between 0.4 and 2 inches thick — the clamp's stated range on this listing. Flat is the operative word: beveled, curved and waterfall edges do not give the clamp a face to grip, and a rear crossbar or modesty panel where the tray needs to hang can block it in either install direction. Measure the edge's thickness and run a finger along it before ordering; both checks together take under a minute.

Will my power strip fit in it?

Measure the strip's body against the basket: 13.38 inches long, 4.72 inches wide. An ordinary strip fits lengthwise with its bricks beside it; a long or wide strip, or bulky wall-wart bricks, is the tight fit reviewers of this tray consistently flag. If your strip is at or over the length, the honest options are a longer tray — Litwaro's 25-inch versions are separate listings this review does not cover — or two of these side by side, one for the strip and one for the bricks.

Can I use it on a standing desk?

Yes — the clamp mounts the tray to the desk, so it rises and lowers with it, and roundup testing reports the clamp holding through repeated height cycles without loosening or sagging under a loaded strip. The discipline is in your cables, not the tray: leave a service loop between the tray and the floor outlet with enough slack for the desk's full height, and tie the loop so it cannot snag on the frame on the way up.

What is the difference between installing it inward and outward?

Direction. Mounted inward, the basket hangs on the wall side of the desk edge — the most concealed position, and the one that can conflict with a rear crossbar. Mounted outward, it hangs on the room side, a little more visible and a little easier to reach into. The listing supports both on the same clamp; choose by which side of your edge is clear and how often you expect to add or remove a cable.

Will the clamp damage my desk?

The clamp faces carry soft rubber pads, and reviewers report them protecting the desk surface as intended. The remaining risk is the one any clamp carries: over-tightening on a soft or hollow top can compress or mark it. Hand-tight until the tray does not move is the whole technique — the load it carries is about 15 lb at most, and the clamp does not need tool torque to hold it.

Is the white Litwaro tray the same product?

It is a different listing, not a colour option on this one. This review covers the Black tray, model BJXCJ_01, ASIN B0BZ3GHM8N. The White version (B0C8B33DC2) lists a different length — 15.75 inches against this one's 13.38 — so its fit is not this fit, and Litwaro's 25-inch trays are further listings again. Every figure in this review belongs to the Black listing only; if you buy the white one, re-run the fit checks against its own numbers.

Bottom Line

The Litwaro Under Desk Cable Management Tray is the right first product in this category because it does the category's core job mechanically: a carbon-steel mesh basket, clamped to a flat desk edge in minutes, that takes one power strip and its slack off the floor — no drilling, no adhesive to fail, backed by 4.6 stars across 2,555 reviews on Amazon's listing for this model. Its limits are printed in its own figures: 13.38 inches is one strip, 4.72 inches makes bulky bricks tight, and the clamp fits flat edges from 0.4 to 2 inches and nothing else. Measure your strip and your edge; if both are in range, this is the tidy under-desk answer, and the starting point for the cable layer of a desk setup.
