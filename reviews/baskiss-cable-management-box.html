What Could Be Better

The narrow interior is the first limit, and it is not a footnote — it is the purchase decision. The listing's compatibility warning sets the gate at a strip smaller than about 11.4 by 4.1 inches, the roundups repeat 'measure your power strip first' as the headline caution, and the failure stories are measurement stories: buyer notes include a regular strip that simply did not fit, with a smaller strip bought separately. Height is the quieter half of the same limit — the lid needs the interior's height for the strip plus whatever plugs and bricks stand on it, and pros-and-cons lists for this box note cords that stop the lid sitting properly. If your strip and its tallest adapter do not fit with room to spare, no tidiness argument survives contact with the tape measure.

Heat is the second limit, and this review will not paper over it: no source found for this box states ventilation holes in the lid or body, so none are claimed here. The outlets are the openings. A strip and a phone charger at ordinary loads are the setup owners report living with, but a box is a closed space around warm electronics, and high-draw bricks or a heavily loaded strip are a genuine fit consideration — load the box, check after an hour that nothing inside is more than warm, and never seal a brick that already runs hot in the open air into any closed box. The listing's high-impact-resistance line is likewise a manufacturer's claim no roundup in the available evidence tested, so it earns no weight in the verdict.

The third limit is what freestanding means: the box conceals the strip but does not lift, anchor or move it. A floor box still sits where feet, vacuums and chair casters go — anti-skid lines resist sliding, they do not bolt anything down — and there is no wall mount, because the format has none. Access also runs the other way from its siblings: the tray and the raceway let you reach a cable in seconds, while every change inside this box starts with lifting the lid — if the setup changes weekly, that lid becomes the product's personality. Buy it for a strip that stays put.

Tray, Raceway or Box? The Three Reviewed Cable Formats, Sorted by Job

This review completes the category's format trio, and the sort matters more than any single pick. A tray gathers: it takes the pile where cables collect — the strip, the bricks, the slack — and lifts it off the floor. A raceway routes: it carries a line of cables along an edge or wall and hides the run. A box conceals: it sits on a desk or floor and puts a lid over a strip you would rather not see but do not need to move. Most tidy desks use a tray or box where the strip lives, plus a raceway for runs that leave it; buying one format to do another's job is how cable money gets wasted.

The short version: take the Litwaro tray when the pile can clamp under the desk and disappear entirely. Take the Yecaye kit when cables travel along a desk edge or down a wall — and accept that its mount is a bond, with the surface preparation that implies. Take this Baskiss box when the strip stays where it is and the job is simply not having to look at it, with a barrier over it as the bonus. Frequently Asked Questions

Will my power strip fit inside?

Only if it is smaller than about 11.4 by 4.1 inches — the gate in the listing's own compatibility warning. Measure the strip's body, then check the tallest plug or brick standing on it against the lid. Note the listing's fields conflict: its description gives a 12 by 5 by 4.5-inch exterior while its Product Dimensions field reads 11.42 by 4.13 by 4.13 inches; this review publishes the description's figures as the exterior and the smaller figures as the fit gate. Roundup buyer notes include regular strips that did not fit, so measure rather than hope.

Does the box have ventilation holes?

No ventilation feature is stated for this box in any source found — not in the listing's description, its bullets or the roundups — so this review does not claim one. The cord outlets are the openings the interior has. Treat load as a fit check: after the first hour with your setup inside, lift the lid and confirm nothing is more than warm, and never seal a strip or brick that already runs hot in the open air into any closed box.

Is it pet-proof or child-proof?

It is a barrier, not a lock — which is also how the listing and the roundups frame it. The closed lid limits direct access to the power strip and its outlets, and keeps slack from dangling at chewing height. A determined pet or older child can lift a loose-fitting lid, so use it as one line of defence alongside routing exposed runs out of reach, not as proofing by itself.

Can I wall-mount it or hang it under my desk?

No. The listing's installation type is tabletop and freestanding: the box sits on a desk, a stand or the floor, and has no mount, clamp or adhesive. On a desktop it travels with a standing desk; on the floor it stays at floor level. Hanging a strip under a desk is the clamp tray's job — the Litwaro tray reviewed on this site is the catalogued option — and this box is the pick for strips that stay where they are.

What is it made of?

The lid is a wooden-style cover — the listing's enclosure-material field reads 'Wood', which describes the lid's style and finish — and the listing does not clearly state a body material for this model, so this review claims none. Do not import other boxes' specifications: the ABS construction advertised on competing boxes belongs to those listings, not to this one.

Is the white box the same product as the other Baskiss boxes and trays?

They are different listings, not variants of this one. This review covers the White 1-piece box with the wood lid, model 4330227480, Mfr Part 40-100-C02, ASIN B07Q2SK4JQ, first available January 4, 2018 per the US listing. Baskiss also sells other box sizes, clamp-on trays and clip sets under other ASINs, and no warranty term is stated on this listing. Every figure here belongs to this listing only; re-run the fit checks for any other variant.
