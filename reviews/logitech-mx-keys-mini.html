Our Verdict

4.4 out of 5 — Good

Logitech MX Keys Mini review: the MX Keys S's dished, quiet scissor keys, smart backlight and three-device switching, minus its numpad, in one 11.65-inch board. That cut is the whole argument, and it is an ergonomic one before it is a tidiness one: with no numpad pushing the mouse out to the side, the mouse sits inside the shoulder's comfort arc, which is why UCLA's ergonomics catalogue lists this exact model as its compact-keyboard example for reducing right-hand mousing reach. PCWorld's summary of the board is the honest one: essentially identical to the original MX Keys with the 10-key and control cluster chopped off, scissor switches with a pleasant, quiet feel that will not bother anyone in the next cubicle. Owners back it on the listing itself: 4.4 stars across 4,302 ratings for this exact ASIN (read October 2026), with quality, compact size and key feel the themes they name most. The limits are the mirror of the cut and of one quiet omission: there is no numpad and never will be on this board, the function row is a single shared row of media-first keys, the typing angle is fixed with no tilt feet, and — the detail buyers miss — this listing includes no USB receiver at all: it is Bluetooth-only in the box, a Logi Bolt receiver is a separate purchase, and Logitech's older Unifying receivers are not compatible with it at all. Owners' connectivity reports are the most divided theme on the listing, with a minority describing Bluetooth wake lag that a Bolt receiver fixed. Judge it as the compact MX board it is: the right buy when desk width and mouse reach are the problem, the wrong one when numbers, a receiver in the box, or an adjustable angle are requirements.

What We Liked

The typing feel survives the shrink intact, and it is the thing reviewers and owners praise first. The keys are the same spherically dished Perfect Stroke scissor switches as the full-size MX boards': each cap is shaped to centre the fingertip, the stroke is short and even, and the board stays quiet doing it. Tom's Guide's tester recorded his joint-fastest typing speeds on this board — 93 words per minute after three hours, at his usual accuracy — and called it the best keyboard he had used for typing; RTINGS judged it good for office use, light and responsive, and so low-profile it does not need the wrist rest it does not include. Owners on the listing use plainer words for the same thing — smooth, premium, butter-smooth — and key feel and build quality sit at the top of the listing's owner summary.

The size is the second strength, and it is a working advantage rather than a style one. At 11.65 inches wide the board hands roughly five inches of desk back to the mouse side compared with our Logitech MX Keys S review's full-size board, and that is precisely the change ergonomics guidance points at: a compact board lets a right-handed mouser work inside the shoulder's comfort arc instead of reaching around a numpad all day. Owners who bought it for small desks and laptop trays say the same in reverse — full-size key spacing where their fingers land, in a footprint that finally fits. The 506 g metal-topped body helps here too: heavy enough to stay planted while typing, light enough to be a genuine travel board, which several owners use it as.

The smart package around the keys is the third, unchanged from the bigger boards. The white backlight wakes as your hands approach and dims itself with the room; three devices pair over Bluetooth and switch from dedicated Easy-Switch keys; the Smart Keys put dictation, emoji and mic mute on the function row where video-call workers actually reach for them; and Logi Options+ Flow can carry the pointer — and copied files — between computers when it is teamed with a Flow-enabled Logitech mouse. Owners rate the battery theme positively too, with the listing's top review describing roughly two weeks between charges in real use with the backlight working, against Logitech's rated ten days.

What Could Be Better

The missing receiver is the complaint with a paper trail, and it starts before the box is opened: this listing is Bluetooth-only, and more than one owner reports ordering it in the belief — encouraged by listings that mention Bolt — that a receiver was inside. It is not. A Logi Bolt receiver is a separate purchase, and the older Unifying receiver many Logitech desks already run cannot pair with this board at all, a fact Logitech states plainly in the product description and owners discover the hard way. On a desk standardised on Bolt this costs one spare port or nothing; on a Unifying desk, or a locked-down work laptop where Bluetooth is restricted, it is a real second purchase or a real blocker.

Connectivity is the most divided theme in the owner feedback for this ASIN — the listing's own summary counts it mixed, with a substantial minority reporting problems against a majority who pair once and forget it. The recurring specific complaint is wake behaviour over Bluetooth: the board sleeps, and the first keystrokes after a pause can lag or be lost, which one long-term owner's detailed review says a Bolt receiver cured outright. That pattern — fine for most, laggy for some Bluetooth setups, fixed by the receiver the box omits — is the honest summary, and it is why the receiver question belongs at the top of the buying decision rather than in a footnote.

The rest of the limits are the compact format's own. There is no numpad, and spreadsheet-heavy work feels it within the hour; the function row is one shared row with media actions as the default, so F-key-heavy software takes an Fn habit; the typing angle is fixed — no tilt feet at all, which reviewers list among the cons and which wrists that like a raised rear edge will notice; and a few owners find the dished caps slippery under fast typing. None of these is a defect. All of them are the price of the 11.65 inches, and they are knowable before purchase in a way the receiver omission is not.

Performance in Daily Use

In daily work the MX Keys Mini performs as the MX line's answer to a specific desk geometry: it is the board for the worker whose screen, laptop and mouse are competing for a narrow surface, or whose shoulder has started filing complaints about the reach a full-size board imposes. Where the typing happens, nothing is traded away — the dished scissor keys are the same ones reviewers score at the top of the office category on the bigger boards, the backlight manages itself, and three-device switching makes a laptop-plus-desktop desk feel like one machine. Tom's Guide's speed testing and RTINGS' office verdict agree on the core: this is a top-tier typing instrument that happens to be small.

Where it performs differently from its siblings is in the two budgets it spends. The space budget is the win: five inches returned to the mouse side, a board that fits the keyboard tray, the shallow desk and the laptop bag our other keyboard reviews do not fit. The connection budget is the watch item: Bluetooth to a modern laptop is, for most owners, set-and-forget, but the minority wake-lag reports are consistent enough — and the Bolt-receiver cure consistent enough — that a buyer whose work machine is Bluetooth-restricted or Bluetooth-flaky should price the receiver into the purchase from the start. Battery performance lands where the rating says: around ten days to two weeks of real work with the smart backlight earning its keep, and months with it off, so the charging rhythm is a fortnightly habit rather than the weekly one the backlight's critics predict.

Against the rest of our keyboard coverage its position is exact. It shares its keys, backlight and battery rating with the MX Keys S and gives up the numpad, the included Bolt receiver and five inches of width. It shares its footprint problem — deliberately — with nobody: the Ergo K860 is wider than full-size, the MX Mechanical wider still in effect. It is the only board we have reviewed that a small-desk worker can buy without measuring twice, and the only one whose box contents need reading twice.

Who This Is For

Small-desk and narrow-surface workers, laptop-tray and travel typists, and right-handed mousers whose shoulder reach is the actual ergonomic problem: a compact board with full-size key feel, a self-managing backlight, three-device switching, and a mouse zone pulled back inside the shoulder's comfort arc. It is the compact fourth of the keyboard fork our ergonomic desk setup guide covers at elbow height: split shape, refined full-size, mechanical feel — or, now, the same refined keys with the width cut away.

Skip it if numbers are your day: there is no numpad, and our Logitech MX Keys S review's full-size board — same keys, same backlight, receiver included — is the same money's better answer for spreadsheet work. Skip it if your desk runs on Unifying receivers or your work laptop forbids Bluetooth peripherals, unless a separately bought Bolt receiver is acceptable. Skip it if your wrists need geometry: the split frame and negative palm lift of our Logitech Ergo K860 review answer a problem this straight, fixed-angle board deliberately does not touch. And skip it if you want mechanical switches in this footprint — that board exists in Logitech's line as a different listing, not this one.

Will It Fit Your Desk and Your Hands? The Width, Numpad and Receiver Check

This board carries three fit facts, and they matter more than any feature on it: it is 11.65 inches wide with no numpad, its typing angle is fixed with no tilt feet, and its box contains no USB receiver. Everything else about fit follows from those three, so check them against your desk, your work and your laptop's Bluetooth before anything else on this page matters.

The receiver check comes first because it is the one buyers get wrong: this listing pairs over Bluetooth out of the box, works with a Logi Bolt receiver bought separately, and does not work with Unifying receivers at all. If your work computer restricts Bluetooth, or your desk is built around a Unifying receiver, that is a second purchase or a different keyboard — decided now, not at unboxing. The numpad check is second: an hour of spreadsheet work a week is a habit you can route around; a day of it is a full-size board's job.

MX Keys Mini or MX Keys S? The Same Keys at Two Widths

Logitech sells the same typing instrument at two widths, and the choice between them is unusually clean because the shared parts are genuinely shared: identical Perfect Stroke dished scissor keys, the same smart white backlight with proximity wake, the same 1500 mAh battery rated at ten days lit or five months dark, and the same three-device Easy-Switch system. Nothing about the typing decides this. Three other things do.

Width and the numpad decide it for most buyers. The MX Keys S is 16.94 inches wide with a full numpad; the Mini is 11.65 inches without one. Spreadsheet and data work belongs on the S; a small desk, a keyboard tray or a travelling typist belongs on the Mini, and a right-handed mouser's shoulder will vote for the Mini on any desk. The receiver decides it for the rest: the MX Keys S includes a Logi Bolt receiver in the box, the Mini includes none and needs Bluetooth or a separate Bolt purchase — and neither board speaks Unifying. The practical rule: if you type numbers or need a receiver on day one, buy the S. If you measured your desk or your shoulder reach before you measured anything else, buy the Mini. Our Logitech MX Keys S review covers the full-size side in full, and our Logitech Ergo K860 review covers the third answer, for wrists that need a different shape rather than a different width.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which version of the MX Keys Mini is this review of?

The Graphite consumer version: model 920-010388, ASIN B098JPSVKY. The Pale Gray (920-010473), Rose (920-010474) and Black (920-010475) versions, the for Mac versions and the for Business version are different listings — the Business version, notably, adds the Logi Bolt receiver this listing leaves out.

Does the MX Keys Mini come with a USB receiver?

No — and this is the detail buyers most often miss. This listing is Bluetooth-only in the box: keyboard, USB-C charging cable and documentation. It works with a Logi Bolt USB receiver bought separately, and Logitech states it is not compatible with Unifying receivers, so an older Logitech receiver already on your desk will not pair with it.

How long does the MX Keys Mini's battery last?

Logitech rates the 1500 mAh battery for up to 10 days with the backlight on or up to 5 months with it off. Owners on the listing report roughly two weeks of real work between charges with the backlight active. It recharges over USB-C and keeps working while plugged in — wirelessly; the cable carries power only and there is no wired typing mode.

Is the MX Keys Mini good for a small desk?

It is the keyboard in our coverage built for exactly that: 11.65 inches wide (295.99 mm) and 506 g, with no numpad pushing the mouse arm out to the side. That narrower footprint is also an ergonomic point — a compact board lets a right-handed mouser work inside the shoulder's comfort arc, which is why ergonomics catalogues use this model as their compact-keyboard example.

MX Keys Mini or MX Keys S — which should I buy?

The keys, backlight and battery are the same, so decide on width and the box. Buy the Mini if your desk is small, you travel with the board, or mouse reach bothers your shoulder. Buy the full-size MX Keys S if you use a numpad or want the Logi Bolt receiver included — it is 16.94 inches wide and comes with one. Neither board works with Unifying receivers.

Does the MX Keys Mini work at a Mac login screen with FileVault on?

With one Logitech-documented caveat: FileVault can prevent Bluetooth devices from connecting before you have logged in, and Logitech's own manual for this model advises a compatible Logi Bolt receiver in that setup. Since no receiver is in this listing's box, FileVault users on Bluetooth-only desks should treat the Bolt receiver as part of the purchase.

Bottom Line

The Logitech MX Keys Mini is the space-first keyboard in our coverage: the MX line's dished, quiet scissor keys and self-managing backlight in an 11.65-inch board that gives the mouse its desk back and the shoulder its arc back. Its limits are knowable and mostly fair — no numpad, a shared function row, a fixed angle — plus one that is neither: no receiver in the box, on a board whose only owner-complaint pattern is Bluetooth wake lag that a receiver fixes. Buy it when desk width or mouse reach is the problem it solves. Buy the MX Keys S instead when the numpad or the included receiver matters more than the five inches.
