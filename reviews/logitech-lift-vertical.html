Our Verdict

4.4 out of 5 — Good

Logitech Lift review: a vertical mouse at a 57-degree handshake angle, built for small-to-medium hands, with quiet clicking, and 2 years on a single AA battery. That sentence is the whole design: instead of asking your hand to lie flat and your forearm to twist palm-down to meet a conventional mouse, the Lift stands the hand up at 57 degrees — what Logitech calls the natural handshake position — on a compact body shaped for small-to-medium hands, with a softly textured grip and a snug thumb rest. It is ergo-certified: designed, developed, tested and approved against criteria set out by leading ergonomists. Reviewers who live on vertical mice keep landing in the same place — Ars Technica found the Lift easier to grasp than any other vertical mouse it had used, with side buttons easier to reach than its larger rivals, and long-term switchers in TechRadar's testing worked longer before needing a wrist stretch once they learned to move from the forearm. Owners back that up on the listing itself: 4.4 stars across 13,783 ratings on Amazon's Canadian listing for this exact ASIN (read October 2026). The honest limits are built into the same shape: it is sized for small-to-medium hands and large hands will crowd it, the 57-degree angle is fixed, there is no rechargeable battery — it runs up to two years on a single AA — and it is a work mouse, not a gaming or fast-precision tool. Judge it as the fixed-angle vertical mouse it is — the right buy when that angle and that hand size match yours, and the wrong one when they do not.

What We Liked

The angle is the feature, and it is a considered one. At 57 degrees the hand rests in the handshake position rather than twisted palm-down, and Logitech's ergonomic framing for the design — a more natural forearm posture with less muscle fatigue and wrist pressure — is the direction independent testing points too: TechRadar's long-term switcher stopped bending the wrist side-to-side, learned to move from the forearm, and could work noticeably longer before discomfort arrived, while Pocket-Lint's reviewer found the hand settled onto the mouse with minimal effort and almost no learning curve. The Lift is ergo-certified against criteria set out by leading ergonomists, which is a stronger claim than a sculpted shell and a marketing adjective.

The size is the second feature, and for its intended hands it is the reason to choose this mouse over Logitech's own larger MX Vertical. The Lift is built for small-to-medium hands, and owners in that range say so in exactly those terms — small-handed owners on Best Buy's review pool call it tiny and comfortable, a fit where the bigger vertical mice felt oversized. Ars Technica's tester, whose hands suited both Logitech verticals, still found the Lift easier to grasp and its side buttons easier to reach. Fit is the whole game in a vertical mouse, and this is the one shaped for the hands the category usually forgets.

The clicks really are quiet. Logitech calls them whisper-quiet, and owners and reviewers use the same word unprompted: silent buttons that colleagues on calls do not hear, in shared rooms and quiet houses alike. The SmartWheel earns its place next to them — a ridged, grippy wheel that shifts between precise line-by-line scrolling and fast document flight, which reviewers describe as smooth and second-nature rather than a spec-sheet extra.

The battery life removes the mouse from your attention for years at a time. One AA battery, included, is rated for up to 24 months, and owners who have run the mouse for six months or more describe that estimate as conservative. There is no charging cable in the weekly routine and no dead mouse on a Monday morning; the battery level is visible in Logi Options+ long before it matters. Owners also single out a small piece of industrial design that rarely gets mentioned: the battery cover is magnetic, so it survives the drops that snap the plastic covers off lesser mice.

What Could Be Better

Hand size is a hard gate, not a preference. Logitech sizes the Lift for small-to-medium hands, and every source agrees on the consequence: large hands crowd it. Pocket-Lint's reviewer, using a claw grip because a palm grip overflowed the short body, had fingertips extending well past the buttons; roundups route large hands to the bigger MX Vertical instead. Measure your hand before ordering — if your current mouse already feels small, this one will feel smaller, held upright.

The 57-degree angle is fixed. There is no adjustment toward a flatter or a fully upright 90-degree posture, so if the handshake angle itself is what your forearm dislikes, no setting on this mouse corrects it — a different angle is a different mouse. The adaptation is also real even when the angle suits: switchers describe an initial period of subconsciously trying to grip it palm-down, a perceived loss of click accuracy, and side-to-side wrist habits that have to be unlearned in favour of forearm movement. Reviewer experience puts the awkward phase at days, not weeks, but budget your first workdays accordingly.

It is not rechargeable. The two-year AA is the trade that buys the battery life, and a consistent minority of owners name it as the one change they would make — if your desk standard is USB-C charging for everything, this mouse is the exception you will keep a spare AA for. Relatedly, the included receiver is USB-A Logi Bolt: on a current MacBook with only USB-C ports it needs an adapter, a point AppleInsider flags for Mac buyers (Bluetooth pairing avoids the receiver entirely).

The soft grip is a maintenance item. The textured, rubberised surface that makes the mouse secure in the hand can show dirt and oils over time, and at least one long-term reviewer notes the tall body can feel heavier to move and easier to knock than a low, flat mouse if you lift and reposition constantly. Finally, this is a work mouse: reviewers are blunt that it is not suited to gaming or fast precision pointer work, and its 4,000 DPI ceiling and office-tuned buttons should be read as a category statement, not a flaw to discover after purchase.

Performance in Daily Use

In daily office work the Lift performs the way its test record says it should. Tracking is even and uneventful across ordinary desk surfaces at its adjustable sensitivity, the connection owners report on both Bluetooth and the Logi Bolt receiver is stable, and switching among up to three paired devices — a work laptop, a personal machine, a tablet — happens from the Easy-Switch button without re-pairing. Logitech Flow, set up in Logi Options+, lets the pointer cross between two computers and carry copy-paste with it, the feature owners with a laptop-plus-desktop desk actually use.

The ergonomic performance is the product, and it is worth separating what is claimed from what is observed. What is claimed, by Logitech, is posture: the 57-degree angle puts the forearm in a more natural position and the design is certified against leading ergonomists' criteria. What is observed, independently, is narrower and more credible: reviewers who switched full-time report working longer before wrist or forearm discomfort forced a break, describe the relief as real but not total, and are explicit that a vertical mouse does not treat anything — TechRadar's tester still had an elbow problem the mouse never claimed to fix, because it came from resting the elbow on a chair arm. That is the honest shape of the benefit: one common source of strain — the twisted, palm-down forearm — removed, with the rest of your posture still yours to manage.

The one performance cost is front-loaded, and it is the adaptation. The first days feel slower: accuracy dips while your hand relearns where the buttons sit at an angle, and the mouse punishes the wrist-flick habits a flat mouse trained. Reviewers put basic comfort at a few days for most switchers, with full fluency — moving from the forearm without thinking about it — following over the first couple of weeks. Owners who stuck out that window describe the flat mouse as the strange one afterwards; owners who needed pixel-fast pointer work on day one are the ones who sent it back.

Who This Is For

Small-to-medium-handed desk workers — writers, analysts, support and operations staff, anyone whose hand lives on a mouse for most of the working day — who feel the palm-down twist in their forearm or wrist by mid-afternoon and want the vertical category's most approachable shape: a fixed, moderate 57-degree angle, quiet clicks for shared spaces, three-device switching, and a battery they can forget for two years. It is the natural mouse half of the ergonomic pairing our Logitech Ergo K860 review builds on the keyboard side: the K860 keeps the typing wrist straight, the Lift keeps the mousing forearm untwisted, and both follow the placement rules in our ergonomic desk setup guide — mouse at elbow height, close enough that your shoulder stays down and your elbow stays near your side.

Skip it if your hands are large — the compact body is this mouse's defining limit, and Logitech's larger vertical, reviewed in our Logitech MX Vertical review, is the honest alternative in the same category. Skip it if you need gaming-grade speed or fast precision pointer work, if you refuse disposable batteries on principle, or if you know a fully upright or much flatter angle is what your forearm wants — the 57 degrees here does not adjust. Left-handed readers should also note this review covers the right-hand Graphite listing: Logitech sells a dedicated left-hand Lift, but it is a separate listing with its own ASIN, in fewer colours — order that one deliberately, not this one hopefully.

However you choose, place whatever mouse you use the way the setup guide sets layer 1: on the same level as the keyboard, close to the body's midline, so the reach never pulls your shoulder forward — a vertical mouse held at arm's length gives back the strain its angle saved.

Will It Fit Your Hand? The Size, Angle and Habit Check

This mouse carries three fit facts, and they matter more than any feature on it: it is sized for small-to-medium hands, its 57-degree angle never adjusts, and it asks you to move from the forearm instead of flicking the wrist. Everything else about fit follows from those three, so check them against your hand, your current mouse habits and your desk before anything else on this page matters.

Hand size is the first check because the body is compact by design: if your hand measures in the small-to-medium range — roughly, if a standard office mouse already feels about right or a little large — the Lift's grip, thumb rest and side buttons land where your fingers expect them. The habit check is the second: if you can give the angle a few ordinary workdays, reviewer experience says it turns natural; if this week's work is deadline-critical pointer precision, time the switch for a quieter week.

What the 57 Degrees Actually Does — and What It Does Not

A conventional mouse forces the forearm into pronation: palm flat down, the two forearm bones crossed over each other, held there for every working hour. The Lift's 57-degree tilt rotates the hand most of the way toward a handshake, where the forearm sits nearer its neutral, uncrossed position. That is the entire mechanism — no electronics measure or correct your posture, and the benefit reviewers report (longer comfortable sessions, less end-of-day forearm ache) follows from that one rotation, which is also why the benefit stops where the rotation stops: a wrist still bent upward on a hard desk edge, or an elbow still crushed on a chair arm, keeps its own problem.

Two honest boundaries follow. First, 57 degrees is a compromise angle — flatter than a fully upright 90-degree vertical mouse, steeper than any conventional mouse — chosen so the hand still rests securely on the body instead of sliding off it; owners occasionally describe an early feeling of the hand slipping or pinching until the thumb rest habit forms, which is the compromise being learned, not a defect. Second, no figure or certification on this page promises treatment: the ergo certification attests the design met ergonomists' criteria, and the honest role of this mouse is prevention and comfort for working hands, judged over your own first two weeks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Logitech Lift the same as the Lift for Business or the Lift for Mac?

No — and the distinction matters when you order. This review covers the consumer right-hand Graphite version, model 910-006466 (ASIN B09J1TB35S), which ships with a Logi Bolt USB receiver and pairs over Bluetooth. The Lift for Business (model 910-006491) is a separate listing built around IT deployment and security modes, and the Lift for Mac is a Bluetooth-only variant in Off-White that ships without a receiver. The mouse body and 57-degree shape are shared; the bundle and receiver are what change.

What hand size is the Logitech Lift for?

Small-to-medium hands — that sizing is the product's defining choice and the reason it exists alongside Logitech's larger MX Vertical. Owners with small hands describe the fit as immediate, while reviewers with larger hands found their fingertips extending past the buttons and the grip crowded. If your hands are large, buy the larger vertical category, not this mouse in hope.

Is the Logitech Lift rechargeable?

No. It runs on a single AA battery, included, rated by Logitech for up to 24 months, with the battery level visible in the Logi Options+ software. Owners running the mouse for six months or more describe that rating as realistic. The trade is deliberate — years of battery in exchange for keeping a spare AA — and it is the complaint owners name most often.

How long does the Logitech Lift take to get used to?

Reviewer experience puts basic comfort at a few days and full fluency at roughly one to two weeks. The awkward phase has a specific shape: trying to grip it palm-down out of habit, a temporary dip in click accuracy, and having to unlearn side-to-side wrist flicks in favour of moving from the forearm. Time the switch for an ordinary week rather than a deadline week.

Does the Logitech Lift work with a MacBook that only has USB-C ports?

Yes, over Bluetooth Low Energy, which needs no port at all. The included Logi Bolt receiver is USB-A, so on a USB-C-only MacBook the receiver route needs an adapter; most Mac owners pair over Bluetooth and leave the receiver in the box as a backup for a second machine. Windows, Chrome OS, Linux, iPadOS and Android are also supported, with up to three devices paired at once.

Is the Logitech Lift good for gaming?

No, and reviewers say so plainly. Its sensor tops out at 4,000 DPI, its buttons are tuned for quiet office clicks, and its tall vertical body is built for a resting hand, not for fast lifts and flicks. It is an all-day work mouse; a gaming mouse is a different purchase with a different shape, and this one does not pretend otherwise.

Bottom Line

The Logitech Lift is the vertical mouse for the hands the category usually skips: a certified 57-degree handshake shape sized for small-to-medium hands, genuinely quiet clicks, a SmartWheel that earns its place, three-device wireless and two years on a single AA, in a body reviewers found easier to grasp than any other vertical they had used. Its limits are the mirror of its design — a fixed angle, a compact body large hands will crowd, no rechargeable battery, and office manners instead of gaming speed. Buy it when your hand size matches and the palm-down twist is the strain you feel; it is the mouse our ergonomic coverage has been assuming exists every time a guide said to untwist the mousing arm as well as the typing wrists.
