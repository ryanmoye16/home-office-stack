Our Verdict

4.4 out of 5 — Good

Logitech MX Keys S review: a full-sized, low-profile, backlit keyboard with dished scissor keys, three-device switching, and USB-C charging for quiet desk work. That sentence is the whole design: instead of reshaping the keyboard around your hands, the MX Keys S keeps the straight, full-size layout most typists already know — numpad included — and refines everything on it. The keys are Logitech's Perfect Stroke scissor switches under spherically dished caps shaped to centre your fingertips, the white backlight wakes on its own as your hands approach and dims with the room, and the board pairs to three computers over Bluetooth or its included Logi Bolt receiver, switching between them from three dedicated keys. Reviewers keep landing on the same strengths: TechGearLab named it Best Overall in its wireless keyboard testing, with top-tier typing-noise scores, and AppleInsider praised the solid typing feel and the depth of the feature set. Owners back the package on the listing itself: 4.4 stars across 3,858 ratings for this exact ASIN (read October 2026), with keyboard quality, battery life and key feel the themes they mention most. The honest limits are just as specific, and owners name them too: the backlight that sells the board is also what eats its battery (ten rated days with it on, against five months with it off), there is no palm rest in the box and no way to adjust the fixed typing angle, the function row runs in one unbroken line with media actions as the default, and its Bolt receiver cannot pair with Logitech's older Unifying devices. Judge it as the refined conventional keyboard it is — the right buy when you want a familiar straight layout done about as well as it can be done, and the wrong one when what your hands need is a different shape.

What We Liked

The typing feel is the feature reviewers and owners praise first, because it is the thing you touch ten thousand times a day. The spherically dished caps centre each fingertip, the scissor switches land with a short, even stroke, and the board stays quiet doing it — TechGearLab's testing gave it a 9.8 of 10 for typing noise alongside an 8.3 for typing experience, and its testers' accuracy scores sat on or above their own averages. Owners on the listing use plainer words for the same thing: smooth, quiet, satisfying, and the single most-mentioned theme in the owner summary is keyboard quality itself.

The smart backlight is the second strength, and it is smarter than a lit keyboard usually is. The keys wake as your hands approach — before you touch anything — and the brightness follows the room, so the board is lit at dusk and calm at noon without a settings trip. In Options+ you can take manual control of brightness and how long the light stays. Owners call the auto-backlight brilliant in the listing's top positive review, lighting exactly when needed without distracting, and for anyone who works into the evening in a dim room it solves the problem our Ergo K860 review has to route around with a desk light: this keyboard simply has lit legends, at every hour.

The three-device system is the third, and it is the one multi-computer desks feel daily. Three Easy-Switch keys hold three pairings across Bluetooth and the Bolt receiver, moving between a work laptop, a home machine and a tablet is a single press, and owners describe the switching as fast and reliable. Paired with a Bolt mouse — our MX Master 3S review's mouse is the natural partner — one receiver can serve both devices, and Logi Options+ Flow then carries the pointer, and copy-paste with it, between two computers. Smart Actions in the same app automate repeated multi-step tasks onto a single keystroke, the feature AppleInsider singled out as the thing that lets its reviewer fly through work.

The build and the battery-off battery round it out. At 810 grams the board is heavy for its size in the way owners approve of — solid, planted, no flex or slide mid-sentence — and the low-carbon aluminium plate inside is part of why. And the battery has two honest personalities: with the backlight off, Logitech rates up to five months, and owners who run it dark report rarely plugging in at all. The keyboard also keeps working while it charges over USB-C, so even the ten-day backlit rhythm never stops a morning.

What Could Be Better

The backlight is a trade, not a free feature, and the terms are steep. With smart illumination on, the rating is ten days — and owner reports match the arithmetic, with one Best Buy owner measuring roughly 70% of the battery gone in ten days of auto-backlight use. Tom's Guide found the white-only light dim and lacklustre next to the best, some owners find the proximity wake inconsistent — lit late, or flickering — and longer legends on wide keys can light unevenly. None of this is hidden; it is the price of lit keys on a rechargeable board, and the K860 in our other keyboard review makes the opposite trade (no light at all, a two-year battery). Decide which side of that trade your room puts you on before ordering.

There is no wrist support and no angle adjustment, and for some hands that is the whole review. The keyboard-only box contains no palm rest — the rest is a separate accessory or part of the Combo bundle — the body sits at one fixed, shallow angle with no tilt feet to raise or flatten it, and the listing's most detailed critical review is from a long-time palm-rest user whose wrists and fingers fatigued within a week. A low profile keeps wrists nearer neutral than a tall board does, but if you know your hands need a cushioned rest or negative tilt, that is the Ergo K860's design brief, not this one's aftermarket hope.

The function row is where convention quietly changes. The F keys run in one unbroken line with no gaps between the groups of four that touch typists navigate by, the row defaults to its printed media and system actions rather than F1–F12 (restoring standard F keys is an Options+ setting, which makes the software effectively mandatory), and the key above the numpad is a screen-snip key where many long-time Logitech users expect Print Screen, next to a Lock key where they expect Calculator. None of these is a defect; all of them are relearns, and the listing's critical reviews show they are the relearns that actually send some veteran users back to their old boards.

The ecosystem has the same seams as the rest of Logitech's current line. The Bolt receiver cannot pair Unifying devices — so an older Unifying mouse or our K860's receiver setup means two receivers in two ports — there is no storage slot for the receiver in the board, and customisation lives in Options+ on Windows and macOS only: Linux gets basic typing over Bluetooth or Bolt, without Smart Actions or F-key remapping, a limit Linux owners on the listing flag plainly. And it is a full-size board: the numpad that spreadsheet work loves pushes a right-hand mouse further from the body's centre, the same reach cost our K860 review counts, at 430 mm wide before the mouse zone starts.

Performance in Daily Use

In daily typing the MX Keys S performs the way its test record says it should, with one honest asterisk. TechGearLab's words-per-minute and accuracy testing put it essentially on par for accuracy and a step behind the most streamlined boards for raw speed, and the testers traced the gap to the dished caps themselves: the shallow bowls that centre your fingers also ask them to lift clear on the way to the next key, so a typist who slides flat across a chiclet board may mistype more in the first days, not fewer. Owners who stay with it describe the opposite end of that curve — fast, fluid, precise is the listing's own summary of the feel — so read the dish as an adjustment with a payoff, not a pure win.

The connection performance is boring in the best way. Pairing over Bluetooth and the Bolt receiver is quick, Easy-Switch changes are a press rather than a menu, and Flow, once configured in Options+, genuinely moves text and files between a Windows and a Mac machine by pushing the pointer through the screen edge. The one recurring owner note is that the Bolt receiver route can feel the more dependable of the two on machines with crowded Bluetooth environments — the same pattern our mouse reviews record.

Battery performance splits exactly where the spec says it does. Backlight on, expect the roughly ten-day rhythm the rating promises and owners confirm; the low-power mode that kills the light under 10% is the board protecting your last hours, not a fault. Backlight off, the five-month rating reads as marketing until the owner pool is checked: battery life is one of the most-mentioned and most-positive themes on the listing, with owners describing months between charges as normal. Either way the failure mode is gentle — plug in the USB-C cable and keep typing.

The ergonomic performance needs the boundary this site puts on every keyboard. What is claimed, by Logitech and its reviewers, is comfort within a straight layout: a low profile that keeps wrists flatter, dished caps that reduce fingertip hunting, a solid base that does not move. What is observed is narrower: owners report comfort and reduced fatigue relative to ordinary boards, and reviewers rate its comfort highly — but the hands still sit side by side at shoulder-narrowing width, wrists still bend outward to meet a straight row, and there is no rest under the palms. If that geometry is your strain, this keyboard refines the posture causing it rather than changing it; our Ergo K860 review is the same money spent on the other answer.

Who This Is For

Full-time typists — writers, analysts, accountants, operators, anyone whose day is hours of continuous keyboard work — who want the conventional straight layout at its most refined: dished quiet keys that feel like a good laptop board's bigger sibling, a backlight that manages itself in a dim room, a real numpad for number work, and three-device switching that makes a two-computer desk feel like one machine. It is the natural keyboard partner to our Logitech MX Master 3S review's mouse — both speak Logi Bolt, so one receiver serves the pair, and Flow can carry the pointer between computers — and it slots into a setup built our ergonomic desk setup guide's way as the straight-layout option at elbow height.

Skip it if your wrists need geometry, not refinement: the split frame, integrated rest and negative palm lift of our Logitech Ergo K860 review answer a problem this board deliberately does not touch. Skip it if you want mechanical switches — our Logitech MX Mechanical review is the tactile-bump alternative in the same line, though its switches are soldered rather than swappable — or gaming-grade response: the scissor keys are quiet office instruments and do not pretend otherwise. Skip it for a small desk or a laptop bag: at 430 mm wide and 810 grams it is built to stay put, and the numpad's mouse-reach cost is real on a shallow desk. And skip it if a ten-day charging rhythm will irritate you more than darkness does — with the light on, that rhythm is the product.

Will It Fit Your Desk and Your Habits? The Width, Light and Layout Check

This keyboard carries three fit facts, and they matter more than any feature on it: it is 430 mm wide before your mouse zone starts, its typing angle is fixed at the factory with no feet to change it, and its function row defaults to media actions in one unbroken line. Everything else about fit follows from those three, so check them against your desk's width, your wrist habits and your F-key memory before anything else on this page matters.

Width is the first check because the numpad is part of the measurement: on a right-hand-mouse desk you need the board's 430 mm plus a mouse zone beside it, and on a shallow or narrow desk that reach pushes the mouse — and your shoulder — outward all day. The light check is the second: the backlight is this board's reason to exist over cheaper straight keyboards, but it only pays in rooms that actually go dim; under bright office light all day, you are carrying a ten-day battery for a feature you never see. The habit check is the third: if your fingers navigate the F row by its group gaps, or your workflow leans on F1–F12 as F keys, budget a relearn and an Options+ setting, because the row you know is not the row that arrives.

MX Keys S or Ergo K860? The Two Logitech Answers, Side by Side

Our two keyboard reviews are the same brand answering two different questions, and choosing between them is choosing what you believe your problem is. The Ergo K860 says the problem is geometry: it splits the keyframe, tents the wrists onto a pillowed rest and lifts the palms at negative angles, accepting a fixed split angle and a relearn as the price. The MX Keys S says the problem is friction: it keeps the straight layout your hands already know and removes everything that interrupts it — darkness, device switching, mushy keys, a board that slides. If your wrists ache in the sideways bend, friction was never your problem. If your hands are fine and your evenings are dark and your desk holds two computers, geometry was never yours.

The trades line up in mirror image almost everywhere. Light: the Keys S has smart white backlighting; the K860 has none at any setting. Battery: that light costs the Keys S a ten-day rhythm (five months with it off, on a rechargeable cell), while the lightless K860 runs up to two years on two AAA batteries. Support: the K860's rest is built in and cannot be removed; the Keys S ships without one. Receivers: the Keys S speaks Logi Bolt and can share one receiver with our MX Master 3S; the K860's consumer version speaks Unifying and cannot share with either. Footprint: both are full-size with numpads, but the K860 is far deeper (233 mm including its rest against 132 mm), while the Keys S is the one that fits a shallow desk. Neither is the better keyboard in the abstract — each is the better answer to one desk's actual complaint.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Graphite MX Keys S the same product as the Pale Gray or the for Mac version?

The keyboard is the same design, but the listings are different and the distinction matters when you order. This review covers the Graphite version, model 920-011558 (ASIN B0BKW3LB2B), with dual Windows/Mac legends. The Pale Gray version is model 920-011559 (ASIN B0BKVZK4TH), the for Mac versions use Mac-only legends and different models and ASINs, and the Combo bundles the keyboard with a mouse and palm rest under its own listing. Ratings and prices differ per listing, which is why this review's figures are tied to this ASIN only.

How long does the MX Keys S battery actually last?

It depends entirely on the backlight, and Logitech publishes both numbers: up to 10 days on a full charge with backlighting on, or up to 5 months with backlighting off, from the built-in 1500 mAh rechargeable battery. Owner reports track the split — roughly ten days is the common backlit experience, months is the common dark experience. Below 10% charge the board turns its own backlight off to stretch the remaining power, and it keeps working while plugged in over USB-C, so a flat battery costs a cable on the desk, not a stopped morning.

Can one receiver run the MX Keys S and my mouse?

Yes — if the mouse is a Logi Bolt model. The MX Master 3S in our mouse review is the clean example: keyboard and mouse share the one Bolt receiver, and Logi Options+ Flow can then move the pointer and copy-paste between computers. What Bolt cannot do is pair Logitech's older Unifying devices — Logitech states the two systems are not compatible — so a Unifying mouse, or the consumer Ergo K860's receiver setup, means a second receiver in a second port, or Bluetooth for one of the pair.

Where did Print Screen go, and why do the F keys trigger media actions?

Two layout decisions explain most first-week complaints. The F row defaults to its printed system actions (brightness, media, and similar) rather than F1–F12; setting the row to standard function keys is done in Logi Options+, which is why the app is effectively required rather than optional. And the key above the numpad is a screen-snip key, not a traditional Print Screen key, sitting near a Lock key where many long-time Logitech users expect Calculator. Owners on the listing flag both changes plainly; neither is a fault, but both are relearns worth knowing about before the board arrives.

Does the MX Keys S work on Linux?

For typing, yes: it pairs over Bluetooth Low Energy or the Bolt receiver and works as a keyboard on Linux and Chrome OS. The limits are in the software layer — Logi Options+ is Windows and macOS only, so on Linux there are no Smart Actions, no per-key remapping and no app-side way to flip the F row's default behaviour, and basic functions are what Logitech promises on systems other than Windows and macOS. Linux owners on the listing name exactly that gap; if deep customisation on Linux is the point of the purchase, a QMK-class board is the right category instead.

Is the MX Keys S mechanical, and is it good for gaming?

No on both counts, by design. The keys are Perfect Stroke scissor switches — shallow, quiet and even, close in feel to a good laptop keyboard — with fixed dished caps; there are no mechanical switches to choose and nothing to swap. Reviewers are equally plain about gaming: the travel, the polling behaviour and the full-size wireless design are tuned for text, and competitive players should buy a gaming board. For fast, quiet, all-day typing — the job this keyboard is actually built for — the scissor design is the point, not a compromise.

Bottom Line

The Logitech MX Keys S is the conventional full-size keyboard at its ceiling: dished, quiet scissor keys reviewers rank at the top of the category, a backlight that wakes and dims itself, three-device switching with a genuine multi-computer Flow system, and a build that stays planted all day. Its limits are the mirror of its design — the light costs battery by the week, the angle and the function row are fixed on Logitech's terms rather than yours, there is no rest under your palms, and Bolt leaves your Unifying gear on its own receiver. Buy it when a straight layout is what your hands want and you type past daylight; it is the keyboard our coverage recommends when the answer is not a different shape, but the familiar one done properly.
