Our Verdict

4.4 out of 5 — Good

Logitech MX Vertical review: a rechargeable vertical mouse at the 57-degree handshake angle for medium-to-large right hands, with its 4,000 DPI sensor and Flow. That sentence is the whole design, and it is the older, larger half of Logitech's vertical pair: where the Lift is compact, AA-powered and sized for small-to-medium hands, the MX Vertical is the roomier body shaped for medium-to-large right hands, rechargeable over USB-C, with the MX line's multi-device machinery — Easy-Switch across three computers and Logitech Flow between them — carried into a vertical shell. The angle is the same 57 degrees Logitech uses on the Lift, set in a natural handshake position the company says reduces muscular activity by 10 percent against a standard mouse, in a design tested against criteria set out by leading ergonomists. Owners back the shape on the listing itself: 4.4 stars across 14,102 ratings on Amazon's listing for this exact ASIN (read May 2026), with comfort and wrist relief the most-mentioned themes in its review pool. The honest limits are the mirror of the Lift's: it is right-hand only with no left-hand version, the 57-degree angle never adjusts, its clicks are ordinary audible clicks rather than the Lift's quiet ones, a minority of owners report button and scroll-wheel faults over time, and it is an office mouse, not a gaming or fast-precision tool. Judge it as the large-hand, rechargeable vertical it is — the right buy when the Lift's compact body is the thing that rules the Lift out, and the wrong one when your hands are small or your mouse hand is your left.

What We Liked

The size is the feature, and it fills the gap the Lift leaves. Logitech's own FAQ sizes the MX Vertical for medium-to-large hands, and the owner pool on its Amazon listing says the same thing from experience — bigger palms call out the fit by name, and the listing's own guidance routes smaller hands to the Lift instead. On Amazon's review summary for this ASIN, comfort is the most-mentioned theme by a wide margin (881 mentions, most of them positive), with reviewers describing a hand that can simply rest on the body between tasks. If every vertical mouse you have tried felt like a toy in your hand, this is the one shaped at your scale.

The wrist story is consistent, and it is reported rather than promised. Wrist pain is the second-biggest theme in the same owner pool (490 mentions, most positive), with owners describing strain that eased over days of ordinary work — one software engineer's Best Buy review puts the dull forearm ache at mostly gone within three days, and Amazon owners using it six to eight hours a day report rarely feeling the pain that sent them shopping. The mechanism is the same one the Lift uses — the 57-degree handshake angle in place of the palm-down twist — and Logitech frames its own claim carefully, as a 10 percent reduction in muscular activity against a standard mouse, in a design tested against leading ergonomists' criteria. Read it as owners report it: one common source of strain removed, not a treatment for anything.

It is rechargeable, and the charging is genuinely convenient. A full charge is rated for up to four months, owners describe two to three months of heavy use as realistic, and the one-minute quick charge returning about three hours of use is the specification owners actually cite when the battery finally fades mid-week. It also works while plugged in, so a dead battery is a cable for an afternoon, never a stopped day — and there are no AA spares living in a desk drawer, which is the Lift's one recurring owner complaint answered.

The MX machinery is all here. Easy-Switch pairs three computers and moves between them from the button on the base; Logitech Flow, set up in Logi Options+, carries the pointer — and copy-paste — across machines; and the cursor speed switch on the top ridge is the feature reviewers single out as elegantly done: hold it and swipe, or tap it, and the 4,000 DPI sensor's sensitivity changes without opening software. Macworld's reviewer called that button a particularly elegant touch, and owners migrating from the MX Master line describe the transition as learning one new grip, not a new system.

What Could Be Better

It is right-hand only, completely. There is no left-hand MX Vertical and no ambidextrous fallback in the box; left-handed readers are routed to the Lift, which is the Logitech vertical that actually sells a left version. The same fixed-ness applies to the angle: 57 degrees is the posture, it never adjusts toward flatter or fully upright, and owners who wanted a steeper or adjustable angle name exactly that as the wish. If the handshake angle itself does not suit your forearm, no setting on this mouse changes it.

Small hands will crowd it from the other direction. The body that makes it the large-hand answer makes it bulky for everyone else — Amazon's own owner summary carries the warning in reviewers' words ('not ideal for small hands — can feel bulky'), and Logitech's comparison table routes small-to-medium hands to the Lift. Its tall, narrow-based shape is also easy to knock over in the first weeks: owners describe catching it with a sleeve or the back of a hand while reaching across the desk, a foible of every tall vertical that this one's height makes real.

The buttons are the owner pool's sore spot. On Amazon's summary for this listing, button functionality is one of the few themes that runs more negative than positive, and the specific complaints repeat across retailers: side buttons some owners find low or awkwardly reached, and a minority reporting click faults — including click-and-drag releasing early — developing after months of daily use. Best Buy's owner summary likewise carries scroll-wheel durability among its recurring cons. These are minority reports in a large, mostly satisfied pool, but they are consistent enough that precision click-and-hold work (video timelines, CAD dragging) is the workload to weigh hardest before buying.

The clicks are not quiet, and the receiver is the older standard. An owner who loved everything else about this mouse still apologised for its ordinary, audible click sound — in a shared room or on calls, the Lift's near-silent clicks are a real advantage this mouse does not have. And its bundled receiver is Logitech Unifying, not the Logi Bolt receiver the Lift and the MX Keys S ship with: on a desk standardised on Bolt, the MX Vertical needs its own USB-A port or a Bluetooth pairing, and the mouse itself has no storage slot for that receiver when you travel — a small omission owners notice because Logitech's own trackball has one.

Performance in Daily Use

In daily office work the MX Vertical performs like the MX-line mouse it is, held sideways. Tracking from the 400–4,000 DPI optical sensor is described by reviewers as precise and drift-free across ordinary desk surfaces, including the glossy ones that defeat cheaper verticals, and the cursor speed switch earns its keep exactly where a high-DPI sensor should: fast travel across a wide monitor at one sensitivity, fine placement in a spreadsheet or photo editor at the other, changed from the top button without a software detour. Connections over Bluetooth and the Unifying receiver are reported stable, and Easy-Switch plus Flow make a laptop-plus-desktop desk feel like one machine — the feature multi-device owners name first.

The ergonomic performance follows the pattern every honest vertical-mouse account follows. What is claimed, by Logitech, is posture and a measured 10 percent reduction in muscular activity against a standard mouse. What is observed, independently, is that owners and reviewers who switched full-time report the forearm ache fading over the first days, describe the benefit as real, and are equally clear about its boundary: Macworld's reviewer found the concept sound and the mouse good while noting it is not ideal for high-intensity mousework, and the owner pool's mixed functionality reports say the same from the other side. This is a comfort tool for knowledge work — documents, browsers, code, design placement — not a gaming sensor or a treatment.

The adaptation cost is front-loaded and, by vertical-mouse standards, short. Reviewer and owner experience clusters at hours-to-days rather than weeks: an engineer on Best Buy's pool had precision back within about four hours of active use, Amazon owners put the new-grip adjustment at a few days, and the recurring description is that the old flat mouse feels strange afterwards. The one habit that has to be relearned is the same as on any vertical — moving from the forearm instead of flicking the wrist side-to-side — and the tall body will get knocked over once or twice while that habit forms. Budget a quiet day for the switch, not a quiet month.

Medium-to-large-handed desk workers — developers, analysts, designers, operations staff, anyone right-handed whose hand lives on a mouse all day — who feel the palm-down twist in their forearm by mid-afternoon and found the compact vertical category too small: this is the 57-degree handshake shape at your scale, rechargeable over USB-C, with three-device switching and Flow for a multi-computer desk. It pairs with the keyboard half of the ergonomic setup our Logitech Ergo K860 review covers, under the placement rules in our ergonomic desk setup guide — mouse at elbow height, close enough that the shoulder stays down. Readers choosing between Logitech's two verticals should read the comparison section below before the specifications: hand size and battery, not features, are what separate them.

Skip it if your hands are small-to-medium — the Lift is the same angle in the body actually shaped for you, and it is the honest alternative rather than this mouse in hope. Skip it if you mouse left-handed (no version exists), if you need quiet clicks for a shared room, if your work is gaming or fast precision pointer work, or if your daily grind is sustained click-and-drag and the owner pool's minority button reports worry you — that workload is the MX Master shape's home ground, and our MX Master 3S review covers the conventional alternative.

However you choose, place whatever mouse you use the way the setup guide sets layer 1: on the same level as the keyboard, close to the body's midline, so the reach never pulls your shoulder forward — a vertical mouse held at arm's length gives back the strain its angle saved.

Will It Fit Your Hand? The Size, Angle and Habit Check

This mouse carries three fit facts, and they matter more than any feature on it: it is sized for medium-to-large right hands, its 57-degree angle never adjusts, and it asks you to move from the forearm instead of flicking the wrist. Everything else about fit follows from those three, so check them against your hand, your handedness and your desk before anything else on this page matters.

Hand size is the first check because the body is generous by design: if a standard office mouse already feels small in your palm, the MX Vertical's grip, thumb rest and button reach land where your fingers expect them — it is the vertical the category's larger-handed owners were waiting for. The habit check is the second: reviewer experience puts basic precision at hours and full comfort at a few days, so time the switch for an ordinary day rather than a deadline day.

MX Vertical or Lift? Logitech's Two Verticals, Side by Side

Logitech sells two 57-degree vertical mice, and the choice between them is not good-versus-better — it is a fit fork. The MX Vertical (this review) is the older, larger, rechargeable design; the Lift is the newer, compact, AA-powered one. Same angle, same ergonomist-tested framing, same three-device switching: nearly everything that differs follows from the body size and the battery choice, which is why hand size should make this decision before any feature list does.

The practical rule: measure your hand against your current mouse first. If ordinary mice feel small and you want USB-C charging and the MX line's cursor speed switch, the MX Vertical is your vertical. If ordinary mice feel right or large, you want quiet clicks, or you mouse left-handed, the Lift answers every one of those and this mouse answers none of them. Our Logitech Lift review covers that side in full.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Logitech MX Vertical the same mouse as the Lift?

No — they are Logitech's two different vertical mice, and the distinction is the buying decision. Both hold the hand at the same 57-degree handshake angle and switch between three devices, but the MX Vertical (this review, model 910-005447, ASIN B07FNJB8TT) is the larger body sized for medium-to-large right hands and is rechargeable over USB-C, while the Lift is the compact body sized for small-to-medium hands, runs up to two years on a single AA battery, clicks quietly, and is the one that also sells a left-hand version.

What hand size is the Logitech MX Vertical for?

Medium-to-large right hands — Logitech's own FAQ states that sizing, and owners with bigger palms confirm the fit while smaller-handed reviewers call the body bulky. There is no left-hand version. If your hands are small-to-medium, or you mouse left-handed, Logitech's Lift is the same 57-degree design in the body actually made for you.

Is the Logitech MX Vertical rechargeable?

Yes. Its built-in lithium polymer battery is rated for up to four months on a full charge, recharges over the included USB-C cable, returns about three hours of use from a one-minute quick charge, and the mouse keeps working while it is plugged in. Owners running it heavily describe two to three months per charge as the realistic figure.

Does the MX Vertical use the Logi Bolt receiver?

No — it ships with the older Logitech Unifying USB receiver, which is a different and incompatible standard from the Logi Bolt receiver bundled with the Lift and the MX Keys S. On a desk standardised on Bolt, pair the MX Vertical over Bluetooth or give its Unifying receiver its own USB-A port. Note also that the mouse has no internal storage compartment for the receiver.

How long does the MX Vertical take to get used to?

Less than most vertical mice, on reviewer and owner experience: basic precision returns within hours of active use for many switchers and full comfort within a few days. The habit to relearn is moving from the forearm instead of flicking the wrist side-to-side, and the tall body may get knocked over once or twice while that habit forms. Time the switch for an ordinary workday.

Is the Logitech MX Vertical good for gaming?

No, and reviewers say so plainly. Its 400–4,000 DPI sensor and office-tuned buttons are built for documents, browsers, code and design placement, and its tall vertical body is made for a resting hand, not fast lifts and flicks. Macworld's verdict was the measured one: a good mouse, not ideal for high-intensity mousework. It is an all-day work mouse; a gaming mouse is a different purchase.

Bottom Line

The Logitech MX Vertical is the large-hand half of Logitech's vertical pair: the same ergonomist-tested 57-degree handshake angle as the Lift, in a roomier body sized for medium-to-large right hands, rechargeable for up to four months over USB-C with a one-minute quick charge, and carrying the full MX multi-device kit — Easy-Switch, Flow, and the top-ridge cursor speed switch reviewers praise. Its limits are plain: right-hand only, a fixed angle, ordinary audible clicks, an older Unifying receiver with nowhere to store it, and a minority of owner reports about button and scroll-wheel faults over time. Buy it when the Lift's compact body is what ruled the Lift out; buy the Lift when it was not.
